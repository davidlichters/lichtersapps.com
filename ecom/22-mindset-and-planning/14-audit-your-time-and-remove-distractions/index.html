Audit your time and remove distractions

Section 22.14 · 14 of 26 in this chapter · 9 min read

In short

  • Before you change anything, track your real time for 30 days. Write down what you actually did, not what you think you did.
  • Every switch between tasks costs about 15 minutes to get back into focus. Keep this in mind.
  • Do the same task in one long block of 60 to 80 minutes. Short pieces waste time.
  • Watch for more than 1 hour a day on unimportant tasks, like checking ads all day.
  • Be honest in your log. Do not blame yourself. Small, steady improvement is enough.

Measuring where your time goes

1 min
  • Before you improve anything, you need a starting point. This is a baseline: a detailed record of where your time really goes, not a general feeling about what you did. Without this starting point, you cannot tell whether later changes help.
  • Time is the one thing an e-commerce owner (someone who runs an online shop) cannot add more of. Each day has a fixed 24 hours. Customers only shop for limited hours.
  • The only lever (a way to change your results) is using your existing hours better. Choose what to work on. Set priorities (decide what matters most first). Reduce procrastination (putting tasks off).
  • Owners often slide into short tasks. These feel productive. But they do not grow the business in the medium term (months) or the long term (years).
  • Focus on tasks only you can do. Stay focused while you do them.
  • The more focused the owner is on the business, the greater the chance it grows and succeeds. Distractions (things that pull your attention away) lower that chance directly. So protecting focus time is part of the plan.
  • Constant urgency (the feeling that everything must be answered now) is often a feeling, not a fact. Protecting focus time is worth more than answering every request right away. Attention is one of an entrepreneur's most valuable resources.
  • Do not blame yourself for struggling with time management. Most business owners find it hard. Slow, steady improvement in how you use time reduces stress and pressure. It also builds confidence in the business.

Running the 30-day baseline

1 min

Procedure (about four calendar weeks, meaning four weeks on the calendar):

  1. For the next four weeks, record every task done in or on the business.
  2. Estimate the time spent on each task.
  3. Break the record down day by day.
  4. Include every distraction. For example, a 30-minute detour into Facebook (a social media site) during a work block.
  5. Add a procrastination line each day with the total estimated time lost to procrastination. Details of what you did are optional.
  6. Enter the entries at the end of each day, as you go, or as a running log. Choose what works for you. The entries must match what you actually did.
  7. Review the 30 days afterward to find patterns and decide what to change.

Steps that make the baseline work:

  • Set a daily reminder in your phone or calendar. Then the time log gets filled in every day for the full 30 days.
  • Start the time log right away, on the day you first set it up. Do not wait until other planning or learning is finished. Setup takes only a couple of minutes, so there is no reason to delay the 30-day baseline.
  • Tracking does not have to run all the time. It can be done in rounds. A one-month tracking run is enough to refresh your view of how you spend time.

Tools for time tracking

1 min
  • Toggl Track (toggl.com/track) is an app and online service. It times your activities automatically on a computer and, optionally, on a phone. You do not have to remember to start and stop each task. It can show how much time goes to apps such as Instagram. Some sources spell it "Toggle."
  • Manual day-by-day spreadsheet tracker, available in Excel and Numbers versions (spreadsheet programs). Each day is a column. Each row is a task with the time spent. A procrastination row is included. Add a column for each extra day to extend the tracker past the sample days in the template.

Example entries for one Monday:

Task Time
Customer service phone calls 1.5 hours
Writing product descriptions for the website 1 hour
Updating business social media pages 30 minutes

Logging honestly

1 min

  • Time tracking only works if you are honest with yourself. Writing down distractions and procrastination can feel uncomfortable. But skipping them makes the baseline useless.

  • Your tracking records are private. Do not share them with a coach or anyone else. Their purpose is to show patterns in your own working time.

  • Do not analyze or change your habits during the 30 days. First collect the data. Patterns and fixes come after the tracking period ends.

  • Writing up the whole day at the end relies on memory. That is hard if you struggle to recall your day. Log each task as it happens. It is more reliable.

  • A time log shows the real picture only if it includes distractions and detours, not just planned work blocks. Record examples such as:

    • switching to another website or social media during a work block
    • checking the phone instead of working
    • deciding to leave for a break, such as an hour away

    Log each one with its estimated duration.

Reviewing the log after 30 days

1 min

Procedure: Review the time-tracking log after at least 30 days (a practical minimum, a month of tracking). Find four categories:

  • distractions
  • rapid task switching
  • long stretches on unimportant tasks
  • tasks that could be delegated (handed to someone else)

Use an app such as Toggl Track, or a spreadsheet, to pull the data. Then check each category in order.

  1. Distractions: Note interruptions in the middle of a task and off-topic breaks. Count how often each one recurs.
  2. Task switching: Look for many short blocks of 15 minutes or less on the same kind of work.
  3. Unimportant tasks: Find activities that take long stretches of time. Classify them with a prioritization matrix (a grid that sorts tasks by importance and urgency, meaning how time-sensitive they are). This is an important/urgent grid.
  4. Delegation: Find tasks taking more than one hour per day, especially recurring ones over 30 days. Ask whether only the owner can do them.

Deciding what counts as a priority:

  • Treat a distraction as a priority problem only when it recurs often over the tracking period. A distraction seen once in 30 days may not matter.
  • Classify each recurring distraction in two ways. Is it business-related or not? Is it something you control or not?
  • The worst distractions keep appearing at the same point in the daily schedule. A single occurrence in a 30-day tracking period is usually not worth acting on.

Distraction types and how to handle them

1 min

Habit-based distractions

  • Habit-based distractions are addictive behaviors (repeated actions that are hard to stop). They last until you break the habit with a specific plan. Examples include checking email, social media, or the Shopify app (the tool for running your online store) again and again, such as 57 times a day. Such habits do not speed up sales or business growth.

Business-related distractions: scheduling

  1. Decide whether the distraction can be moved to a later time.
  2. Add it to the daily planner at a fixed time instead of dropping it entirely. For example, 30 minutes of email at 1 p.m., after important work is finished.
  3. Enforce the schedule through self-discipline (keeping your own promise to yourself), since no tool can enforce it for you.

Uncontrollable interruptions

  • Interruptions you cannot control need a different solution than habit-based distractions. Examples include a business phone ringing before you have a customer service agent (a person who answers customer questions), or a family member entering unexpectedly.
  • Address the phone-ringing case through staffing or outsourcing (paying others to do the work), not willpower.

Task switching

1 min
  • Each interruption costs about 15 minutes to return to flow state (a deep, focused way of working). Every switch loses at least 15 minutes of focused work.
  • Task switching is inefficient when you work on the same task in blocks of 15 minutes or less. Your brain needs at least 10 minutes to reach peak efficiency (your best working speed) on a task. Short, repeated blocks lose a large share of productive time.
  • Example: A blog post (a written article on a website) was written in four 20-minute blocks on different days. It took 80 minutes of time across the sessions. When the same post was written in one 80-minute block, it took about 60 minutes. Focus was sustained and work quality rose.
  • Example, same case within one day: A blog post written in four separate 20-minute blocks within one day took 80 minutes in total. When the same post was written in one continuous 80-minute block, it took about 60 minutes. The writer kept full focus and the quality was higher. Combining the fragments into one block of the same task saves time.
  • Combine short pieces of the same task into one longer block, around 60 to 80 minutes, to finish faster.

Procedure to reduce task switching:

  1. Identify the tasks you switch between often in blocks of 15 minutes or less.
  2. If you are not using a daily to-do list (a written list of today's tasks), start one and build a habit around it.
  3. If you already have one, enforce it with self-discipline. Reward yourself for sticking to it.
  4. Schedule the task in one larger block.

Comfort tasks and ad account over-checking

1 min
  • Spending more than one hour per day on tasks that are unimportant for the business is a warning sign. Review these tasks with a prioritization matrix.
  • People often spend too long on unimportant tasks they feel comfortable with. An example is hours in the Google Ads account (Google's paid ad system). They avoid important tasks they feel less confident about. Examples are SEO (search engine optimization, getting your site found in search results for free) or difficult supplier or customer conversations.
  • For a high-ticket dropshipping business (selling expensive products that a supplier ships directly to customers), SEO has far greater potential than Google Ads. Spending hours on ads while neglecting SEO means the time is misallocated (put in the wrong place).
  • Optimizing Google Ads has diminishing returns (each extra hour gives less). You can easily spend hours on it. Spending five hours a week to raise CTR (click-through rate, the share of people who click your ad) by 0.3% rarely produces revenue or profit worth that time. Those hours could go to other work.

Ad account time limits:

  • Ad account work needs hours daily only in rare cases. Setting up a new account, building new campaigns (groups of ads with one goal), or making major changes justifies extra time.
  • Routine data review and small tweaks (minor changes) that take hours are over-analysis. Limit them to 30 minutes instead of two hours.

Over-optimizing by hand:

  • Refusing automation (letting software do repetitive work) and optimizing every bid (the price you pay per click) by hand in spreadsheets takes very many hours. One keyword example (one search term you target) was estimated at 20 hours. That time is then missing for conversion rate optimization (getting more buyers from the same visitors), store branding, and other growth work.

Protecting focus blocks

1 min

Blocked work sessions (set times when you do only one job):

  1. Use Do Not Disturb (a phone setting that silences alerts) or focus mode during a blocked work session.
  2. Silence phone notifications and close apps.
  3. Handle urgent calls or messages only if they are repeated or clearly an emergency.

Unfocused time:

  • Two hours of checking email, searching, and messaging without a defined task does not count as productive work. Recognize unfocused time as lost time, not as progress.

Supplier outreach:

  • A common failure point (the place where progress breaks down) is avoiding supplier outreach (contacting the companies that provide your products), especially phone calls. Postponing calls to a later weekend or vacation stops progress. Schedule the calls and make them.

What a brand is and when to build one

Section 8.1 · 1 of 24 in this chapter · 11 min read

In short

  • A brand is the feeling people have about your company. A logo is only one small part of it.
  • Do not build a brand before your store makes sales. First test a product, then build the brand around the proven product.
  • A brand brings cheaper customers, repeat buyers and higher prices, because people come to know and trust your name.
  • If you look and sound like every other seller, you become a commodity and can only win on price.
  • In the early stage, spend your money on ads and sales, not on logos and design.

Brand beyond the logo

2 min
  • A brand is the set of beliefs, feelings, perceptions and attitudes a customer has about a company and its product. Logo, colors and typography (the font style) are signs that strengthen these feelings. They are not the brand itself.
  • A brand is everything that shapes how people see a company, not only its logo. It includes the look (logo, website layout, store or office design), the verbal style (the words you use), the company persona (the character the company shows, as if it were a person), the customer experience, and the expectations customers bring with them.
  • A brand has five parts: aesthetic design (the look), verbal language (verbiage, meaning the words you choose), company persona, customer experience, and customer expectations. Together they form the full picture people have of your company.
  • Brand equity is the extra value a brand earns beyond its product. It comes from its values, its tone of voice (the way it sounds) and the culture it stands for. A brand sells who you represent, not only the item itself.
  • Brand perception (how people see the brand) is shaped mainly by how the business acts: how it treats customers, its tone of voice, and how it behaves on social media. The name only makes the first impression. Consistent good behaviour matters more than a perfect name.
  • A dropshipping store (a store that sells products without holding stock, because the supplier ships each order) becomes a brand when the product gets a name and people recognise it by that name, not by its generic category. Brands show faces, custom packaging, labels and custom content in their ads and store.
  • Good support (help for customers) and consistent branding build a loyal customer base. This is what turns a dropshipping product into a brand. Brands often reach this stage with branded packaging and branded creative (ads and images made for the brand).
  • When branding is done correctly, the specific products you sell matter less. The brand succeeds through connections built with customers through content, not through product features or benefits.
  • Consistent design across logo, colors and typography lets customers recognise the store at once. They connect it to their past experiences. Inconsistency weakens these connections.
  • Visual choices must match what customers expect in their niche (a small, specific market). For example: green and earthy tones for gardening products, luxury type for luxury goods, and friendly type for family or children's products. Designs that clash with the niche make customers doubt the store.
  • A brand can be built even on everyday products such as toothpaste, shampoo or coffee. These brands earn loyalty through trust in the name. They also stress what makes them different in a commodity market (a market where all products look the same).

What a brand creates

3 min

Lower customer acquisition cost

  • A brand lowers your customer acquisition cost (what you pay to win one new customer), because referrals come without ad spend.
  • Brand awareness moves the buying journey earlier. Customers who already know the company find it when they are ready to buy, instead of comparing product listings. When the relationship comes first, product choice matters less.
  • A real brand attracts customers who know your name before they search for the product.
  • Your dream customers (your ideal buyers) are your biggest source of new visitors, through word of mouth and referrals. Content they like and engage with creates a growth engine that sustains itself.
  • A dream customer buys, engages with your content and recommends the brand to others. So your goal is an advocate (a customer who speaks up for you), not just a one-time buyer. Advocates bring in new people and lower the cost of winning them.

Repeat purchases

  • A brand increases repeat purchases, meaning customers who buy again.
  • Brands with returning customers can afford a higher customer acquisition cost. Stores that rely only on new buyers become unsustainable when ad costs rise.
  • Trust, built through good product quality, on-time delivery and working tracking (the shipment location updates), drives repeat purchases.
  • Customers come back when their first order went well: the product arrived on time, tracking worked and the quality matched what they expected. Trust is the main reason people buy again.
  • A brand creates value through identity alignment (the brand matches who the customer is), trust and loyalty, emotional attachment, a clearly defined market segment, and influence on how customers buy, post and recommend. Each of these increases repeat sales and organic reach (free views, not paid ones) over time.

Identity and loyalty

  • Customers defend what they identify with. A brand that represents a customer's identity has a strong position. Designing the store around the customer's self-image works better than designing it around the founder's taste alone.
  • Large sportswear brands often sell a culture and values rather than the product itself. Their ads may barely show the shoe. A brand that represents a lifestyle can charge more than a product-focused competitor. Brand building is a long-term job that must be earned through consistent messaging.

Pricing power

  • A commodity product can carry a high price when the brand adds identity and status. For example, a running shoe with little material difference from cheaper ones can sell for around 120 to 140 dollars because of its brand story and the athletic identity it represents.
  • Strong brand equity makes new products easier to sell. Customers pay more for a known name with similar quality. The speaker reported that 70% to 80% of new items sold for his established brands.

Resale value and market recognition

  • Building a brand increases the valuation (the price a buyer would pay for your business) if you sell it, and it sets up market recognition.
  • A recognisable brand is harder to copy, which raises the valuation when the business is sold.

Ownership of the customer relationship

  • On a marketplace (a site such as Amazon, where many sellers sell), the platform owns the customer relationship. Buyers see Amazon as the service provider. The seller cannot build brand identity or a community from customer data.
  • Build the brand for the long term. Aim to be competitor-proof (rivals cannot easily beat you), marketplace-proof (you do not depend on one platform) and timeless (it lasts). Treat your own website as the main sales channel, even if a marketplace also sells for you. Your own store has higher margins (the profit left after each sale) and lets you control the customer interaction.

Brand versus commodity

2 min
  • A seller who looks and sounds the same as competitors becomes a commodity. Then the only way to compete is on price. This is a losing position, because someone will always undercut you. Price-only competition also fails for resellers who cannot lower prices any further.
  • When customers see a business as essentially the same as its competitors, it becomes a commodity. Commodities are much harder to sell. Being seen as unique makes selling easier.
  • Competing only on product features or technology turns a product into a commodity. Buyers then compare it mainly on price. Stores should add identity, experience and a clear promise, so customers have a reason to choose them.
  • Selling a generic product from a marketplace such as AliExpress (a large online site for cheap goods from suppliers), without your own branding, means the same item appears from many sellers in Google Shopping (Google's product search with price comparison). Shoppers see the same product across the whole platform and have no reason to choose you.
  • The product must be desirable (wanted by buyers). It does not have to be totally different from competitors, but it must give buyers a clear reason to want it. Desirability comes first, and the rest of the marketing builds on it.
  • Brand and product support each other. Some buyers purchase because they love the brand. Others buy because they love the product. A strong brand identity helps buyers choose your label over copies and lookalikes.
  • Competition can inspire you, but the aim is a brand that stays desirable no matter how crowded the market is. Other brands leave room for many players when the product is distinct and wanted.
  • A brand you create yourself usually has zero search volume at launch. Search volume is how many people type a word into search each month. Because nobody searches for your new name yet, generic keyword ads (ads triggered by typed words) will not bring in your first customers.
  • Ads that target competitor brand and product names work for dropshipping early on. But this approach does not carry over to your own brand. Do not expect the same ad approach to bring your first customers for your own label. **

Naming the brand

1 min
  • A general store name is fine when the owner is testing several products. The store should still define what it stands for. Colors and typography should reflect how the store wants to be seen, even if its positioning (where it sits compared with others) is somewhat vague.
  • Give the brand a name you can build on, so people can search for it once demand exists.
  • A recognisable store name only helps once customers search for it. **

Staging: validate with sales first

3 min

Core rule

  • Validate (prove) a product with dropshipping sales first. Then build a brand around it once there is proven demand.
  • Do not build a brand before a dropshipping store works. First learn the skills by running a profitable dropshipping store. Apply them to a brand once you have generated several six figures.
  • Starting a brand from scratch can cost thousands of dollars. It also needs lots of testing to confirm product-market fit (proof that enough people want the product). A dropshipping store is a cheaper way to build skills first.
  • Do not build a brand before you have sales. **
  • Branding builds loyalty and repeat purchases. It works best after a product has proven sales. Build it through custom images, a clear story, a visible face, reliable shipping and good customer support.
  • A brand built in a single high-demand niche can perform well. A leather jacket store doing strong daily sales is one example. Brand work often starts after general stores already bring in steady revenue.

What to avoid before sales

  • Beginners waste weeks on logos, colors and brand storytelling for a pump-and-dump product (a product that sells fast for a short time, then dies). A simple brand name and product images are enough to test.
  • Spending time on a logo, a custom branded store or polished brand assets before a product has any sales wastes money and attention. Publish content first, learn from how it performs, and build the brand once sales arrive.
  • Spending too much time on branding, such as styled photos, models and Instagram posts, slows down sales testing. Focus first on making sales from the product. Build the brand later.

Minimum viable brand early

  • Minimum professional standards are enough for a successful start. You do not need branding packages that cost thousands of dollars to look professional and attract customers and suppliers. **
  • In the early stage, spend on paid traffic (visitors you pay for through ads) and sales, not on brand design, social followers or custom photography. Build social channels after traffic and an email list (a list of people who agreed to get your emails) exist. Then link to those channels from your emails. **
  • A minimum viable brand (MVB) is the smallest brand that is enough to go live and give customers a believable picture of the store. It avoids a full brand project at launch. It grows as the store grows and as test results show what works.

Early-stage creative spending

  • In the early stage, do not hire an expensive creative director (the person who leads the look and design). That salary money can go straight into ads. With a good product, more ad spend grows your brand faster than brand planning does.
  • In the early stage, the product is the brand. Invest in the product itself and let it speak through ads and word of mouth. Good products go viral (spread fast on their own). Do not pretend you are too sophisticated for direct response (ads built to make people buy right away).
  • Athletic Greens is one of the highest-performing direct-to-consumer brands ever. It put direct response advertising and conversion metrics (numbers showing how many visitors buy) ahead of design aesthetics. It reached massive scale and now has Joe Rogan, a famous podcast host, as a promoter. This shows that a brand grows from performance and ad spend, not from early creative direction.
  • In the early stage, do not sacrifice ad performance for brand consistency. Brand continuity (keeping the same colour palettes, tone of voice and iconography, meaning the set of symbols and icons) lifts you up. But only if it does not reduce your ad spend.

Testing a product before its own design

  1. Test a product by using an existing design from another seller.
  2. Shoot your own photos for it, so your listing does not match the photos competitors use.
  3. Once results show demand and the brand has traction (real signs of growth), move to your own designs.

Full brand later

1 min
  • Brand continuity and consistency pay off once demand is proven and your ad spend is working.
  • Three critical requirements for brand success: you must know your customer better than anyone, you must invest significantly in content creation, and you must commit to the long game rather than seek quick results.
  • CrossFit shows complete brand alignment. Its look is not a normal gym. Its verbiage includes its own terms: Box (the gym), WOD (workout of the day), AMRAP (as many rounds as possible) and Games (its competition). Its persona is polarising, meaning people either love it or hate it. Its customer experience stresses family and community. Its expectations centre on consistency. Every part reinforces one identity that attracts dream customers.
  • Build the brand for the long term. Aim to be competitor-proof, marketplace-proof and timeless.

Further details

1 min
  • A brand built from scratch needs research into the niche and its sub-niches (smaller parts of a niche). It needs a clear customer avatar (a detailed picture of your ideal buyer). It also needs a sample that proves product-market fit before you scale up. Skipping this makes brand building far riskier than dropshipping tests.

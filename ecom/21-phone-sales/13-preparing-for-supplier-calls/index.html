Preparing for supplier calls

Section 21.13 · 13 of 26 in this chapter · 6 min read

In short

  • Call suppliers before you email them. A phone call gets a clear answer faster.
  • Call early on weekdays, during the supplier's opening hours and in their local time. If nobody answers, call back at 9:00 a.m.
  • Build a list of at least 20 suppliers before you start calling.
  • Follow up after each call. Many suppliers only answer after a second contact.
  • Feeling nervous is normal. Practise the call first, and treat their questions as a talk, not a test.

Why phone beats email

  • Phone works better than email.
  • People connect through voice.
  • A call allows real-time discussion, meaning talking live and solving problems together.
  • Long back-and-forth email chains cannot provide that.
  • Call suppliers rather than relying on email.
  • A phone call gets a clear answer far more often.
  • Reported approval rates above 60% come from calling suppliers. An approval rate is the share of suppliers who say yes.
  • Email follow-up alone was less effective.
  • Calling gives an immediate answer: yes, no, or call-back (they will ring you again).
  • Calling speeds up approvals.
  • If a call is not possible, use email and follow up persistently. A follow-up is another message or call to remind them.
  • Make phone calls to supplier contacts the default approach, meaning the normal first choice. (2023.)
  • Emails are easy to ignore.
  • Calls build a stronger connection.
  • Call first and email second.
  • A phone call gets a yes or no faster than email.
  • Call the supplier first and make your case by phone rather than email. Making your case means explaining why they should work with you.
  • A call cuts through the noise.
  • A call gets you to the decision maker (the person who says yes or no) faster.
  • Calling is faster than emailing for approval, especially when you need a direct answer.
  • Pair calls with follow-up emails.
  • Many suppliers only respond after a second contact.
  • Phone calls to suppliers are the most efficient route to approval. (2024.)

Vetting suppliers

  • Vet a supplier before calling. To vet means to check carefully.
  • Search the company name for reviews and reputation.
  • Confirm they are sold on competitor sites, meaning other shops that sell similar products.
  • The call then only confirms the details.

Building the call list

  • Keep building the supplier list.
  • Collect all contact details during the holiday period.
  • Build a contact list of at least 20 suppliers before you start calling.
  • Not all of them will approve you from the first call.
  • Some suppliers need education about the business model, meaning how a company earns money.
  • Approval can take many months.
  • Prepare your own pitch script, a planned set of words for your sales talk.
  • Expect a dialogue, meaning back-and-forth talk, rather than a single call.
  • Suppliers with strong brands are usually the hardest to get approved.
  • Start with silver-tier suppliers, meaning suppliers in the "silver" ranking group.
  • Build traffic (visitors to your shop) and sales with them first.
  • This makes it easier to return to them later.

Holiday-period plan

Full steps, kept complete:

  1. Keep building the supplier list and collect all contact details during the holiday period.
  2. Finish the demo store, a sample shop you build to show suppliers, so it looks complete before you start calling.
  3. Start supplier calls on the first Monday after the holidays, when companies return to normal business.
  4. Call with the supplier sheet, your list of supplier details, ready to go.

Step 3 is tied to the return to normal business after the holidays. Timing for calls outside that window is covered under Timing and time zones below.

Reviewing the supplier's website

  • Before calling a supplier, review the supplier's own website. (2023.)
  • Speak back to what it highlights about its brands, as if preparing for a job interview.

Timing and time zones

  • All supplier calls must happen during the supplier's business hours, meaning the hours the company is open.
  • This applies even if it means calling at inconvenient times from your location.
  • When calling suppliers, call early on weekdays in the supplier's time zone, meaning the local clock where they are. For example, call at 8:30 a.m. (2025.)
  • If no one answers, call back at 9:00 a.m.
  • This was the fastest way to get through.
  • Use a phone-based process for supplier contact. Call first thing in the morning and follow up by phone or email. (2023.)
  • Contacting suppliers early in the day gives you a better chance of reaching the decision maker. (2023.)
  • Calls to suppliers, especially early in the morning, reach decision makers more often than emails or calls at busy times of the day. (2023.)
  • If no replies come in, change the timing and test again. (2023.)

Where the timing sources differ

  • The 2020 holiday plan starts calls on the first Monday after the holidays.
  • It applies to the post-holiday restart, the first return to work after a break.
  • The 2023 and 2025 rules recommend early weekday morning calls in the supplier's time zone.
  • They apply to routine calling, meaning everyday calls.
  • Where the supplier's business hours and your location conflict, the business-hours rule takes priority.

Mindset before the call

  • Fear of calling suppliers often comes from treating one deal as make-or-break, meaning it decides everything.
  • Having several options lined up makes each call feel less critical.
  • The calmer energy that results makes others more willing to work with you, without changing your words.
  • Present yourself as a partner who will bring them sales, not as a pestering retailer.
  • Approach the call expecting that they are glad to hear from you.
  • Supplier calls are normal conversations, not scripted interviews.
  • Treat their questions as a discussion, not as objections (reasons they say no) to overcome.
  • Only some of the questions covered here will come up on any given call.
  • Mistake: do not overthink supplier questions.
  • Mistake: do not read their questions as rejections.
  • Preparing answers in advance is useful.
  • A call should still stay natural and confident.
  • Supplier calls are relaxed, informal conversations, not hiring interviews.
  • No salary is offered.
  • Prepare by learning about the company.
  • Expect questions about your business, but treat the call as a conversation, not a test.
  • Many new sellers get stuck calling suppliers. (2023.)
  • Make the calls in one single block, meaning one long stretch of time.
  • Record notes on each call.
  • Start with the top of the list.
  • This helps overcome reluctance, meaning holding back or feeling unwilling.
  • Nervousness with senior decision-makers often comes from seeking their approval as if you had to beg for it.
  • Ask whether you would physically behave that way in the same situation.
  • If you would not, the posture, meaning the stance you take toward them, is a belief you can test and drop.

Nervousness and accent

  • An accent or non-native English, meaning English learned after childhood, is not a barrier to supplier approvals.
  • Suppliers want business relationships that benefit both sides.
  • When making cold calls, meaning calls to people who do not know you yet, a non-native accent or nervousness can be named openly as part of your background.
  • Naming it reduces tension and builds trust.
  • Presenting this trait honestly often works better than hiding it.

Rehearsal and coaching practice

  • Rehearse hard tasks, like supplier cold calls, with a coaching call, meaning a practice session with a coach, before doing them alone.
  • This reduces nervousness.
  • It builds the skill over time.
  • Rehearse the script and practise on less important suppliers first. (2024.)

Mental rehearsal procedure

Mental rehearsal means practising in your head before the real thing.

  1. Before a sales call, list the questions and objections the prospect (a possible buyer) is most likely to raise.
  2. For each one, work out a clear, specific answer in your mind.
  3. Rehearse the full conversation in your imagination, including the prospect's objections and your replies.
  4. Repeat the rehearsal, especially for difficult objections, until your replies come easily before using them on real calls.

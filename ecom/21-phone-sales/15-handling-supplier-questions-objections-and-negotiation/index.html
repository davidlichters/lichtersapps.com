Handling supplier questions, objections and negotiation

Section 21.15 · 15 of 26 in this chapter · 7 min read

In short

  • Start with what the supplier gains, not with what you want.
  • Never accept the first no. Ask why, and never push for a yes on the spot.
  • Do not ask for a discount in your first contact. Ask about price levels first.
  • Check your margin (the money you keep after paying the supplier) before you sell at MAP (the lowest price you may show).

Opening supplier calls and pitching your store

1 min

  • Lead with a benefit to the supplier (the company whose products you sell), not a request.

  • You bring visitors, exposure (people seeing the brand) and sales. They only ship orders and answer emails.

  • Keep every conversation pleasant. Avoid negativity.

  • Differentiate with a specific reason.

  • Suppliers talk to many sellers. A general claim, like "we are reliable," is weaker than a named product feature.

  • Use this formula:

    1. Name a specific feature of the supplier's product that stands out from copies in the market.
    2. Show that you researched the brand.
    3. State how your store will treat their customers with care.
    • Furniture example: "Your outdoor sofas use weather-resistant frames that most competitors copy without the same build quality, and we want to show that quality to buyers who need durable outdoor furniture."
    • Skincare example: "Your formulas avoid the fragrance additives that most shelf brands use, and our store targets shoppers with sensitive skin who are looking for exactly that."

  • Show credibility and make the pitch easy to act on.

  • Some suppliers hold back a large amount of their trust until an account is set up.

  • So offer flexible shipping arrangements to reduce the barrier (the thing that stops them saying yes) to working with you.

  • Prepare a short paragraph about the marketing channels (the ways you reach buyers, such as ads or email) you will use to sell their products.

  • Be ready to read it out when asked how you will market them.

Handling hesitation and refusals

1 min

  • Never accept the first no.

  • Politely ask why. Refusals are often quick exits, a busy moment or a need for more time.

  • The real objection (the reason behind the no) can often be resolved.

  • Hanging up without digging can cost you your best supplier.

  • Do not pressure for approval on the spot. Pushing damages a relationship you want to last for years.

  • Many buyers want a day or so to think. Pressure makes them less likely to agree.

  • Give them an easy way out of the call, so they do not feel trapped.

  • Where a supplier is not taking new online retailers right now, follow this procedure:

    1. Politely ask why there is a limit, for example a supply shortage (not enough stock), limited product volume or a temporary issue.
    2. If the reason is temporary, offer to set up now but delay your first sales by about a month, so their current supply flow (how products move from them to buyers) is not disturbed.
    3. If they still refuse, thank them, say you would like to work together later and leave your contact details.
    4. Ask them to contact you if a retailer spot opens up, and offer to start selling straight away.
    5. Record a callback date in your supplier sheet (your list of suppliers and their details), for example three months later, and make that call.

  • Look for an arrangement that works for both sides, not a rigid setup (a fixed deal that cannot change).

  • Starting small and proving sales lets you ask for better terms (deal conditions such as price) later.

  • A hard line at the start can end the relationship before it begins.

Deposits, upfront payment and pre-purchase requests

1 min

  • When a supplier asks for a deposit (money paid before any sales) or a pre-purchase (buying stock before you sell it), tell them you need to see sales first.

  • Propose a test. This reduces risk and keeps the deal open.

  • Upfront payment (you pay before the supplier ships) removes the supplier's financial risk.

  • Credit terms (the supplier ships first and you pay later) could leave them chasing an unpaid invoice (a bill not yet paid) 30 days later.

  • So zero risk is the most effective way to get a yes.

Answering "what's in it for us?"

1 min

When a supplier asks what is in it for them, offer these three benefits:

Benefit for the supplier What it gives them
Payment for each order upfront Reliable cash flow (steady money coming in) and no late payments to chase
Access to new online customers Their products reach new buyers and their market share (their part of total sales in the market) grows
Fast route for excess stock A quick way to move excess stock (extra products that are not selling) or discontinued stock (products they are stopping)
  • Suppliers often offer up to 40% off excess stock if you promote it actively.

Wholesale discounts, MAP and margins

2 min

  • Do not ask for a discount in the first contact with a new supplier.

  • Ask about pricing tiers (price levels that depend on how much you buy).

  • Let the supplier name its own volume-based pricing (prices based on how many units you order) before you offer anything.

  • On a first call, after receiving the supplier's price list (their sheet of prices), follow this sequence:

    1. Thank them and say everything looks fine.
    2. Explain that you always look for the best prices so you can put the most money into advertising for every brand you sell.
    3. Ask whether they offer tiered pricing, such as lower rates above a certain dollar amount or a set number of units per month, and what the pricing would be at those levels.
    4. Stop and let the supplier answer.
    5. If the supplier has no tiers, add: most brands you work with have arrangements like that, so would they be open to discussing terms or incentives (rewards for good sales) for selling more of their products?

  • When asked for a wholesale discount (a lower buying price), explain the MAP margin reality (how much profit you really keep when you sell at MAP).

  • Ask whether wholesale can be negotiated so that wholesale is half of MAP, rather than asking only for cheaper pricing.

  • If the gap between wholesale and MAP is too narrow (too little profit between them) to promote the brand heavily, use this email:

Hello [Supplier contact name], while going through all our price sheets, I found that the gap between wholesale and MAP is too narrow for us to promote your [brand name] line heavily. We'd genuinely like to do that, but we need a bigger margin to fund marketing for your products. Is there any kind of incentive pricing available? Do you have volume discounts or tiered pricing, such as a percentage off wholesale once we hit a set sales target?
Thanks,
[Your name]
[Company name]
[Phone number]
  • For a phone version of the same request, use this script:
Hi, this is [your name] from [your brand]. We were looking over the price lists you sent us. We really want to put a real ad budget behind these products and become one of your top retailers, but the margins aren't where we need them to be. Do you have any incentive programs where, if retailers reach a certain number of units or a certain sales volume, wholesale prices get discounted? If not, please let me know so we can decide our next steps.
  • When a supplier asks about private label (products they make that you can put your own name on), answer in terms of the products that can be relabeled (given your own label), not as a unique product.

Shipping costs that erase the MAP margin

1 min
  • If shipping costs wipe out the margin at MAP (shipping eats all your profit), use this call script. A carrier is the delivery company that moves the parcel.
Hi [supplier contact name], this is [your name] from [your store name]. We are interested in selling [product name] and we are ready to move forward. It costs us about $[shipping cost] to ship one [product name], and if we sell at MAP of $[MAP price] we lose about $[loss per sale] on every sale. I can see that [competitor 1], [competitor 2] and [competitor 3] are all selling at MAP. Do you know which carrier they ship with, or do they have better wholesale pricing? Can you help us find a shipping method or wholesale price that lets us make money at MAP?
  • A supplier usually knows how other sellers ship products.
  • Ask them directly how competitors achieve profitable shipping, instead of guessing.

Checking MAP compliance and pricing discrepancies

1 min
  • MAP compliance means every seller keeps to the lowest-price rule. A discrepancy is a price that does not match that rule.
  • If you see a product listed below the MAP, ask the supplier to confirm before you assume an error on your side:
Hi [Supplier contact name], I am not sure if I am misreading your price list. Your MAP for [product name] is [MAP price], but I see this product listed for [lower price] at [link]. Can you confirm whether I have the MAP wrong, and let me know what is going on?

Promotions that conflict with MAP

1 min
  • Before running a bonus promotion (a free extra item with a purchase) that may conflict with MAP, ask for written approval:
Hello [SUPPLIER CONTACT], I'd like to run a promotion on our store. When a customer buys [PRODUCT X], they also receive [BONUS PRODUCT Y] free. I know this may conflict with your MAP policy, so I'm asking for your approval before launching. Could you confirm in writing whether you approve this promotion? Thank you, [YOUR NAME] [STORE NAME].
  • If the supplier declines, look for a similar product to use as the bonus instead.

Which other brands you work with

1 min
  • Do not lie about other brands.
  • If asked, name only the suppliers you are actually in an approval process with (you have already submitted an application, or were told one would be sent).

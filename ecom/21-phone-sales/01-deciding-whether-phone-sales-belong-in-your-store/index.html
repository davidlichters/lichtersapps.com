Deciding whether phone sales belong in your store

Section 21.1 · 1 of 26 in this chapter · 4 min read

In short

  • Above about $1,000, you need someone ready to answer and return calls fast.
  • Without that, keep prices around $1,000 and avoid expensive niches.
  • Phone calls build trust, and a call is usually a sign that someone wants to buy.
  • Above $1,500 to $3,000, expect many calls from buyers checking you are real, and fewer calls as reviews grow.

Phone as a trust signal

  • A visible phone number tells customers they could call if needed.
  • This is true even when few customers actually call.
  • Phone calls matter for trust (feeling safe to buy) even when few customers call.
  • The few who do call usually buy.
  • Keep a working phone number on the store and answer customer calls promptly.
  • Phone responsiveness (how fast you answer) affects conversions for higher-priced items.
  • This is older advice from 2020.

Inbound calls as a buying signal

  • A phone call on the store is usually a buying signal (a sign someone wants to buy), not a waste of time.
  • Over about 2.5 years, more than 90 percent of calls either led to a sale or came from existing customers.
  • Many shoppers call a store before buying.
  • Those who get an answer tend to buy from that store.
  • Answering the phone quickly and reliably is a competitive advantage (an edge over rival shops).
  • Answering the phone or returning calls generates significant sales volume that competitors miss.
  • Customers are often surprised that a human answers.
  • That surprise often leads them to place orders.
  • For established businesses (shops that have run for a while), this represents millions in annual revenue (yearly sales).
  • Phone calls before purchase are a normal part of selling products above about $1,500 to $3,000, because buyers check that the business is real.
  • Calls decrease as the store builds reviews and credibility (trust) over time.

Capacity you need before taking calls

  • A store with high-priced products needs someone who can answer customer phone calls.
  • Without that capacity, keep the average price in a lower range.
  • For high-ticket products, the store owner should be willing to handle phone calls.
  • The store owner should also be willing to handle extra emails.
  • That willingness is part of how higher order values are closed.
  • Only enter the $1,500 to $3,000 price range if you are willing to take these calls.
  • Return calls from high-ticket buyers quickly.
  • Respond to or call back high-ticket callers quickly.

High-ticket offers and live selling

  • Changing the selling environment (where and how you sell) is needed for high-ticket offers.
  • Few people will buy a very expensive product from a sales letter (a long written sales page) alone.
  • Move high-ticket offers to a phone call, direct mail (printed letters sent by post), or a live event.
  • An 8,000 USD buy button is unlikely to be clicked from an email.
  • A booked phone appointment makes the sale far more likely.
  • A phone conversation gives live feedback, so the salesperson can answer objections (customer doubts).
  • The salesperson can also help the prospect (a possible buyer) decide on the spot.
  • Listeners pay closer attention to a voice than to a page.
  • Use phone sales for offers that are too costly to close online.

Decision: phone work or lower prices

  1. Check whether you can answer and return calls quickly and reliably.
    • If yes, you can consider products in the higher bands above $1,000.
  2. If you cannot, keep prices in the lower band around $1,000.
    • Do not move into high-ticket niches (narrow markets of expensive products).
  3. If you want a hands-off business (one that runs without you taking sales calls), do not pick niches with an average price above roughly $2,000.
    • This applies if you want no phone sales at all.
  4. If you enter the $1,500 to $3,000 range, expect calls from buyers checking legitimacy.
    • Accept that these calls decline as reviews grow.

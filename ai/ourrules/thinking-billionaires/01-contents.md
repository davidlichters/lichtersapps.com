## 1. The starting question: if money is not what stops us from scaling, what is?

State on Friday 2026-11-06, 11:30. David's challenge was "your mindset is bad". The honest answer: the thinking was not too timid, it was pointed at the wrong quantities. Five things stop us, in this order:

1. **Proof.** Our new store's ads had 0 impressions at that moment. Planned spend was 17 EUR a day against an account limit of 74.35 EUR a day, so not even the limit was binding.
2. **The math per purchase.** Return on ad spend (ROAS) was 1.46 on average and 1.61 on the best campaign (our partner store); we need 2.5 to 3.
3. **Ad supply.** Of 55 ad images, 12 were running. A dry run on 2026-11-06 for a new product built 12 images, 0 of them ready to run.
4. **Account and limit.** One ad account, about 86 USD a day. The target runs would need about 9,900 USD a day at the peak [GUESS].
5. **Our own speed.** Of 103 hours 52 minutes from idea to switching the ads on, 92 hours 16 minutes were spent waiting on a human decision from David.

Boundary: this only holds once there is a filled test budget. Below that, money does slow us down: 69 hours 21 minutes of standstill were waiting for test money. Above about 11,500 USD in the till, extra cash buys almost no extra profit [GUESS].

## 2. The 10 rules that survived the counter-check

Each rule: the rule, the evidence (with source), the limits of the evidence, and what it changed for us. "Hours" in the changes count from 2026-11-06 11:30.

**1. Every attempt takes over something named from the last one.**
Evidence: successful and unsuccessful founders had the *same* number of failed attempts; only the successful group got better and faster from attempt 2 on. Source: Yin, Wang, Evans, Wang, preprint 2019, published in Nature 2019, https://arxiv.org/pdf/1903.07562 (full text).
Changed: every test card gets three fields within 32 hours: what was taken over, better in what, hours until start. Goal for attempt 2: under 52 hours instead of 103:52 [GUESS].

**2. Every test answers a question written down before it starts.**
Evidence: randomized trial with 116 startups; those who tested like scientists performed better. The performance effect is only shown in this small study. Source: Camuffo, Cordova, Gambardella, Spina, Management Science 2019/20, https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1287/mnsc.2018.3249 (abstract).
Changed: within 48 hours, every angle of the second ad batch has a written prediction of click price and click rate before upload. For the second product (an elastic full-body sleep sack): "Does anyone buy without a health claim?"

**3. The stop point is fixed with an amount and a date before money runs.**
Evidence: 51% of inventors kept going with their time and 29% with their money after clear advice to stop. That shows the problem; that a pre-set rule solves it was not tested in the study, it is our conclusion. Source: Åstebro and co-authors 2007, https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1002/bdm.554.
Changed: there was no rule for "ads do not deliver at all". New rule: if there are still 0 impressions at Sunday 06:10 (about 43 hours later), the cause is read and written down within 2 hours (review status, payment method, page).

**4. The risk of zero must be zero; after that, every single attempt may fail.**
No study behind it, just arithmetic: a single zero wipes out any streak of good results. Buffett on debt says it (a survivor, so per the counter-check not evidence, but the arithmetic holds for everyone). Source: https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2010ltr.pdf.
Changed: our "zero" is not an amount of money but our single ad account. Checked 2026-11-06 11:40, read-only: the Facebook page and the ad account both belong to David's own business portfolio for the store, and he has full control.

**5. Many cheap bets, each with a clear assumption.**
Evidence: 6% of US venture first rounds produced about 50% of the returns, and at one successful fund the partners' entry scores did not separate later winners from failures. Source: Kerr, Nanda, Rhodes-Kropf, NBER 20358, https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20358/w20358.pdf.
Changed: ten tests in one image style test only one assumption. Jumping around unconnected is still wrong (rule 1). Wave 1 must contain at least 3 image types within 10 days, counted on the test card.

**6. Stay narrow in one industry.**
Evidence: top-growth rate 0.12% without industry experience, 0.26% with 3+ years in the narrow industry. Measured on employment, so it is correlation. Source: Azoulay, Jones, Kim, Miranda, NBER 24489, https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w24489/w24489.pdf.
Changed: written down within 72 hours: our industry is US consumer goods sold through Meta ads; the product-finder pipeline stays inside it. This pays off from about age 21 and alone does not carry the 500,000 USD goal.

**7. The kind of work counts, not the number of hours.**
Evidence: hours rose in both groups; annual income went +11.6% in one and -16.5% in the other (same table). The first group already earned about 36% more before, so it is also selection. Source: Levine, Rubinstein, NBER 19276, https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w19276/w19276.pdf.
Changed: many hours are not an advantage by themselves. From Monday, log daily how many hours went into the actual bottleneck; target 2 of 5 business hours [GUESS]. For the AI: no new model calculation before the first real click number.

**8. Reach does not replace the math per purchase.**
Evidence: Pinduoduo, despite group buying where customers recruit customers, spent 102% of revenue on marketing in 2018 and still 29% in 2025 (our own division of SEC data). One company, one survivor: it only shows that reach does not save ad costs. Sources: https://data.sec.gov/api/xbrl/companyconcept/CIK0001737806/us-gaap/SellingAndMarketingExpense.json and https://data.sec.gov/api/xbrl/companyconcept/CIK0001737806/us-gaap/RevenueFromContractWithCustomerExcludingAssessedTax.json.
Changed: the sleep-sack product had only 0.18 USD of margin headroom, so within 96 hours we ask a second supplier for a quote (through our sending checks). The post-purchase upsell from wave 2 moves into wave 1, only if it passes the margin gate.

**9. Don't ask "how do we get more", ask "what breaks next".**
Evidence: at Shein marketing costs rose because stock was missing, not because ads were bad; the factories were won by paying on time. One company and our interpretation. Source: https://kr-asia.com/decoding-shein-growing-pains-and-bitter-splits-part-2-of-3.
Changed: from Monday 2026-11-09, every Monday one line: bottleneck, measured value, target, plus the ad account limit (target for 2027-01-31: 320 USD a day [GUESS]).

**10. People who know the business may kill a project.**
Evidence: more founders among randomly assigned business-school classmates reduced mainly the *failed* startups afterwards. Size of the effect not checked; the "why" is interpretation. Source: Lerner, Malmendier, Review of Financial Studies 2013, https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1093/rfs/hht024.
Changed: a judgment question to an experienced person drafted within 32 hours, David sends it within 96 hours, goal: within 9 days a written answer that is allowed to stop the sleep-sack product.

## 3. What the counter-check overturned

These are the mindsets billionaires name in interviews. Each one shows up just as often among people who failed, so none of them explains who wins.

| Mindset | Present in failures too? | Evidence | What actually separates |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strong belief in yourself | Yes. 2,994 founders rated their own chances far above average; badly prepared founders were as optimistic as well prepared ones; belief and objective success factors barely correlate | Cooper and co-authors 1988 | Nothing: belief is not a signal |
| Optimism as an engine | Yes, even harmful: more optimism, less revenue and employee growth, especially for experienced founders and in fast industries | Hmieleski, Baron 2009 | According to this study: less of it |
| Never give up | Yes. Same number of failed attempts as the successful | Finding 5 below | Building on the last attempt |
| Keep going against all advice | Yes. 51% of inventors continue with time and 29% with money after clear advice to stop; optimists spend 166% more; someone who sank 10,000 USD spends another 10,000 | Åstebro and co-authors 2007 | A stop rule fixed in advance |
| The effort pays off | Mostly no. Of 1,091 inventions, 7 to 9% reach the market; of those 75, 6 returned over 1,400%, 60% had negative returns, the median was negative | Åstebro 2003 | Very few hits carry everything |
| All on one card | Yes, typical of owners, with no extra return; 73% of venture-backed founders get zero exit value | Findings 9, 12 | You see survivors, not the others |
| Break the rules | Yes, also among simple self-employed people whose income falls | Finding 6 | Rule-breaking only together with ability |
| Work more than everyone | Yes. Hours rise in both groups, only one earns more | Finding 6 | The kind of work |
| Failure makes you stronger | Barely: 22.1% vs 20.9% success | Findings 3, 4 | Success repeats, failure barely teaches |
| Start young | Success rates are lowest in the early twenties | Finding 1 | The value of starting at 18 is the early years inside one industry |
| Copy billionaires | One interview is a sample of one. "Timing explains 42%" (Bill Gross, TED) is his own unpublished analysis of about 200 companies, which he himself calls not final | Finding 11, TED transcript | Nothing reliable |

## 4. The evidence base in detail

All fetched 2026-11-06. "Full text" means the working paper or article was read; "abstract" means only the summary via OpenAlex, because the publisher blocks downloads (AOM, Oxford, Wiley, PNAS, AEA, SSRN).

Summary: what actually separates in large data is experience in exactly this industry, earlier success, cognitive strength combined with rule-breaking, family background, and whether the next attempt builds on the last. Even professionals can barely spot winners in advance. A large rest is luck, and nobody has measured it cleanly in percent.

1. **Age: the young founder is the exception.** [EVIDENCE] 2.7 million US founders 2007 to 2014 (firms with at least one employee, Census Bureau data). Average age 41.9; among the top 1 in 1,000 fastest-growing firms 45.0. A 50-year-old founder has a 1.8 times higher chance of top growth than a 30-year-old. The rate is lowest in the early twenties. Successful sale or IPO: about 4,000 cases, 0.15%. Limit: success means employee growth or exit, not profit; sole proprietors excluded. Source: NBER 24489 (Azoulay, Jones, Kim, Miranda 2018), full text.
2. **Industry experience: the strongest clear single value.** [EVIDENCE] Top growth (top 0.1%): 0.11% without experience in the broad industry, 0.22% with 3+ years in it, 0.26% with 3+ years in the narrow industry. Only 1 to 2 years in the broad industry adds nothing (0.11%). Absolute numbers stay tiny. Correlation. Same source, table 6 (cohorts 2007 to 2009).
3. **Serial founders: success repeats, failure barely helps.** [EVIDENCE] 9,932 firms of 8,808 founders, venture-backed only, 1975 to 2000, success = IPO or filing. Success rate 30.6% after an earlier success, 22.1% after an earlier failure, 20.9% first time. Once earlier success is in the model, plain experience loses significance; authors read this as skill. Limit: earlier winners also raise money more easily. Source: Gompers, Kovner, Lerner, Scharfstein, NBER 12592, 2006, full text.
4. **Learning exists, but small.** [EVIDENCE] Almost 2.3 million small retail businesses in Texas 1990 to 2011; only 25% from repeat owners. Having run an earlier business lowers the closing probability by 7%, also comparing the same person with themselves. Each additional earlier, now closed, business: -3.8%; still-running ones add nothing measurable. Source: Lafontaine, Shaw, NBER 20312, 2014, full text.
5. **Persistence does not separate, building does.** [EVIDENCE] 58,111 startups, 253,579 people (VentureXpert 1970 to 2016), plus research grant applications and terror attacks as comparison. Those who eventually succeeded made neither more nor fewer failed attempts than those who did not (startups: p = 0.175, distributions indistinguishable). The first attempt is indistinguishable too. From the second attempt only the success group improves, and the gaps between their attempts shrink. The authors' model: below a threshold people try unrelated things; above it they refine what they had step by step. The timing of the first three failures predicts the outcome of grant applications with an AUC near 0.7. Source: arXiv 1903.07562, Nature 2019, preprint full text.
6. **"Smart and rule-breaking": only the combination counts.** [EVIDENCE] NLSY79, 12,686 people followed from youth, plus CPS. Rule-breaking as a teenager shows up both in later owners of incorporated businesses and in later simple self-employed. Only the first group also had high test scores. Switching into an incorporated business: income +18%. Switching into simple self-employment: -16.5% mean, -14.9% median. The first group already earned about 34% more as employees than comparable employees. +100,000 USD family income: more than 50% higher chance of the incorporated route. Both groups work more hours after switching. Source: Levine, Rubinstein, NBER 19276, 2015 version, full text.
7. **Background: many never start.** [EVIDENCE] 1.2 million inventors (patents linked to tax data) plus 2.5 million New York school children. 8 in 1,000 children from the top 1% become inventors, ten times the rate below the median. 3rd-grade math explains less than a third of the gap. Exposure to inventors as a child causally raises inventing. Source: Bell, Chetty, Jaravel, Petkova, Van Reenen, NBER 24062. Forbes 400 1982 to 2012: more often company founders and more often upper middle class, not rich families (Kaplan, Rauh, AER 2013, abstract); self-made share rising but heirs stay on the list longer (Korom, Lutter, Beckert, MPIfG 15/8, 2015).
8. **Hybrid start: direction shown, size not checked.** [EVIDENCE, abstract] Founding next to a job and then switching survives clearly more often than jumping in full-time; stronger with earlier founding experience. Hybrids are also more risk-averse and less self-confident, so groups are not random. Source: Raffiee, Feng, Academy of Management Journal. No percentage was available, so none is stated.
9. **The distribution is extremely skewed, and professionals cannot see winners in advance.** [EVIDENCE] All US venture first rounds 1985 to 2009: about 55% ended with a loss, 6% returned more than 5x and together about 50% of gross returns. At one successful fund with over 1 billion USD invested, partner scores at entry did not statistically differ between later huge successes and failures (NBER 20358). 20,961 venture-backed US firms from 1987: 73% deliver zero exit value to the founder; mean 4.4 million USD, carried by few hits; with relative risk aversion of 2 and under 0.7 million USD of wealth, an employee job would be better on paper (Hall, Woodward, NBER 14219, 2008).
10. **Luck in an experiment: same product, different fate.** [EVIDENCE] 14,341 participants, 48 unknown songs, eight parallel worlds with identical starts. Visible download counts increase inequality and unpredictability. The best songs never do very badly, the worst never extremely well; in between almost anything is possible. The cleanest experiment on the topic because history can be replayed. Limit: music, not business; quality sets a floor and a ceiling, no more. Source: Salganik, Dodds, Watts, Science 2006.
11. **Why copying winners misleads.** [EVIDENCE] If you only see survivors you underestimate failure, and risky practices without real benefit look success-promoting because their dead are missing (Denrell, Organization Science 2003). If extreme success needs luck, it says little about skill, and the very best should not necessarily be copied (Denrell and co-author, PNAS 2012). Simulation of 1,000 agents over 40 years: the richest had talent 0.61 at a mean of 0.6, the most talented (0.89) ended below starting capital (Pluchino and co-authors 2018; only proof of the possible). Of the 11 "Good to Great" companies, only one kept superior stock performance by Collins' own yardstick in a re-check (Resnick, Smunt 2008, original not read).
12. **Average returns: the mass pays extra.** [EVIDENCE] Self-employed earn 35% less in the median than in employment after 10 years, not because they are worse people (Hamilton, JPE 2000, abstract). About 75% of private business equity belongs to households with at least half their wealth in it, on average over 70% in a single firm, with returns no higher than the stock market (Moskowitz, Vissing-Jørgensen, NBER 8876). More than half of new founders name non-financial reasons as main motive, only 34% income (Hurst, Pugsley, NBER 17041).
13. **What can be trained: testing hypotheses.** [EVIDENCE] The 116-startup randomized trial (rule 2). Replication with 759 firms in four experiments: more ideas are ended, a few targeted pivots instead of none or many; no revenue effect stated in the abstract (Strategic Management Journal 2024). Randomly assigned business-school sections (rule 10): honest feedback from people who know filters bad projects.

Timing: [LEGEND] Bill Gross's "Timing accounted for 42 percent" is his own estimate. Indirectly: experienced VCs raised investments most when the stock market gave good signals, with little loss of success rate (NBER 11385). No founder timing number exists in what we could fetch.

## 5. Founders with little capital: the first five years

Seven founders checked, sources fetched 2026-11-06.

- **"Little capital" is only true for Blakely (Spanx), Knight (Nike) and Musk's Zip2.** Blakely: 5,000 USD, all her savings. Knight: 50 or 500 USD from his father (sources contradict). Musk Zip2: about 2,000 USD own, 15,000 USD among three people, later about 200,000 USD from angels. Walton: 25,000 USD (20,000 loan from his father-in-law, 5,000 own), about 447,000 USD in today's money. Bezos: 10,000 USD for shares plus 84,000 USD own loans, then about 245,600 USD from his parents (our calculation from share counts at 0.1717 USD). Koum (WhatsApp): about 400,000 USD own savings from Yahoo, then 250,000 USD seed. Musk at SpaceX and Tesla: 180 million USD from PayPal, not a low-capital case.
- **[CONCLUSION] The hardest common line is not courage but cushioning:** keep income, put a number on the chance in advance, keep costs near zero. Blakely kept her sales job for two years and quit two weeks before the Oprah show of November 2000. Knight worked as an accountant until 1969, five years after the 1964 handshake. Kamprad ran IKEA evenings and weekends during military service. Counter-examples: Bezos quit before starting; Koum lived on savings.
- **Say the chance as a number beforehand.** Bezos told his parents 70% total loss and gave himself 30%; decided to quit in 48 hours with the "regret minimization" question (what will I regret at 80?). Musk expected under 10% at SpaceX and accepted the total loss in advance, saying it lowered the fear.
- **The start was a measured gap, not a vision.** Kamprad: pencil at the wholesaler 0.5 öre, in the shop 10 öre, twenty times. Musk: raw material of a rocket about 2% of the usual price. Koum: SMS billed per message, a "cash cow for carriers".
- **What they explicitly did not do.** Koum and Acton: no ads as business model, no press, no venture money until an investor chased them for eight months, no salary in the first years. Blakely: wrote her own patent (saved 3,000 USD), no investors, debt or advertising until 2012, 100% ownership. Kamprad: no lawsuits against copycats, a better model instead. Musk did the opposite at Zip2 (took 3 million USD, lost the CEO role), so avoiding investors is no law.
- **Walton, Musk and Koum were close to the end early; a second person, chance or an outside event saved them, not a plan.** Musk 2008: three failed launches, "running on fumes"; flight four succeeded 2008-09-28, then the NASA contract and Tesla investor money two days later on Christmas Eve; he split his last 30 million USD between both companies and took 33,280 USD salary as Tesla CEO to save cash. Koum 2009: wanted to quit; Acton talked him into a few more months; Apple's push notifications (June 2009) and users using status messages as chat led to version 2.0 and 250,000 active users.
- **Seven survivors prove no rule.** The large data says industry experience beats youth.

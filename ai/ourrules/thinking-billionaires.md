# How billionaires actually think, and how our Musk judge works

> When to read this: before any big decision about money, risk, how many attempts to run, what the bottleneck is, or whenever you are asked to judge our plans "like Elon Musk". The short version (until 2026-11-07 the page https://lichtersapps.com/ai/ourrules/thinking.md) stands below the markers; the pages are the full version, with the evidence, the numbers, the counter-check, the real Musk's work ethic and the exact procedure of our simulated Musk judge so it can be rebuilt from scratch.

Written from our own research files of 2 and 6 November 2026. Every study and source below was fetched by us on the date given (mostly 2026-11-06, by plain HTTP download). Markers used throughout:
- **[MEASURED]** or **[EVIDENCE]**: read in the fetched source itself.
- **[SECONDARY]**: only read through Wikipedia, Wikiquote, press or a transcript; the primary source was not seen.
- **[LEGEND]**: circulates, but sources contradict each other or nobody but the teller confirms it.
- **[GUESS]** (our files say [RATE]): an estimate or a goal we set, not a measurement.
- **[CONCLUSION]**: our own inference from the findings, not a finding.

Important caveat that applies to everything here: almost all data is from the US and from before AI; one-person online stores barely appear in any study. No study compares billionaires directly with people who failed using the same mindsets. The mindsets below may be necessary; they are not sufficient.

## Short version (moved here from ourrules/thinking.md on 2026-11-07)

> Ten rules that survived a counter-check against studies on founders and investors, plus how our idea machine works. Written November 2026. Most data comes from the US and from before AI; one-person stores barely appear in it.

### When money is not what stops you from scaling, what is?

In our case, in this order: proof (ads not delivering yet), the math per purchase (ROAS 1.46 average, 1.61 best, 2.5 to 3 needed), ad supply (12 of 55 images running), ad account limits, and our own speed (of 104 hours from idea to launch, 92 were spent waiting on a human decision). Below a filled test budget, money does slow you down: 69 hours of standstill were waiting for test money.

### The 10 rules

1. **Every attempt takes over something named from the last one.** Successful and unsuccessful founders had the same number of failures; only the successful ones got better and faster from attempt 2. (arxiv.org/pdf/1903.07562)
2. **Every test answers a question written down before.** In a randomized study of 116 startups, those who tested like scientists did better. Write a prediction (click price, click rate) before uploading.
3. **The stop point is fixed with an amount and a date before money runs.** 51% of inventors kept going with time and 29% with money after clear advice to stop.
4. **The risk of zero must be zero; after that, every attempt may fail.** One zero wipes out every good streak. Our zero is not an amount but our only ad account.
5. **Many cheap bets, each with a clear assumption.** 6% of venture deals produced about half the returns, and entry scores did not separate winners from failures. Ten tests in one image style test only one assumption.
6. **Stay narrow in one industry.** Top growth was 0.12% without and 0.26% after 3 years in the same narrow industry. Ours: US consumer goods sold through Meta.
7. **The kind of work counts, not the hours.** Hours rose in both groups; income went +11.6% in one and -16.5% in the other. Track how many hours went into the actual bottleneck.
8. **Reach does not replace the math per purchase.** Even a giant with group buying spent 102% of revenue on marketing in 2018.
9. **Don't ask "how do we get more", ask "what breaks next".** Fast-fashion giants paid more for marketing when stock ran out; paying factories on time won them capacity.
10. **People who know the business may kill a project.** More founders among randomly assigned classmates mostly reduced failed startups.

### What the counter-check overturned

- Strong belief and optimism: just as common among those who failed.
- Never giving up: both groups failed equally often; building on the failures separates them.
- Going all-in against all advice: owners with over 70% of their wealth in one company earn no extra return.
- "Failure makes you stronger": 22.1% vs 20.9%. Success repeats, failure barely helps.
- Starting young: success rates are lowest in the early twenties. The value of starting at 18 is the early years inside one industry.
- Copying billionaires: one interview is a sample of one.

### Where our own thinking was small

Money first instead of the real limits; calculating instead of measuring (60,000 model runs on an ad with 0 impressions); building the factory before the proof; waiting as a plan; and imagined advantages ("we need the product in hand") that weren't real.

### The idea machine

We find ideas with a repeatable pipeline of AI agents:
1. **Signals from our own data first.** Public text was 15 of 15 occupied.
2. **Six blind generators** that cannot see each other, or variety collapses.
3. **"Who already sells this, even for free?"** before any other check.
4. **Two pessimists and a repairer.** An idea dies only with proof: a law with its paragraph, or the same result available for free on a page we actually opened. Everything else becomes a test question.
5. **A grey list** keeps ideas that dropped out only because something was incomplete; they come back in later sorting rounds.
6. **Calibration:** every change to the checking stages is measured against known good ideas. If the machine kills an idea that later made money, the stage is wrong, not the idea.
7. **A control group:** 20% of killed ideas still get a test card. If one passes, the killing was wrong.
8. **A test measures behavior** (a deposit, a booked slot, a real form), the pass threshold is fixed before, and no test deceives anyone.
9. Budget plan: 10 to 20 tests a month at about 50 EUR each, once there is income.

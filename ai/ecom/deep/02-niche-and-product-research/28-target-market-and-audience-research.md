## Target Market and Audience Research

### Market Definition: Start With the Customer

- Define the market by the customer, their interests and their needs, not by the product list. Choose products afterwards to serve that customer, because research that starts from products alone leads to weak markets. ** [L0771, 2021-22]
- Start from the customer rather than the product. Define the ideal customer first (their problems, where they search, who they trust and what they spend), then select products and markets that serve them. [L0414, 2021-22]
- Write down where the business and its products sit in the market. Competitor analysis, market research and a written outline of the business structure belong together, because they decide positioning and target customers. ** [L0761, 2021-22]
- Search for a winning problem in a niche, not a winning product. Find small, specific niches with large addressable markets. [L0307, 2023+]
- The same product sold by many competitors can succeed if it is repositioned to solve a different problem or to target a different emotion. Example: a tea brand does not sell taste but benefits such as focus, energy or libido, using a white-label manufacturer. The product is the same as competitors', but the market positioning differs. [L0307, 2023+]
- Research a market deeply once, then reuse that knowledge for every product in the same niche. Repeat the full research phase only when you enter a new market. [L0378, 2019+]
- Define one dream customer as the persona for all product research, then ask which product would solve that persona's problem. Across several years of testing, women over 40 proved to be the most responsive audience for this approach. [L0492, 2023+]
- Russell Brunson's *Dot Com Secrets* is a practical starting point for defining a clear dream customer before product research. His companion books, *Traffic Secrets* and *Expert Secrets*, cover traffic and positioning. [L0492, 2023+]

### Niche Selection Filters

- A market should pass the standard filter: a price point suited to high-ticket selling, reliable shipping, enough suppliers and sufficient traffic volume. Check competition as a separate step, because an idea can meet all four criteria and still be crowded. ** [L0905, 2021-22]
- Prefer enthusiast markets, where buyers purchase out of passion, such as horse riding, RV solar kits or boating accessories. Avoid needs-driven products such as refrigerators, because those markets are harder to sell into and usually more competitive. ** [L0771, 2021-22]
- Choose a niche where you already have knowledge or passion, not complete ignorance. This speeds up problem identification, audience understanding and solution positioning. Example: in fitness, identify real gym-consistency problems (New Year motivation, lack of home workout tools) and solve those. Avoid niches like video games if you have no knowledge, because you will not identify real customer problems. [L0307, 2023+]
- Gym example: after New Year, members are highly motivated but fail to keep up habits. They abandon memberships within weeks because of busy schedules, lost motivation and lack of accountability. Position products around "stay consistent" and "remove distractions" rather than "get fit." Solve the real problem (motivation maintenance, not only fitness results). Relevant solutions include home workout tools, accountability coaching, habit trackers and time-management courses. [L0307, 2023+]
- Choose a target market whose members are proud of and passionate about their hobby, skill, background or trait, and who are easy to identify on social platforms. Passion for the mission matters more than the product itself. [L0487, 2018]
- Your target market should be proud of its hobbies, skills, backgrounds or traits; passionate about its interests; easily identifiable as a group; and specific enough to count as a niche. [L0237, 2018]
- Avoid markets where people have no passion or feel uncomfortable talking about the topic publicly. They will not share or tag friends even if they buy. Health-related products can fail this test even when they sell well. [L0487, 2018]
- Look for niches that support recurring purchases, seasonal packages and educational content. Check whether competitors are dull or uneducational. Many long-established stores are weak at education, which leaves room for a new brand to differentiate. [L0487, 2018]
- Focus on a select group of people who share the same underlying problem rather than targeting everyone. A narrow group is easier to reach, and a service that solves its shared problem is easier to sell repeatedly. [L0701, 2015]
- Enthusiasm must belong to your customers, not to you as the seller. You do not have to share their passion to sell to them well. ** [L0994, 2021-22]

#### Crowded Markets

- Even in a crowded market, a product can succeed by finding a narrower marketing angle, because a broad audience dilutes the appeal of any single message. Look for a specific group within the market rather than assuming the category is closed. [L0997, undated; L0998, undated]
- In a saturated market, narrowing the audience to a specific group can reveal marketing angles that a broad product ad cannot. The same product can stand out when positioned for one defined buyer group instead of everyone in the category. [L0997, undated; L0998, undated]
- In a market that is not very competitive, a focused customer segment may not be needed at the start. In a crowded market full of general stores, pick one target customer and build the whole business around that person. ** [L0905, 2021-22]
- Electric bikes are a crowded high-ticket category, with up to 30 high-ticket dropshipping stores selling similar bikes and brands. Possible buyer segments include commuters, surfers who ride to the beach, hunters, retirees and people who travel with RVs. ** [L0905, 2021-22]
- Market size matters for long-term brand planning once sales reach 20k to 30k. A market of 10 billion USD with a competitor at 100 million USD is a reference point for deciding whether to compete. [L0895, 2021+]

#### Market Validation Procedure

**

1. Run the broad market through the standard criteria: price point, shipping, number of suppliers and traffic volume. [L0905, 2021-22]
2. Check whether customers exist for the products and whether the product fits them. [L0905, 2021-22]
3. If the market meets the criteria but looks too competitive, narrow the focus to a subset of customers that no competitor speaks to directly. [L0905, 2021-22]
4. If such a subset is found, proceed with the market even if the broad market looks crowded. [L0905, 2021-22]

### Learning an Unfamiliar Niche

- Learning a new niche is feasible in about a week. Spend that week on videos, reviews, beginner guides and websites selling the product. This gives enough knowledge to talk with suppliers and customers and to build a credible site. Deeper expert knowledge comes later through practice. ** [L0357, 2019]
- You do not need existing expertise in your niche. Position yourself through direct manufacturer relationships that let you answer customer questions by consulting suppliers, and keep a frequently asked questions section on your site. [L0031, undated]
- To learn an unfamiliar niche quickly **:
  1. Set aside about one hour a day for one week. [L0247, 2021]
  2. Read introductory books or watch introductory videos on the niche. [L0247, 2021]
  3. Aim for a conversational understanding, not expert knowledge. [L0247, 2021]

### Target Audience Research: Sources and Methods

- Target audience research should be enough to know the problem, the customer, and their needs, pain points and motivations. It should not become a long research project. Thirty minutes of reading Amazon reviews for recurring trends is often enough to pick an angle and start testing. Over-researching can stall a launch. [L0552, 2023]
- Quick method for a product:
  1. Read Amazon reviews of comparable products and note recurring trends. [L0552, 2023]
  2. Note the demographics, psychographics, needs and motivations of the buyers. [L0552, 2023]
  3. Use those findings to pick one angle to test. [L0552, 2023]
  4. Refine the audience later if time allows. [L0552, 2023]
- Before any market research starts, ask for the brand website or key product information. Every later step depends on it, and without that input the research cannot be grounded. [L0613, 2026]
- Audience research for a physical product, in order:
  1. Start with a plain Google search for the product category. Note which product types, settings and pet or user types come up. [L0450, 2022+]
  2. Search forums for buyer questions and recommendations about the category (for example, "best pet hair remover") and read the replies. [L0450, 2022+]
  3. Read Amazon reviews of the top competing products and of their rivals. [L0450, 2022+]
  4. Look up data and statistics sites (Statista, Google Trends) for hard numbers such as ownership counts and popularity. [L0450, 2022+]
  5. Check current news for the niche to find topics and events your audience is discussing. [L0450, 2022+]
  6. Use AI research tools as a supplement, never as a replacement for forums and Amazon. ChatGPT is useful for analysis; AgentGPT can run automated web research toward a goal such as "Research three target audiences for XYZ niche." [L0450, 2022+]
  7. Check competitor stores and their social accounts, the Meta Ad Library and Instagram to see how they market and who follows them. [L0450, 2022+]
  8. Find the most-followed brands in the wider category and read their audience's comments. [L0450, 2022+]
  9. Dig into the problem behind the purchase (for example, health or allergy effects) and build the persona from what you find. [L0450, 2022+]
- Statista and Google Trends provide hard data and statistics for a niche, such as breed popularity or ownership numbers. News sources reveal current events that can serve as conversation topics for the target audience. [L0450, 2022+]
- Competitor research (sites, social accounts, customer comments) is one of the most important ways to identify the target audience and find gaps. A product's target audience must be defined before writing ads or messaging. [L0497, undated]
- Check breed or type popularity with authoritative sources to see which types of owners form a large audience. For example, the American Kennel Club publishes a yearly list of the most popular dog breeds. [L0450, 2022+]
- The AKC's 2022 ranking of the most popular dog breeds placed French Bulldog, Labrador Retriever, Golden Retriever and German Shepherd in the top four. This figure is dated (2022). Use the current ranking to size dog-owner segments by breed before choosing an audience. [L0450, 2022+]
- Among breeds on a worst-shedding list, German Shepherd, Welsh Corgi, Chow Chow and Pyrenees are named as heavy shedders. For a pet hair product, owners of these breeds form segments that can be reached through breed interests in ads. [L0450, 2022+]
- A heavy-shedding breed segment can be targeted on Facebook through interests such as a particular breed. [L0450, 2022+]
- Gift buyers can make a product sell beyond its target audience. On TikTok, a product aimed at one group may be bought by relatives or partners. Include in the ad buyer persona those who purchase for the target customer. Comments that mention buying for a parent, partner or relative signal this. [L0819, 2020+]

#### Hunting for Dream Customers Before Launch

- Find out whether a niche responds before investing in a store, by publishing content and watching how people react. Engagement is a cheap signal that tells you whether the path is right before you build products. [L0487, 2018]
- Find dream customers before the store launches, in this order:
  1. Identify the target market and who these people are. [L0487, 2018]
  2. Define the brand persona and brand voice. [L0487, 2018]
  3. Create content for the target market. [L0487, 2018]
  4. Go to the platforms where they are active and find them there. [L0487, 2018]

### Demographics, Psychographics, Behaviour and Motivation

#### The Four Pillars

- Target audience research has four pillars: demographics, psychographics, behaviour, and goals, needs and motivations. Goals, needs and motivations sit below the surface and reveal what the buyer is trying to fulfil or escape. [L0353, 2023]
- Audience segmentation rests on four pillars. Goals, needs and motivations are the most important and should be prioritised over the others. [L0497, undated]
- Needs and motivations are the most important pillar because they explain why someone buys. Demographics, psychographics and behaviours are the other three pillars. [L0995, 2023]
- Segmentation rests on four components, each describing the audience from a different level, from surface facts to deep drivers. [L0997, undated]
- Four components of audience segmentation:

| Component | What it describes | Examples | Depth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Demographics | Static traits of a person | Age, gender, income level, education, marital status | Surface level, define last |
| Psychographics | Personality, values, beliefs, attitudes | Openness, conscientiousness, organization, tendency to worry, need for control or belonging, religious views, opinions about the world | Middle depth |
| Behavior | What people do and in which context | Browsing a product, using a product, the trigger that causes the need, where the problem fits into their day | Observable |
| Goals, needs and motivations | The underlying reason behind the behavior | Status, freedom, security, belonging, fear of falling behind | Deepest, most persuasive |

  [L0998, undated]
- A complete audience profile across all four pillars is not required before starting. Build the picture step by step and fill the gaps as far as possible, weighting motivations and goals above demographics. [L0497, undated]
- Segment the audience by demographics (who they are, such as dog or cat owner, age), psychographics (what they value and fear), behaviour (what they do and which solutions they already use), and needs and motivations (what they desperately want, what they fear, their biggest problem and their trigger). Keep the research simple and fill in what you can. [L0450, 2022+]

#### Demographics

- Demographics are static statistical facts about a person, including age, gender, income, geolocation, occupation and education level. [L0497, undated]
- Demographics are static attributes such as age, gender, income level, education and marital status. They are surface-level and should usually be the last layer you use when defining a target audience. [L0997, undated]
- Demographics describe who someone is, not their personality. They are useful as a filter but are the weakest basis for a message. [L0998, undated]
- Demographic data alone is a weak targeting tool. The further you move from underlying motivations toward demographics, the less important targeting becomes, although demographics still help describe a segment. [L0497, undated]
- Demographics alone tell you who a buyer is, but not what they do or why. Combining demographics, behaviour and motivation gives a clearer picture, which makes marketing messages stronger. [L0644, undated]
- Demographics can decide whether a product fits at all, for example when a product only makes sense for a certain life stage. Users outside that range are excluded, even if their other traits match. [L0997, undated]
- Example of a demographic filter deciding fit: the average age at which menstruation ends is over 50, so for a menstruation-related product, buyers above that age are generally excluded regardless of their other traits. Check life-stage fit at the demographic layer before spending time on psychological targeting. [L0997, undated]
- For a status-driven jewellery piece, buyers might span an age range of 20 to 50. Age matters much less than the underlying desire for status, so the demographic range is informative but rarely the core of the message. [L0997, undated]
- Age and other demographics matter less than the problem and goal. Refine them later if needed. [L0450, 2022+]
- Demographics are useful as a filter, not as a targeting engine. Check them after you have defined the problem and motivation. [L0998, undated; L0497, undated]
- Demographics are the surface layer. Define them last, once the motivations and problem are clear. [L0997, undated]

#### Psychographics

- Psychographics describe who a person is: personality traits (for example openness, neuroticism, conscientiousness), values and beliefs (for example peace of mind, control, power, integrity), attitudes and opinions, lifestyle preferences, interests and hobbies, and emotional tendencies such as anger or feeling overwhelmed. [L0497, undated]
- Psychographics cover personality traits and how a person thinks: openness, conscientiousness, organisation, tendency to worry, core values such as control or belonging, beliefs about the world, religion and attitudes toward specific things. [L0997, undated]
- Psychographics include personality traits and attitudes toward buying, such as price consciousness or status. [L0995, 2023]
- Psychographics show what the buyer values and fears, and they sit in the middle of the depth scale. [L0450, 2022+; L0998, undated]
- Behaviours and psychographics are the surface layer you can observe, but they seldom reveal why someone acts. [L0497, undated]

#### Behaviour

- Behavioural segmentation asks what the person does at the moment they experience the problem, or when the product would fit into their day. Example: rushing to a subway while grabbing breakfast. [L0497, undated]
- Behaviours cover what people do and achieve, the context in which they do it, and the trigger that makes the problem appear. Define where the product fits into the person's routine or life journey. [L0997, undated]
- A vegetable slicer fits the context of someone preparing food in the kitchen. The trigger is cutting vegetables badly, which gives uneven, tough and frustrating results. [L0997, undated]
- Behavioural research checklist:
  1. What is the person doing when the problem occurs, or where does the product fit in their timeline? [L0497, undated]
  2. Which event triggers the problem (for example, lack of sleep causing a late wake-up, or no time to cook breakfast)? [L0497, undated]
  3. What is their attention like at that moment (a crowded or loud place, waiting in line, attention split across other things)? [L0497, undated]
  4. Other behavioural traits: online browsing habits, purchase frequency, where they spend time on social media, and which media they use to communicate. [L0497, undated]
- Behaviours and triggers appear in the buyer's day. Record where and when the problem shows up and what sets it off. [L0450, 2022+]
- Observable behaviour is the tip of the iceberg. Demographics and behaviour are what you can observe. [L0997, undated]

#### Needs, Goals and Motivations

- Demographics and behaviours are what you can observe, while goals, needs and motivations are the underlying why behind what people do. Tapping into these needs is far more powerful than describing observable traits. [L0997, undated]
- Always focus on the underlying need or motivation behind a customer's goal, and whether the customer is moving toward a desired outcome or away from a problem. Positioning that addresses this drives more action than surface features. [L0450, 2022+]
- Goals, needs and motivations are the most important pillar, and they should be prioritised. [L0497, undated]
- Target audience research should start from the fears and pains of the buyer, not from product features. Each persona must be backed by a clear push and pull: what the buyer wants to move toward and what they want to move away from. [L0395, undated]
- Push and pull: split motivation into what the buyer moves toward and what they move away from. [L0395, undated]
- Target audience research should go well beyond age, gender and income. Define the audience's pains, passions, desires, daily search terms, magazines and topics. Use your own past experience with the problem as a guide to what they care about. [L0523, 2015+]
- The motivation behind the purchase often comes from a health or practical consequence. Example: pet dander causes itchy eyes and runny nose, and allergies disrupt sleep. Problems linked to health give motivation that can be used in marketing. [L0450, 2022+]
- Every buyer has a reason for what they do, even when the visible behaviour looks simple. You do not have to state deep motives openly in your ads, but you must understand your audience closely enough to speak to them at that level. [L0997, undated]
- Status and self-image often drive a choice more deeply than convenience. People often choose a coffee chain for its environment and for what being seen there signals about them, not because its coffee is the best. [L0997, undated]
- Every human need is a trade-off between opposing dimensions:
  - Social: power vs. belonging, conviviality vs. standing out and recognition. [L0497, undated]
  - Personal: enjoyment and carefree fun vs. control and order, vitality and freedom vs. security and safety. [L0497, undated]
  - Choosing one means giving up some of the other. [L0497, undated]

#### Laddering

- Behaviours and psychographics are surface layers. Ask "why" repeatedly (laddering) to uncover tensions, fears and underlying human drivers beneath an observed behaviour. [L0497, undated]
- Laddering example for a morning coffee and bagel purchase:
  1. Observed behaviour: buying coffee and a bagel on the way to work. [L0497, undated]
  2. Reason: no time to sit down for breakfast. [L0497, undated]
  3. Fear behind it: being late or missing something at work. [L0497, undated]
  4. Deeper driver: feeling shame, losing control over one's destiny or significance. [L0497, undated]
  5. Keep asking why until the deep human need appears. [L0497, undated]
- The iceberg model: what is visible at the tip is demographics and behaviour, and everything below the surface is the needs, tensions and motivations that explain it. Your job is to uncover the layer beneath the visible behaviour. [L0997, undated]
- Coffee and bagel example in iceberg terms: lacking time for breakfast is a need. Beneath it lies the fear of being late and missing something, which connects to shame, lost promotion, loss of control and financial insecurity. [L0997, undated]
- Core human drivers to test against a segment (the list is not exhaustive):
  - Power or control over outcomes [L0497, undated]
  - Freedom from pain [L0497, undated]
  - Gaining or conserving resources (time, money, energy) [L0497, undated]
  - Status and superiority [L0497, undated]
  - Connection, love and belonging [L0497, undated]
  - Sexual selection (being a valuable mate) [L0497, undated]
  - Keeping up with the Joneses (being at least normal) [L0497, undated]
  - Growth [L0497, undated]
  - Looking after loved ones [L0497, undated]
- Motorcycle gear case, Rurock: the brand entered a market flooded with helmets, leather jackets and trousers. Its target goal was to wear clothing the rider likes and express identity, look risky and rebellious, instead of wearing conventional leathers, uncomfortable boots or ugly helmets. Behaviours were riding naked bikes, pulling wheelies and avoiding conventional clothing. Psychographics showed they valued style over substance and would sacrifice some safety for better looks. Their helmets were not the safest or quietest but had designs the audience connected with. Ages ranged from 20 to 60, so demographics mattered less than the motivations behind the segment. [L0497, undated]

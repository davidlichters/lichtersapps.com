### Recurring revenue and traffic volume

#### Recurring revenue

- A market has recurring sales potential if the customer will buy several different product types over time, or if the main product has accessories or add-ons. A coffee machine can be followed by a grinder and mini roaster, and generators by log splitters and snow blowers. [L0458, 2021-22; older]
- Recurring revenue means one customer can buy several different product types from the niche over time. A durable item bought once and lasting for years gives little recurring revenue. [L0490, 2021-22; older]
- Repeat sales to the same customer carry no acquisition cost, so margins on the second and third sale are often higher. A niche that supports several products is worth more than a single high-ticket item. [L0458, 2021-22; older]
- Score every shortlisted market idea Yes or No for recurring revenue. Answer Yes only if the market offers several different products that would appeal to the same customer and could be sold to them over time. [L1485, 2021-22; older]
- Example: a 4x4 accessories niche scores Yes, because one buyer can later purchase rear cargo drawers, a nudge bar, a turbo system, then suspension or wheels, with email used to bring them back. A niche selling only expensive, long-lasting CNC machines scores No, because buyers rarely replace them and rarely buy a second unit of the same product. [L1485, 2021-22; older]

Procedure, checking recurring revenue when unsure. [L1485, 2021-22; older]

1. Open the competitor websites you already collected in competition research.
2. Check whether they sell a range of different products.
3. Check whether those products would appeal to the same customer.
4. If both are true, mark recurring revenue as Yes; otherwise mark it No.

- Recurring sales in other niches: games and entertainment (customers buy more games once bored with the first), subscription models (customers rebuy mop heads and replacement parts), and fashion (seasonal changes quarterly). [L0324, 2025+]

#### Traffic volume

- Traffic volume is the combined monthly searches across the niche's products. The first example niche had at least 20,000 searches per month, which was far above the traffic targets in every country discussed. [L0490, 2021-22; older]
- Measure traffic volume as the number of people searching each month for the product types you plan to sell on the Google search network. Google is the main channel for a high-ticket dropshipping business, through paid ads or organic search. Bing is much smaller and not a significant part of the marketing effort. [L1485, 2021-22; older]
- Add up the monthly searches for all relevant product keywords as one combined total, not each keyword separately. The total must reach the country threshold to count as enough traffic. [L1485, 2021-22; older]
- Check that the market has enough online search traffic to support a business over the long term. Marketing mostly runs on Google, Google Ads and Facebook, so enough people must be searching for the product at any time. [L0994, 2021-22; older]
- Example, US keyword volumes (2021): about 450,000 monthly searches for "generator", about 74,000 for "generator for sale" and about 49,500 for "generator for home". For log splitters, "log splitter for sale" had about 12,000 monthly searches. Volumes of related products can be added to the niche total to show enough demand. [L0458, 2021-22; L0771, 2021-22; older]
- Thresholds for the combined total are shown in the market table above. [L1485, 2021-22; older]

### Google Trends: reading the five-year view

#### Setup

Procedure, checking seasonality in Google Trends (2025). [L1116, 2025]

1. Open trends.google.com.
2. Set the region to the country where the business operates.
3. Change the time range from past day to past 5 years.
4. Read the chart for yearly peaks and valleys, and compare the busy season with the off season.

- Check the demand trend over the past five years. [L0671, 2021; older]
- Do not use search trend data from around 2004 to judge a niche. Use the last five years, since search behavior has changed a lot. [L0671, 2021; older]
- Judge seasonality in Google Trends over five years for the target country, using the 0-100 scale. [L1090, 2021; older]
- Procedure, Google Trends check with the one source method: search the exact product name, then the problem it solves, the solution it offers and several related keywords, and compare their interest over time. [L0708, undated]
- Set the time range to the past 12 months and search the exact product keyword first. [L1183, undated]
- Use the 12-month and 5-year windows on the product and related keywords. [L0895, 2021+]
- In the one source tool, change the time period from past day to past five years and set the country to the target market. The graph then shows week over week instead of month over month. [L0805, 2024]
- Google Trends searched with the product term, the US set as country and five years of interest is the way to check whether people buy the product year-round. [L0357, 2019; older]
- Google Trends checks evergreen demand, meaning whether search interest holds over time. It does not show when people open emails or click ads. For ad click timing, use the day and hour breakdown in Google Ads reports. [L0971, 2024]
- Procedure, checking a candidate's market trend. [L1174, undated]
  1. Open Google Trends.
  2. Search the main product keyword or, if it returns little data, the niche or a closely related term.
  3. Confirm the term is trending or holding interest before moving the product forward.

#### Interpreting the readings

| Upward trend | Strong sign | [L0636, undated] |

| Dip to near zero every year for several months | Seasonal niche | [L0671, 2021] |
| Gradual decline | Acceptable | [L0671, 2021] |

- The Google Trends interest graph for a product should sit roughly between 25 and 75 to indicate a healthy trend. Also check whether the market is rising, steady or declining, using Google Trends and news. [L0708, undated]
- Procedure, Google Trends check with a wider keyword. Look for interest over time between 25 and 100. A broad keyword flat at zero for one to two months is a red flag. [L1183, undated]
- The 50 floor and the 25 floor are different judgments. Use 25 as the negative line and 50 as a strong year-round benchmark, not a hard requirement. [L1111, 2020; L1088, 2020; L0895, 2021+; older]
- Seasonality severity must match the benchmark shown in the course. Seasonality alone is not a disqualifier. [L0251, 2023; older]
- Example, with Google Trends interest that peaks around 75 and bottoms around 25, a store scaled to $100,000 in peak-month sales could still fall to roughly $33,000 in low months with no changes and perfect execution. At a 20% net margin, the store would earn about $20,000 in peak months and just over $6,000 in low months. [L0357, 2019; older]
- Read a trend graph carefully. Large recurring peaks mean the niche is cyclical, and a first store should avoid strongly seasonal niches. [L0806, 2024]
- Google Trends seasonality score of 50 is not a hard requirement. Judge seasonal dips by depth and by whether interest falls close to zero in the off-season. [L1088, 2020; older]

#### Trend checks over time

- Check Google Trends across years rather than quarters to judge demand stability. Pick products that sell consistently, not trending items. Products that upper-middle-class buyers always need tend to hold up in a recession, while luxury items should be avoided. [L0804, 2023]
- Demand is judged over a five-year window, not a snapshot. Check that a niche does not collapse. [L0671, 2021; older]
- Compare Google Trends and a dropshipping research tool to pick the term with more volume. Run the research with one chosen term. [L0970, 2023]
- Trending products often return months later after a quiet period, so a product that went viral earlier is worth re-checking before you dismiss it. [L1186, 2022]

### Seasonal versus evergreen judgment

#### Rules for the first store

- For a first store, choose evergreen niches with steady sales week over week, month over month and year over year. Seasonal niches produce sales only in a few months and then collapse regardless of how well the store is run. [L0451, 2020-26]
- Do not start with seasonal niches as your first store. Slight seasonality (demand that does not drop to zero) is acceptable. Severe peaks and valleys complicate measurement of what actually works. [L0268, 2020; older]
- Seasonal products should not be the first store, because a season can pass before you know whether the store works. Test that the product sells year-round with an evergreen check before committing. [L1002, 2019; older]
- Seasonal niches with a deep off-season are poor choices for a first store. Demand swings, operations are hard to standardize, and geo-targeting may be needed. Consider them for a second or third store, after a first store is stable year-round. [L1113, 2022; older]
- If a niche is seasonal, a first store should avoid it. [L0855, 2023; older]
- A niche with a seasonal peak is acceptable only if the valleys between peaks are not too low. Seasonal niches are generally not recommended. [L0834, 2021; older]
- For a first store, avoid niches with strong seasonal swings. A niche that never falls close to zero is still seasonal but less risky. [L0357, 2019; older]
- Outdoor patio furniture shows a strong seasonal dip, so it is better kept for a second or later store rather than the first. [L0967, 2020; older]
- A seasonal store launched during its low season still produces some sales and experience. Finish setup, keep suppliers engaged and prepare for the next peak instead of restarting the niche search. Plan a non-seasonal second store or a product that covers the off months. [L1285, 2024]

#### Acceptable seasonality

- Seasonality is acceptable if demand remains consistent year-round with only minor fluctuations. Use Google Trends (five-year view) to verify, watching for drops that do not approach zero. [L0268, 2020; older]
- Seasonal dips do not automatically disqualify a niche. A niche that dips in Q4 but grows year over year can beat a steady niche with much lower search volume. Weigh all research factors together. [L0934, 2023]
- Seasonal niches are acceptable if Google Trends seasonality severity matches the benchmark in the course. [L0251, 2023; older]
- Mild seasonal peaks, such as a November rise from gift buying, are not a reason to add other niches. Launch with the niche as it is. Extreme seasonality, where sales nearly vanish for most of the year, is a poor fit. [L1116, 2025]
- Holiday sales spikes in inherently non-seasonal niches are normal and acceptable. For example, paddleboards sell more in summer. Seasonal products such as holiday decorations are not acceptable in a general niche. [L0272, 2022; older]
- A niche is not seasonal if some products sell in fall and winter while others sell in spring and summer. Together they can produce steady revenue year-round. [L0854, 2022; older]
- Do not choose niches where all seasonal items dominate unless demand is proven to be high and remains elevated year-round. [L0249, 2022; older]
- A bump that only lifts Q4 for a few weeks is holiday demand. A product that sells all year is not seasonal. A product is seasonal only if demand falls to a clear low for most of the year. [L1090, 2021; older]

#### Identifying the pattern

- To check seasonality, compare the average monthly search volume with the trend chart. If most of the yearly search volume falls in November and December, the product is seasonal. [L0970, 2023]
- Example: a keyword averaging 30,000 searches per month, with about 270,000 of the yearly total in November and December, shows a clear seasonal peak. Such a product is seasonal even if it sells steadily the rest of the year. [L0970, 2023]
- A niche passes the seasonality test only with 4 out of 4 seasons, meaning it sells month over month and year over year, so it can sell year-round. [L1081, 2020-26]
- Check seasonality with a trend graph. Strong peaks and valleys indicate a seasonal product, while a flat line indicates an evergreen product. [L1285, 2024]
- Use the US Census Bureau's quarterly retail e-commerce sales data at census.gov to check seasonality. Look at the unadjusted series, where Q4 shows the largest peak in most years. [L1285, 2024]
- Seasonality matters for Google Trends in a 2019 lesson: seasonal dips should be checked against whether the product sells year-round. [L1002, 2019; older]
- A seasonal product shows a repeated rise in a regular period such as September to November before winter. When the last year shows a stronger spike than prior years, it suggests higher potential for the coming year. [L0895, 2021+]

#### Using the seasonal result

- If the busy season is more than double the off season in Google Trends, consider building a second store in a niche with the inverse pattern sooner rather than later. [L1116, 2025]
- Test products with a gift angle only for a season, since a Christmas-driven product can sell well in one quarter and then fall off. Look for problem-solving products with viral potential for long-term runs. [L1440, 2021+]

### Evergreen categories and product choice

- If starting directly with private labeling without dropshipping proof, choose forever-green niches (selling year-round) rather than trend products that peak and then die within months. This reduces loss risk on inventory investment. [L0324, 2025+]
- Forever-green categories include seasonal fashion (jewelry, apparel renewed each season), home health products (posture correctors, foot correctors, leg supports), home improvement, kitchen utilities and appliances. [L0324, 2025+]
- Prefer evergreen products that sell all year over niches that work only for one to three months. Finding a product that earns 10,000 to 30,000 per month is hard, and replacing products every few months is costly. Look for products that solve everyday problems, since many sell year-round. [L0694, 2024]
- Prefer products that can run all year rather than seasonal or one-event products, unless the seller deliberately chooses a season. [L1177, 2023]
- Evergreen products that sell in all four seasons should be sourced first. Seasonal items are added later. [L0472, 2025]
- Evergreen products that people keep buying over years are preferred, even if demand dips for a while. Luxury items can still be evergreen, and a short-term slowdown in purchasing frequency is not a reason to abandon a niche. [L0967, 2020; older]
- Prefer evergreen niches that sold well in the past and will sell in the future over products that are only trending. Long-term demand is what makes a store a lasting asset. [L1069, 2019; older]
- Pet toys keep selling consistently and are a reasonable candidate for testing. Evergreen categories like this carry lower trend risk than short-lived viral products. [L1172, 2023+]
- Women's fashion is an evergreen category that performs well across seasons. If a product underperforms in one country, test it in another market before dropping it. [L1173, undated]
- Seasonality and fast-growing niches create windows of opportunity. Pet products and winter-season items showed strong momentum in the period described. [L0708, undated]

### Trend timing and short-lived products

- A 90-day revenue trend should be checked before committing to a product. A product that peaked and is now slowing needs a different approach or should be skipped. Adjust the plan on the trend, not only on the last 30 days. [L1187, 2026]
- Product demand can drop after a short seasonal peak. A portable blender that sold well for a period stopped selling. Check the recent sales trend before putting money into ads and prepare a replacement product in advance. [L1184, 2023+]
- Procedure, checking a trend before stocking merchandise. [L1214, 2023+]
  1. Search the keyword and view the trend graph, both worldwide and over the past five years.
  2. Judge whether interest is rising, steady or falling, and where you are on the curve.
  3. Estimate how long the trend will last, for example until a season or league ends.
  4. Launch while interest is rising or near its peak, not after it has passed.
  5. Avoid buying large stock at the end of a trend.
- Buying bulk team merchandise tied to a match or season result can leave unsold stock and chargebacks if the team does poorly. Buyers feel misled when the product no longer fits the outcome they expected. [L1214, 2023+]
- A product that went viral earlier can return, so check it again before dismissing it. [L1186, 2022]
- In 2020, gym and fitness equipment had stock shortages. Demand spiked when gyms closed and inventory had been planned for normal demand. Check inventory risk in niches with sudden demand spikes. [L1277, 2020; older]

### Seasonal stock, ad spend and budgets

- For a seasonal product, keep ad spend low or pause ads in the warm months, from April to October. Resume ads once the cold season returns. [L0492, 2023+]
- Check whether your product sells on a seasonal pattern or year-round before planning budgets and campaigns. Knowing the buying seasons tells you when to increase spend and when to reduce it. [L1268, 2026]
- Judge the store's margin across the year. If a store can reach a 20% net margin, it is better to have that margin all year than to rely on a seasonal niche with large swings. Seasonal niches are not automatically excluded, but the owner must decide whether the swings are acceptable. [L0357, 2019; older]
- A 20% net margin on the peak and low months of a seasonal niche (see the illustration above) shows the cash swing that the owner must accept. [L0357, 2019; older]

### Competitor and marketplace signals

- A category leader with a very large review count shows that demand for the product type is already proven. For example, a pet hair brush with about 138,000 Amazon ratings. Review volume on the top sellers is a quick sign of how big a niche is. [L0450, 2022+]
- Evaluate demand by looking at the homepage of competitor stores, since products featured there are usually the best sellers. The Best Sellers tab on those sites shows which brands and suppliers make the top products. [L1069, 2019; older]
- There is no fixed number of reviews within the last 30 days that proves a product is selling. Use competitor homepages and best-seller sections as the signal instead. [L1069, 2019; older]
- Check inventory risk when a niche spikes suddenly, since stock planned for normal demand can run out. [L1277, 2020; older]

### Order of Research Checks

- Older advice. Run the traffic volume check after the recurring revenue check and before the final niche decision. [L1486, 2021-22]
- Complete the traffic volume check for all five shortlisted market ideas before making the final choice of niche. Traffic volume is the last research step before deciding which idea to build. [L1485, 2021-22]

### Recurring Revenue as the First Check

- Older advice. For each market idea, answer yes or no to recurring revenue potential. Answer yes only if the niche includes more than one product that the same customer could buy. The products must appeal to the same buyer. [L1486, 2021-22]
- Example of a yes: in a 4x4 accessories niche, a first sale could be rear cargo drawers, followed by a nudge bar, then a turbo system upgrade, then suspension or wheels, all sold to the same owner at different times. This makes the niche a yes for recurring revenue. [L1486, 2021-22]
- Example of a no: a niche selling only CNC machines is a no for recurring revenue. The machines are very expensive, last a long time, and are rarely replaced, so a second or third sale of the same product is unlikely. [L1486, 2021-22]
- To check recurring revenue potential for a market idea:
  1. Go back to the competitor sites from the earlier competition research.
  2. Check whether they sell a range of different products, and whether those products would appeal to the same customer.
  3. If they do, the market idea has recurring revenue potential and gets a yes. [L1486, 2021-22]

### Traffic Volume Thresholds

- Older advice. Traffic volume is the monthly number of people searching Google for the types of products you want to sell. It is the second research question and is measured after the recurring revenue question is answered. [L1486, 2021-22]
- The target is a combined total across all product keywords in the niche:

| Market | Combined monthly Google searches (target) |
|---|---|
| United States | 50,000+ |
| Australia and any other country outside the US | 20,000+ |

  [L1486, 2021-22]
- Do not judge each product keyword on its own when checking the traffic target. The 50,000 or 20,000 figure is a combined total across all relevant products in the niche, so add up the volumes of every product keyword that fits. [L1486, 2021-22]
- For the shortlist decision, a rough estimate of monthly search volume is enough, because the goal is to see whether a market is large or small. Keyword tool volumes are approximations, so exact precision is not needed. [L1486, 2021-22]
- Once the summed volume of relevant keywords is clearly above the target, stop counting. Either enter the target figure itself (50,000 for the US, 20,000 for other countries) or enter the exact total, as long as the total is clearly above the target. [L1486, 2021-22]
- Example: a US total of roughly 200,000 monthly searches was entered as one number after clearly passing 50,000. Exact summing matters mainly when the total is close to the threshold. [L1485, 2021-22]

### Ecommerce research practice: rule summaries for product-level judgement

- Competition is judged per product on Google Shopping and in ad libraries. [L0063, undated; L0253, 2024; L0801, 2021]
- Per-product counts are set against a country benchmark and a first-page store test. [L0896, 2021-22; L0458, 2021-22; L1114, 2023]
- Marketplaces are excluded from the direct-competitor total and included in the first-page store test. [L0896, 2021-22; L0965, 2024]
- A brand that many retailers sell is skipped; brands with few retailers are chosen. [L0801, 2021; L1085, 2018]
- The product is kept only if the ad and Google Shopping tests show room. [L0361, 2024; L1114, 2023]
- Saturation alone is never the decision; creative, angle and audience decide. [L0347, 2026; L0871, 2021+]

### Judging Saturation per Product, Not per Niche

- Competition for a dropshipping business should be measured at the product level, not the niche level. What matters is whether the specific products you plan to sell can attract profitable traffic against existing sellers. A niche with many stores can still be workable if its individual products have little competition. [L1483, 2020-26]
- High-ticket products sell into a very large online market, so a store can capture only a small fraction of demand and still build a very profitable business. Do not reject a niche just because many stores exist in it. [L1483, 2020-26]
- A competition test is only useful when it runs across the whole master supplier list, because a decision needs data for every brand. Product-level counts for all suppliers together show whether the niche is viable, and these results are later used to decide whether to proceed with the niche. [L1483, 2020-26]
- Judging competition from the total number of stores in a niche, or from sponsored ads, is misleading for this method. Checking only one product or skipping the store-count click gives an unreliable average. Both lead to a wrong go or no-go decision. [L1483, 2020-26]

### Saturation Signals from Ad Libraries

- Saturation is judged by how many competitors run ads for the product. [L1316, 2024+]
- A product that is saturated but has a weak offer can still be kept if you change the offer slightly before giving up. [L1316, 2024+]
- Products from a shared research list that many people have already picked up can become saturated quickly. Re-check their active ads in Meta Ad Library right before testing, and skip the product if the ad count has grown fast or the ads have stopped. [L1316, 2024+]
- Avoid products where thousands of advertisers already run the same top creative. Pick a product that few advertisers run, but that has proof of demand and a rising trend. [L1479, 2026]
- A product is worth a closer look when it is new (not seen in the last six months) and another seller has run ads for it in the last seven days with good engagement. Ads running this way show that the product sells even with competition. [L1436, undated]
- Products that have not been seen for about six months are considered fresh enough to test. [L1382, undated]

### Store Count on Google Shopping

#### Setting

- In Google Shopping, the free product listings sit below the sponsored carousel at the top of the Shopping results. A single seller shows as `[Store name] seller`, and several sellers show as `[Store name] and more`. Clicking "and more" opens a right-hand sidebar that lists every store selling that product. [L1483, 2020-26]
- Only the first page of Google Shopping results is used for the competition test. Sponsored ads are excluded from the count. Column G is the only column to fill in at this stage; the other columns are completed later in the research process. [L1483, 2020-26]

#### Procedure

1. Open the master supplier list or supplier tracking sheet and copy one supplier name. [L1483, 2020-26]
2. Search for that supplier name on google.com and add the product category, for example "supplier name lighting". [L1483, 2020-26]
3. Click the Shopping tab in the search results. [L1483, 2020-26]
4. Skip the sponsored carousel at the top, since it is not used for this test. [L1483, 2020-26]
5. Scroll down below the sponsored carousel to the free Google product listings. [L1483, 2020-26]
6. For each product, read the seller indicator: a single store appears as `[Store name] seller`, and several stores appear as `[Store name] and more`. [L1483, 2020-26]
7. For `[Store name] and more`, click "and more" to open the sidebar on the right with the full store list, then count the stores. [L1483, 2020-26]
8. Count each store name only once per product, even if it appears more than once. [L1483, 2020-26]
9. Check the products in the first row and continue through the results on the first page of Google Shopping, then calculate the average number of stores per product. [L1483, 2020-26]
10. Enter that average in column G of the supplier tracking sheet, under the notes section. [L1483, 2020-26]
11. Repeat the whole process for every supplier on the list. [L1483, 2020-26]

#### Counting Rules

- After clicking "and more" on a Google Shopping product, the right-hand sidebar lists the first group of sellers and may include a "more stores" option. Scroll the sidebar and click "more stores" until the list ends, then count every distinct store before calculating the product's number. [L1483, 2020-26]
- The "and more" label can appear even when only one store is in the sidebar, so the label alone does not give the store count. Always open the sidebar and count the stores listed there. [L1483, 2020-26]
- If a store name appears more than once in the list for a product, count it only once, because repeated listings from the same retailer represent one competitor. This matters for large retailers that show up in several places on the same results page. [L1483, 2020-26]

#### Worked Examples

- Lighting supplier, example A: the first product had 15 stores listed, the second had 4, and the third had 1, so the average after the first row was about 5 stores. Continuing down the results, the next rows showed 1, 3, 3 and 1 stores, and the average settled at about 3 stores, which was entered in column G. The product-level result was very low competition for that supplier. [L1483, 2020-26]
- Lighting supplier, example B: the first product listed 7 stores, the second listed 7, and the third listed 2, so the running average fell to about 5. Further products showed 4, 3, 3, 2 and 1 stores, and one product had 10 stores. The final average was rounded to 5 stores and entered in column G. [L1483, 2020-26]

#### Reading the Result

- Competition benchmark: if the average across a brand's products is about 10 different stores or fewer, that is a green light to keep the brand in the niche. If almost all brands on the list average 20 or more stores, the niche has too much competition for this method. The source gives no rule for brands whose average falls between these two values. [L1483, 2020-26]

### Competition in Mainstream Retail and Marketplaces

- A product sold in mainstream retail such as Walmart or shopping malls, or easy to find on many online sellers, is usually saturated for dropshipping. Saturation makes it hard to win buyers on price and content alike. [L1444, 2022]

### Crowded Niches and Themed Markets

- Trendy themed niches attract many competing stores selling the same products. A store in such a niche has to look polished and distinct, because a basic site that looks newly created loses against established ones. [L1325, 2020-26]
- Before picking a product, check for products in the same niche that many competitors have sold, and avoid those with saturated offers. Products whose audience is already heavily targeted by large players give less room for a new store. [L1382, undated]
- A market that looks uncharted often already has established sellers who simply use different terminology. Ask suppliers about direct-to-consumer services rather than dropshipping, and look for existing competition as a sign of demand before concluding a niche is empty. [L1441, 2018]
- Trending product lists do not show how saturated a product is on organic TikTok, so saturation is hard to judge from a list alone. [L1384, 2022]

### Differentiation for Crowded Products

- Change the offer slightly before giving up on a saturated product with a weak offer. [L1316, 2024+]
- Build a polished, distinct store in a themed niche, since a basic site that looks newly created loses against established competitors. [L1325, 2020-26]
- On organic TikTok, a crowded product is not a reason to skip it when most competitors post inconsistently. Steady posting and better videos can outperform them. [L1384, 2022]
- Use supplier terminology such as direct-to-consumer services to find sellers who already serve the market under different names. [L1441, 2018]

### When Saturation Is a Blocker

- A product sold in mainstream retail or easy to find on many online sellers is usually saturated for dropshipping. [L1444, 2022]
- A product whose top creative is run by thousands of advertisers should be avoided. [L1479, 2026]
- A product from a shared research list whose ad count has grown fast, or whose ads have stopped, should be skipped at the re-check before testing. [L1316, 2024+]
- A saturated offer aimed at an audience already heavily targeted by large players leaves little room for a new store. [L1382, undated]
- If almost all brands on the supplier list average 20 or more stores on Google Shopping, the niche has too much competition for this method. [L1483, 2020-26]

### When Saturation Is Not a Blocker

- Competition is not a reason to drop a product if an ad for it was launched within the last 7 days and is getting good engagement, because the product is working for someone else. [L1382, undated]
- A crowded product is not a reason to skip it when most competitors post inconsistently on organic TikTok and can be outperformed with steady posting and better videos. [L1384, 2022]
- A store can capture only a small fraction of a large high-ticket market and still be very profitable, so a crowded niche is not an automatic rejection. [L1483, 2020-26]
- A niche with many stores can still be workable if its individual products have little competition. [L1483, 2020-26]
- Existing competition is a sign of demand, so a market with established sellers is not automatically empty or unworkable. [L1441, 2018]
- An average of about 10 stores or fewer per brand is a green light to keep the brand in the niche. [L1483, 2020-26]

### Measures at a Glance

| Measure | Where it is taken | What it tells you | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Number of competitors running ads for the product | Meta Ad Library | Saturation; re-check right before testing a shared-list product | [L1316, 2024+] |
| Distinct stores per product, first page of free listings | Google Shopping | Product-level competition; 10 or fewer is a green light, 20 or more on almost all brands is too crowded | [L1483, 2020-26] |
| Same top creative run by thousands of advertisers | Ad libraries | Avoid the product | [L1479, 2026] |
| Sold in mainstream retail or easy to find on many online sellers | Observation | Usually saturated for dropshipping | [L1444, 2022] |
| Ad launched within 7 days with good engagement, product not seen for about six months | Ad libraries | A positive signal; competition is not a reason to drop | [L1382, undated; L1436, undated] |

#### Further details

- Analyze competition by counting reviews on competing products, not by counting total sellers. Products with 1,000+ reviews are practically untouchable due to accumulated momentum and ranking advantage. Target products with fewer than 300 reviews where you can realistically compete. [L0228, 2015]

### Research order before testing

- Spend at least three days researching why customers buy a product before the first test, and five to seven days if time allows. Research comes before testing, not after. [L1195, 2026]
- Testing product demand first and studying the customer afterwards is the wrong order. Start by studying the ads and messaging that sellers use, then check demand. [L1195, 2026]
- Define the ICP (ideal customer profile: demographics, age group, ethnicity, life stage) and the USP (the unique outcome the product gives that alternatives do not) before any product test. [L1195, 2026]

#### Procedure: Audience research for a product idea

1. Define the target market: which problems the audience has, who they are, what they do, how they behave, and which underlying needs and motivations drive the purchase. [L0999, 2023]
2. Gather qualitative data first from reviews and forums, such as Amazon reviews and Reddit threads on the product problem. [L0999, 2023]
3. Note demographics, psychographics, behaviors and needs that people reveal about themselves in their reviews and posts. [L0999, 2023]
4. Back up the qualitative findings with quantitative figures, such as how often a term or behavior is mentioned or which breeds, models or segments are most often named. [L0999, 2023]
5. Narrow the audience to a few specific segments (commonly three) that each have a concrete problem and a tailored message. [L0999, 2023]
6. Write ads, landing pages and creatives that speak directly to each segment. [L0999, 2023]
7. Test the segments and keep the one that responds best, since it may not be the one first expected. [L0999, 2023]

#### Procedure: Get close to the product before writing

1. Obtain the product and use it the way a customer would, including taking it apart and demonstrating it to others. [L1370, undated]
2. For a service, use it yourself if possible, talk to its users and to users of competing services. [L1370, undated]
3. For a special offer, try it out on real people and check that they understand it and find it intriguing before you build the copy around it. [L1370, undated]

### Qualitative and quantitative evidence

- Quantitative data gives the what, such as counts, percentages, ratings and averages. Qualitative data gives the why, such as reasons, opinions and situations. Use both, but start with qualitative insight and confirm it with numbers. [L0999, 2023]
- Before writing copy about a product, become an expert on it through specific knowledge: its features, how it works, and what is truly unique about it. Expertise on the product lets you find the angle that others miss, and it can take minutes, hours or weeks. [L1366, undated]
- For a product that needs to connect on an emotional or spiritual level, such as jewelry or healing products, research pain points, emotional drivers, and why customers buy. Then use 3 to 4 pages of that research as input to write copy. [L1158, 2023]

#### Procedure: Customer avatar research methods

1. On Amazon, open listings of similar products, read reviews, check reviewers' profiles, age, and tone, and note their problems and situation. [L1158, 2023]
2. On Google, search the pain point and the current solutions the market offers. [L1158, 2023]
3. Research pain-point statistics in blogs, journals, news, and industry reports to use as social-proof data on landing pages and ads. [L1158, 2023]

### Buyer, user and customer avatar

- Build the customer avatar from people who have actually purchased, not from site visitors or leads. A profile built from non-buyers does not match who converts. [L1243, 2017]
- Choose the target buyer separately from the user. A gift product may be bought by grandparents or parents for children, so define the customer avatar and the buyer who will pay for the product before writing the angle. [L1440, 2021+]
- Learn who the customer is and what motivates them before writing, using specific information about their likes, dislikes and expectations. If you are a typical customer for the product yourself, that knowledge already counts, but verify it against the real market. [L1366, undated]

### Segmentation pillars

- The four segmentation pillars are:
  - Demographics: static facts such as age, gender, income, socioeconomic status and geographic location.
  - Psychographics: personality traits such as worry, price consciousness, integrity and desire for status.
  - Behaviors: what a person does in the buying context.
  - Underlying needs and motivations.

  [L0999, 2023]
- Needs and motivations are the most important pillar because they explain why someone buys. Demographics and behaviors are surface-level and observable. [L0999, 2023]
- Define demographics last when building a target audience, after you have worked out the behaviors, needs and motivations of the buyer. Demographics then refine the segment rather than define it. [L0998, undated]
- A jewelry product with a status appeal may sell to buyers aged roughly 20 to 50, so age alone does not define that segment. A product tied to a life stage, such as one related to menstruation, makes age a decisive filter, since the average age of ending menstruation is over 50. [L0998, undated]
- Psychographics cover personality traits, values, beliefs and attitudes. Examples are whether a person is organized, tends to worry, values control or belonging, or holds certain religious or world views. These help decide how a message should sound and what it should appeal to. [L0998, undated]
- Behavior covers what people do, in which context, and what triggers the need for the product. Map the product onto the buyer's daily routine or journey to see where the problem appears. [L0998, undated]
- A vegetable slicer fits the behavior of someone preparing food in the kitchen. The trigger is that they are cutting vegetables badly, getting tough and uneven cuts, and finding the process annoying. [L0998, undated]
- A watch storage case sells better to frequent travelers who must choose a different watch for each outfit on a trip than to all watch owners. A message about matching watches to clothes on a trip is more focused than a general claim that the case holds your watches. [L0998, undated]

### Awareness and positioning

- Crowded niches such as supplements, beauty and pet products require knowing the awareness stages (unaware, problem aware, solution aware, product aware) and mapping the customer's pain point to the product. This is advanced knowledge, so it is not the starting point for a beginner. [L1175, 2026]
- Older source (2021-2022): Around seven touchpoints with a specific e-commerce business happen on average before a customer completes a purchase, so a brand needs repeated exposure to become familiar to buyers. [L1117, 2021-22]
- Each product has a nature in the mind of the buyer, and the appeal must match it. Work out what the buyer needs first, then what they insist on, such as a safety product that works reliably and installs easily, and build the copy around those points. [L1366, undated]
- Look for the specific use in which your product beats every alternative. Measure that advantage in a unit the buyer already values, such as output per unit bought. Target only buyers who gain most from that advantage. [L1369, undated]
- A product sold only by price per ton or per unit is a commodity that competitors can undercut. Reposition it on the result the buyer gets, such as cubic feet of gas per pound of coal or tons of iron melted per ton of coke. [L1369, undated]

#### Case: Repositioning a coal and coke producer (early 1900s, historical)

- Situation: A small coal and coke producer in the early 1900s sold through brokers at per-ton prices. Its mines ran only three or four days a week in good times and one or two days in bad times. [L1369, undated]
- Insight: The owner found that his gas coal produced more gas per pound than any other coal in the district. Gas companies within several hundred miles could therefore afford to pay 25 to 33 percent more for it. [L1369, undated]
- Gas coal letters: The letters argued the cost per cubic foot of gas and offered a carload of coal free of charge. If the buyer's own tests did not show at least 25 percent savings, the carload cost nothing. If they did, the buyer signed a contract for a year at 1.25 dollars per ton f.o.b. Powellton, W. Va. [L1369, undated]
- Competitive response: When a customer drifted to a cheaper bid, the seller bought samples of both coals, had them lab-tested, and sent the cost per cubic foot of gas from each without comment. Most of those customers came back. [L1369, undated]
- Coke: The seller found that low sulphur and phosphorus were valuable. A steel company agreed to pay 25 cents more per ton for coke low in phosphorus, on a requirement of 60 to 90 tons a day. [L1369, undated]
- Stove maker test: The coke melted 15 tons of iron per ton of coke, against 8 or 9 tons for ordinary coke. Melting cost fell to 47 cents per ton of iron against 80 to 90 cents, with coke priced at 7 dollars a ton delivered. [L1369, undated]
- Mailing: The seller then mailed letters built on tons of iron melted to every stove founder within 500 miles. This gave it nearly a monopoly in that stove and range territory until competitors matched its oven loading. [L1369, undated]
- Duration: Mail sales of its output continued for about five years from 1907 or 1908. [L1369, undated]

### Benefits, claims and offer fit

- Choose benefits that a broad share of buyers care about, such as beauty or saving time. Do not lead with prevention or problems that few people act on. Prevention claims usually produce a fraction of the response that a direct, desirable benefit does. [L1261, undated]
- A toothpaste that prevents decay and also beautifies teeth will likely draw far more response from the beauty appeal than from the decay-prevention appeal. A soap that treats a skin condition and also improves complexion should lead with complexion, since the condition appeals to only a tiny share of buyers. [L1261, undated]
- Some claims, though not the main appeal, still influence a meaningful share of prospects and are worth including in ads. Their inclusion should be decided by traced returns, not assumptions. [L1261, undated]
- A mailing list must match the buying behavior the offer requires. A mail-order offer sent to a list of local retail customers rarely works, so choose lists of people who already buy by mail. [L1366, undated]

#### Further details

- Define target audience segments by combining demographic identity with core motivation or problem. Example: Sleep-Deprived Professionals (busy workers experiencing burnout and poor sleep quality), Luxury Self-Care Enthusiasts (buyers treating wellness as a ritual and accepting premium pricing), Beauty-Conscious Sleepers (primarily women concerned with skin and hair protection during sleep). This structure reveals the right messaging angle and value proposition for each group. [L0318, 2025-26]

- Niche down into specific groups such as home grillers, student athletes, dog owners, or watch collectors, because broad groups such as general online shoppers or deal seekers have no shared passion. A niche should be narrow enough that its members recognize themselves in the content. [L0487, 2018]

1. Identify the core need, the gap between where the buyer is and where they want to be.
2. Map the buyer's demographics, such as age, gender and location.
3. Map their behaviours, such as what they do each week.
4. Identify the reason behind the behaviour and the deeper motivation driving it.

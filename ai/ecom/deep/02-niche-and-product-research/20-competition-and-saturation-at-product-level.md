## Competition and Saturation at Product Level

### Judge competition per product, not per niche

- Competition shows up at the product level, so one niche can contain products with very different competitive intensity. Evaluate individual products rather than whole niches. [L0063, undated; L0253, 2024; L0665, 2022]
- Saturation is determined at product level. A niche can be unsaturated overall while its most popular brands and products are too contested. Run the "How Much Competition Is Too Much Competition" test on individual products. [L0253, 2024]
- Competition is judged on Google Shopping at product level, not by the number of stores in the niche. A niche is not too crowded just because many stores sell in it. What matters is how many retailers sell the same brand's products. [L0801, 2021]
- Competition occurs at product level, not niche level. A crowded niche still contains many low-competition products, and those products are the opening for new stores. [L0803, 2022; L0854, 2022; L0963, 2022; L0806, 2024]
- Products that few competitors bid on are often the easiest way into a crowded niche. Traffic should come from many products, not only the top few Google Shopping results. [L0803, 2022; L0934, 2023]
- Look for pockets of opportunity inside a crowded niche, such as products where few competitors run ads. [L0854, 2022]
- Competition is a per-product question, and new products appear every year. Some top sellers operate in niches that look saturated, so a niche should not be dropped only because it looks crowded. [L1110, 2019; L0665, 2022]
- Before dropping a niche that looks saturated, assess competition at product level, because the niche may still have room for individual products. [L0663, 2019; L1275, 2019]
- A crowded niche can work if each specific product has only a few sellers, because paid traffic is bought per product. [L1275, 2019]
- Research of niches, suppliers and products reveals real opportunities even when many new sellers enter the market. [L0806, 2024]
- Judge competition at supplier and brand level, not only overall niche. A niche with major players can still contain many brands and products that a new store can sell. [L1068, 2019]
- Check how many stores sell the brands buyers search for, not how many stores exist in a broad niche. [L0804, 2023]
- Count how many stores advertise each brand and product, rather than counting competitors in the whole niche. [L0963, 2022]
- Build a master list of competitors for each niche and judge how much competition is too much before committing. A niche with saturated competition is a poor choice for a first store. [L1057, 2020-26]
- Saturation of a product market should be checked at product level before committing, using the saturation tests in the training material. [L1085, 2018]
- Search the supplier's name in Google Shopping to judge how saturated a supplier's brand is. The supplier-name search gives the needed information; product-level searches are optional. [L0967, 2020]
- Brand-level limits: if one brand's products appear under more than about 25 online stores on Google Shopping, skip that brand and focus on brands that few retailers sell. Carve out space by choosing brands that are not open to everyone. [L0801, 2021]
- Brands sold in more than 50 stores should not be included in Google Product Listing Ads, because paid clicks would compete with too many retailers. Such brands can still be uploaded to the store for free organic traffic, while ad spend goes to brands with less competition. [L1085, 2018]
- Before committing to a niche, confirm it has supplier options and enough sellers for the brands. A niche with too many paid advertisers for the same brands is harder to win through ads. [L0467, 2019]
- A niche can have many products and many competing brands and still work if the brands are not oversold. Even with a few hundred competing products, a niche is worth testing if enough brands exist and demand is confirmed. [L0801, 2021]
- Sales are usually concentrated in a small share of products, often around 20 percent of them. The number of products alone does not predict revenue; demand and product-level competition decide the outcome. [L1068, 2019]

### Count direct competitors, not every seller

#### Who counts and who does not

- Competitors fall into three categories. [L0896, 2021-22]

| Category | Examples | Counting rule | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Marketplace sellers | Large multi-category platforms such as Amazon, eBay, Walmart | Not counted in the total; they market on a different basis and do not target your customer. Note they exist, then exclude. | [L0896, 2021-22] |
| Own-brand sellers | Companies selling only their own brand | Count as 0.5 each | [L0896, 2021-22] |
| Direct competitors | Market-specific resellers selling other brands, whether or not they dropship | Count as 1 each | [L0896, 2021-22] |

- Only direct competitors count fully in the total. [L0896, 2021-22]
- Marketplace examples excluded from the count: in the US, Amazon, eBay, Walmart and Home Depot; in Australia, catch.com.au, Kogan and former chains such as Dick Smith. [L0896, 2021-22]
- A seller counts as a direct competitor only if it is specific to your niche. Stores that sell mostly other niches are not counted, for example a Mustang-focused parts store that added truck parts, or a bumper store that added a few extra products. [L0896, 2021-22]
- Sellers with multiple unrelated categories, such as snowblower specialists, power equipment or broad auto accessories shops, are generally not counted as direct competitors unless their focus is specific to your niche. [L0896, 2021-22]
- If unsure whether a seller is a direct competitor from its name, click through and visit its website. Check whether it focuses on your niche or sells across unrelated categories. [L0896, 2021-22]
- Identify dropshipper competitors by generic products without a brand logo, non-custom content and no unique product feature. Dropshipper-only competitors still count toward the competitor total. [L0895, 2021+]

#### Search per product type

- Search individual product types rather than adding up all competitors across a broad category, because a broad search such as "4x4 accessories" returns too wide a result to analyse. [L0896, 2021-22]
- Search the specific product type you plan to sell, for example "truck winches" rather than "4x4 accessories". For a narrow market like CNC machines, search the market term itself. [L0896, 2021-22]
- Competition varies by brand and price band within the same product type. Compare sellers across brands and price ranges rather than relying on one product's result. [L0896, 2021-22]
- Worked example: for US truck winches, after filtering out marketplaces and non-specific stores, direct competitors reached around 11 for one product and then rose beyond the 15 limit for another brand's list. [L0896, 2021-22]

#### Country benchmarks

| Market | Benchmark (direct competitors per product type or market) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| United States | 15 or fewer | [L0896, 2021-22] |
| Australia, UK, Europe and other markets outside the US | 10 or fewer in the market you plan to sell in | [L0896, 2021-22] |

- Judge competition per market and country, not in absolute terms. [L0896, 2021-22]
- Some competition is a good sign because it shows an established group of buyers is already served. The goal is to avoid markets that are either too crowded or empty. [L0896, 2021-22]
- The US market is larger, so higher competitor counts are expected there. A smaller country with the same number of competitors as the US signals too much competition for its smaller customer pool. [L0896, 2021-22]

#### Per-product range

- Target 5 to 15 competitors on average for each individual product, counted as the number of sellers Google Shopping shows as "compare prices from N stores". Results showing 10+ are borderline; 20+, 30+ or 50+ signal a market to avoid. [L0458, 2021-22]
- Competitor count per individual product should be between 5 and 15. Fewer than 5 usually means a very small market or little buying interest; more than 15 is too crowded for a new store to enter. [L0771, 2021-22]

#### Averaging across product types

- Record competition by averaging across product types: note each product type's competitor count, average them into one figure, and enter that average in the spreadsheet's Competition column for the market idea. [L0896, 2021-22]
- Averaging example: winches averaging 15, rear drawers averaging 10 and turbo systems averaging 8 give a broader category average of 13 direct competitors. That is below the US benchmark of 15 and therefore acceptable. [L0896, 2021-22]

#### Competitor research procedure (market idea)

1. Search Google for the specific product type you plan to sell, not the broad market category (e.g. "truck winches" rather than "4x4 accessories"). For a narrow market like CNC machines, search the market term itself. [L0896, 2021-22]
2. Open the Google Shopping results (Shopping tab) and scroll to the individual seller list below the horizontal sponsored results. [L0896, 2021-22]
3. Look for the "Compare prices from X stores" line on each product listing; the number tells you how many sellers offer that product. [L0896, 2021-22]
4. Click into listings with a high store count to open the full seller list. [L0896, 2021-22]
5. Sort sellers into marketplaces (exclude), own-brand sellers (count 0.5) and market-specific resellers (count 1), checking unclear sellers by visiting their websites. [L0896, 2021-22]
6. Total the direct competitors for that product type. [L0896, 2021-22]
7. Repeat for each product type within the market idea (e.g. winches, rear drawers, turbo systems, nudge bars, bull bars). [L0896, 2021-22]
8. Average the counts across product types to get the market's competitor figure for the spreadsheet's Competition column, and compare it against the benchmark for your country. [L0896, 2021-22]
9. Repeat for each shortlisted idea (five in the course example). [L0896, 2021-22]

#### Competition as an elimination criterion

- Competition is the biggest research criterion that can eliminate a product. If a product shows poor competition results, do not proceed with it regardless of the other criteria. [L0896, 2021-22]

### Google Shopping: how to count and read the results

#### Basic reading

- The "compare prices from X stores" line on a grouped listing is a practical count of competing sellers for that product. [L0771, 2021-22]
- A search on Google for the product type, with Google Shopping results, shows which sellers pay for product ads in the category. Sellers appearing there signal paid competition, and the results can be checked for specialist versus general sellers. [L0450, 2022+]
- Sellers in Google Shopping are already paying for ads. This matters because Google Shopping is one of the primary marketing channels for launching a dropshipping business. [L0896, 2021-22]
- Google's "compare at prices" listing on a shopping search reflects the same competitor signal as a count of five or more stores selling the product. [L1069, 2019]
- A listing showing "compare prices from 2 stores" or "compare prices from 5+ stores" is normal in the US and other markets. [L0896, 2021-22]
- Google Shopping results vary by user location and search history. If your results look different, interpret them with common sense rather than assuming an error. [L0896, 2021-22]

#### Count distinct brands and listings

- Count distinct brands and listings, not just total products, because one supplier can have many products but few brands. [L1090, 2021]
- Keep big box retailers in the results, since they still count in the price averages. [L1090, 2021]
- If two or three brands dominate Google Shopping results, the niche may be locked up, but a few prominent listings alone do not prove that. Before dropping the niche, check the future competitor stores and count their suppliers. [L1090, 2021]
- A quick way to count sellers for a brand is to search each brand or product on Google Shopping and read how many stores are listed. There is no shortcut that avoids checking brands one by one. [L1114, 2023]

#### Seller quality and filters

- Check seller ratings and positive feedback percentages on Google Shopping's seller list to gauge how established a competitor is. Examples: 65% positive feedback on 646 reviews; 98% on 1,323. Also check the price range filter to compare brands within the same price band. [L0896, 2021-22]
- Price bands for a US search (compare competitor counts only within the band that matches your planned selling price):

| Price band | Source |
|---|---|
| Up to $1,000 | [L0896, 2021-22] |
| $1,000-$7,000 | [L0896, 2021-22] |
| $7,000-$20,000 | [L0896, 2021-22] |
| Over $20,000 | [L0896, 2021-22] |

- The same product type can show very different seller numbers across bands. [L0896, 2021-22]
- Other Google Shopping filters in the left column: Show only (Buy on Google, On sale), Condition (New items, Used items), Features (for example For ATV, For Towing), Shipping (for example Free shipping), Product rating (for example 4 and up) and Seller. Use Condition and Product rating to see whether competitors sell new goods and how well they are rated. [L0896, 2021-22]

#### Procedures

**Competition check in Google Shopping (2021)** [L1090, 2021]

1. Search the main niche keyword, not a supplier or brand name.
2. Review the first page of results.
3. Count the distinct brands and listings, not just the total products, because one supplier can have many products but few brands.
4. Keep big box retailers in the results, since they still count in the price averages.

**Check competition for a product on Google Shopping (2023)** [L1114, 2023]

1. Search the brand or product name on Google Shopping, not with a keyword search on your own niche.
2. Count how many stores list that product on the first results page.
3. Keep the product if more than half of the first page shows products sold by fewer than 10 stores. Treat products with many listings as too competitive for paid traffic.

**Procedure for checking how many stores advertise the same product (2024)** [L1115, 2024]

1. Search for the product on Google Shopping.
2. Ignore the Sponsored carousel at the top, because it shows one store per ad rather than the full competitor count.
3. Scroll to the All products section and gather data from the listings there.
4. If a product shows only one store, it has one seller. If it shows an "and more" link, click it to see the full list of advertising stores.
5. Count Amazon, Walmart, Home Depot and Wayfair listings as competitors, since they advertise the same product.

**Check on Google Shopping for each product** [L0671, 2021]

- Check competition on the first page of Google Shopping results for each product before committing to it.

### Store-count thresholds: where the sources agree and disagree

The sources give different cut-offs. Each is kept below with its context.

| Store-count signal | Meaning according to the source | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Fewer than 5 competitors per product | Usually very small market or little buying interest | [L0771, 2021-22] |
| 5 or fewer on the "compare prices" line | Good | [L0771, 2021-22] |
| 5 to 15 average per product | Target range | [L0458, 2021-22; L0771, 2021-22] |
| 10 or more on the "compare prices" line | Borderline | [L0458, 2021-22; L0771, 2021-22] |
| More than 15 per product | Too crowded for a new store | [L0771, 2021-22] |
| 20 or more | Difficult competition territory in the US and other markets | [L0896, 2021-22] |
| 50 or more (e.g. home lighting) | Too competitive; typically drop | [L0896, 2021-22] |
| 30+ or 50+ | Signal a market to avoid | [L0458, 2021-22] |
| Fewer than 10 listings per supplier product | Keep; more than that is too crowded | [L1111, 2020] |
| Fewer than about 10 stores on the first page | Good sign for competition | [L0469, 2021] |
| 10 or fewer stores in free results | Target (limit stays the same as high-ticket sellers enter) | [L1116, 2025] |
| 10 or more stores | Do not advertise for the product | [L0276, 2024] |
| Around 5 competitors | Manageable; 10 is the aim; 25 or more is too many | [L1275, 2019] |
| Brand in more than about 25 stores | Skip the brand | [L0801, 2021] |
| Brand in more than 50 stores | Exclude from Google PLAs; may still be uploaded for organic traffic | [L1085, 2018] |
| More than 20 stores occupying over half the first page of popular products | Competition too high; broaden or change the niche | [L1285, 2024] |
| Forty dropshipping competitors in a niche | Good sign of market viability | [L0963, 2022] |

#### First-page share tests

- On the first page of Google Shopping organic results, if more than half of the products come from stores with 10 or more sellers, the niche is too crowded. In that case, move up at least one niche level. [L0965, 2024]
- On the compare-price listings, more than half of the results should come from 10 or fewer stores for a good niche. Count only compare-price listings, not sponsored results. [L1279, 2021]
- A product is a red flag if the competition test shows high competition right away and you cannot change the keyword or niche to reduce it. [L1125, 2020-26]
- During the competition test, the suppliers and brands on the first page of results signal how crowded a product search is. If heavy competition from these brands shows right away and you cannot narrow the keyword or niche, treat it as a warning sign. [L1125, 2020-26]
- Two statements from 2023 conflict. One says to keep the product if more than half of the first page shows products sold by fewer than 10 stores. [L1114, 2023] The other says the benchmark is under half of results from ten or fewer stores. [L1283, 2023] The two cannot both be read literally. Confirm against the source before applying either; the majority reading in other units favours "more than half from 10 or fewer stores" [L1279, 2021; L1114, 2023].

#### How to read the thresholds together

- Sources give different values because they count different things. one source's figures count direct competitors and exclude marketplaces, with 15 for the US and 10 elsewhere. [L0896, 2021-22] one source's figures count listings and stores on the Google Shopping results page. [L1111, 2020; L0965, 2024]
- When to use the direct-competitor count: for a market-level go or no-go decision by country. [L0896, 2021-22]
- When to use the first-page store count: for checking whether a specific product's paid traffic is viable. [L1114, 2023; L1116, 2025]

### Big-box retailers and marketplaces

#### Big-box presence

- Big box presence (Walmart, Home Depot, Amazon) selling a category does not eliminate opportunity. Multiple suppliers and variations exist within categories sold by big-box retailers. Example: chandeliers appear at Home Depot and Lowe's, yet hundreds of online chandelier suppliers are profitable. [L0276, 2024]
- Chandeliers are sold in big box chains that carry about five brands, while the market has roughly 500 to 600 brands with many variants. The opportunity is in selling the brands and variants those chains do not stock. [L0968, 2021]
- Treat a brand sold in physical big-box stores as a competition warning, but brands sold only by online retailers are acceptable. A niche can still have room when big-box stores stock only a handful of brands while hundreds exist in that category. [L0665, 2022]
- Compare the number of brands sold by big-box retailers with the number sold by online-only sellers. If big-box stores carry only about five brands with limited stock while online sellers offer hundreds of companies and thousands of products, the niche has room for an online store despite local competition. [L0803, 2022]
- A product type sold by big home-improvement chains can still have hundreds of suppliers with different products suitable for online sale. A few brands that also run showrooms do not rule the niche out. [L1068, 2019]
- A niche that Walmart or Home Depot also sells is not disqualifying if the niche is broad, with thousands of products from hundreds of companies. Big retailers carry only a small slice, so shoppers cannot find the exact product locally. [L1002, 2019]
- Big box retailers on the first results pages are not a problem if the niche contains many brands and products they do not carry. They are a concern only when a few big players dominate the niche. [L0963, 2022]
- Use local availability as a filter: if buyers can easily find the same product, with the size, colour or pattern they want, at nearby home improvement or big-box stores, online dropshipping has less advantage. Products with many sizes, colours and patterns that local stores do not fully stock are stronger candidates. [L0766, 2020-26]

#### Large niches with many brands

- Large niches like furniture can support multiple successful stores because they have thousands of brands, tens of thousands of products, and new ones introduced yearly. High competition does not eliminate opportunity if supplier count is sufficient. [L0249, 2022]
- Furniture is a large viable niche with thousands of brands manufacturing tens of thousands of different products, with new styles introduced yearly. This scale creates room for multiple new stores without market saturation. [L0249, 2022]

#### Marketplaces as competitors

- Large online marketplaces that host many third-party sellers and are not niche-specific count as weak competitors. If they are the main results in Google Shopping for a niche, that is usually a sign of opportunity. [L0359, 2020]
- Products that appear on eBay, Walmart Marketplace or Overstock at lower prices are not a threat in most niches. Walmart Marketplace listings are often sold by third-party sellers rather than Walmart itself. [L0468, 2020]
- Walmart's ads mostly promote third-party marketplace sellers rather than products Walmart stocks itself. Because that marketplace is not drop-shipping, a niche specialist store with a focused buyer does not need to treat Walmart as direct competition. [L0469, 2021]
- Many sellers in a niche, including large marketplaces such as Amazon and eBay, are not a problem. You can stand out through usability, service and offers rather than trying to match their scale. [L0852, 2020]
- Large marketplaces such as Wayfair selling the same products are not a reason to drop a niche. Being the focused expert in one niche is the advantage against general shopping sites. [L1111, 2020]
- Marketplaces such as Amazon, eBay, Etsy and Target count as sellers in the 10-plus store test. The test measures how many sellers carry the product, so the presence of marketplaces alone does not decide whether a niche qualifies. [L0965, 2024]
- Sources differ on whether marketplaces count. Excluded from the direct-competitor total [L0896, 2021-22]; included in the store-count tests as sellers [L0965, 2024; L1115, 2024]. Use the exclusion for the market benchmark and the inclusion for the first-page store test.
- A niche where most sales go through eBay is a warning sign, because the main brands would compete directly with eBay. Look for suppliers whose products are not sold on eBay. If the top brands are only there, consider building a store anyway, since Google Shopping competition may be lower. [L1085, 2018]
- Phone accessories such as screen protectors and cases are heavily sold on Amazon, which makes organic TikTok sales difficult for them. Dropshipping is usually not much affected by Amazon, but in these categories Amazon can dominate quickly. [L0372, 2022]
- Avoid products that are easy to find on Amazon with simple keywords and have sellers with 5,000+ reviews. Buyers who see your ad will search Amazon and find a cheaper or more trusted listing. The product should be hard to find elsewhere, so customers trust your store as the owner. [L0636, undated]

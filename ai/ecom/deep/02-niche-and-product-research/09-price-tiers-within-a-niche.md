### Price Tiers Within a Niche

- When choosing between price points in the same niche, higher-priced products are generally easier to make profitable, unless the product is a luxury good with different buying behavior (2018). [L1085, 2018]
- Keep a niche's products in the price range where the brand and product mix is strongest rather than mixing segments (2020). [L0967, 2020]
- Product tiers within a niche: choose the tier that fits your service capacity, as stated in the two-tier rule. [L0469, 2021]
- Within a niche, carve out a price tier above the cheap versions, for example studio lighting sets at $400 or more instead of $100 sets (2021). [L0801, 2021]
- Price variation inside a niche is normal and should be handled by choosing the tier with the strongest mix (2020). [L0967, 2020]
- Adding products above a range can still work if supplier quality and pricing are right (2018). [L1109, 2018]
- Products sold per square foot or per piece: to estimate the typical bundle size, look at competitor sites for bundle or multi-pack listings and use those sizes. No reliable estimation method was given beyond this. [L0359, 2020]
- Products priced well above the calculated range can still be sold, and higher-priced suppliers raise margin. [L1150, 2020-26]
- Products whose quality tier can absorb shipping are better than the same products at a lower tier. [L1180, 2020-26]

### Volume and Realism

- For a high-ticket niche product that sells around $1,000, a realistic monthly volume for a new store is about 10 to 50 units, with 20 as a plausible middle figure. A plan built on 1,000 units of such a product is unrealistic. [L1147, 2020-26]
- A smaller audience paying high prices can produce the same profit with far fewer orders and far less traffic than a large audience buying cheap products. Large, heavy or expensive products look harder to sell but can be profitable (no year). [L1147, 2020-26]
- Higher-priced products earn more revenue per order, so fewer orders are needed to reach the same profit (no year). [L0941, 2022]
- Plan the operation for fewer, more valuable orders when selling premium products (no year). [L1472, 2022]

### Decision Sequence for an Operator

Work through the tests in this order. Stop at the first failure.

1. **Price test.** Average of new, fixed-price products on the unfiltered first page of Google Shopping is at least $200, and at least half of results are at $200 or more. Average around $700 to $800 is the working target under one source's framework. [L0667, 2025; L0451, 2020-26; L1281, 2022; L0458, 2021-22; L0994, 2021-22; L1329, 2021-22]
2. **Shipping test.** Check size and weight, then the COGS and shipping arithmetic. Standard parcels should sell at about $200 or more; large or heavy items should ideally sell above $1,000 and at $3,000 or more under the older working threshold. [L0015, undated; L1179, 2020-26; L0994, 2021-22]
3. **Margin test.** Net margin target of about 30%, or 25% in some niches. Gross margin at least 3x purchase price with a minimum of $20 per product. [L0016, undated; L0895, 2021+]
4. **Supplier test.** Confirm wholesale-to-MAP spread after approval, or use the non-MAP procedure. Reject a 10 to 15% markup. [L0804, 2023; L0671, 2021]
5. **Service test.** Stay under $2,000 for phone-free operation, or plan service capacity for higher averages. [L0050, undated; L0836, 2024]
6. **Buyer test.** Keep impulse-range prices for cold traffic, and be cautious above about $40 for shoes or about $79 for high-ticket items. [L0661, undated; L1440, 2021+]
7. **Volume test.** Plan around about 20 units per month for a new store at about $1,000 per product. [L1147, 2020-26]
8. **Ranking.** When several niches pass, choose the one with the highest average order value, ideally at least $1,000. [L1115, 2024]

#### Further details

- When screening niches before exact cost and shipping data exist, rough intuition about price points is an acceptable first filter. Those estimates should later be replaced with measured COGS and shipping figures. [L1179, 2020-26]

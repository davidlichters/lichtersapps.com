### Designing the tier ladder

#### Tier structures

- Create tiered offers with a simple layout. Buy 1, Buy 2 and Buy 3 quantity tiers with progressive discounts are used for a single-product store. [L0860, 2026; L1149, 2026]
- Multi-tier discounts such as "buy any 2 pieces, get the 3rd one 50% off" encourage multiple items per cart order while maintaining per-unit profitability. [L0232, 2026]
- Pre-selected starter kits: present the six most popular combinations with the items included, rather than making the customer pick from a long list. Customers who face around 70 stone options and 11 setting options cannot judge which choice is best. [L1225, 2025]
- Replace a custom bundle builder with ready-made bundles and an extra 10% off the pre-created bundle. Fewer decisions and clicks can help lift AOV. [L1225, 2025]
- Keep the single-unit price low for entry, then make the larger quantity or the subscription the default that drives value. Visitors are more likely to accept a bigger option when it is preselected. [L0524, 2023]
- Price a single unit intentionally higher than the cost structure suggests (for example $25) so the bundle offer (for example 2 units for $30) looks like an exceptional value. This raises bundle attachment rate even for low-margin products. [L0298, 2023]

### Badges and labels

- Competitor stores mark the best-value bundle with a Most Popular badge. Use the badge to guide buyers toward the bundle with the highest margin or volume, once you know which one it is. [L0600, 2026]
- Label the 2-pack as "Most Popular" and the 3-pack as "Best Value". Add small icons such as a gift and a truck to make the tiers easier to scan. [L0860, 2026]
- Label one bundle as "most popular" and highlight it visually to steer shoppers toward the higher-value option. A clearly marked default reduces choice effort and lifts AOV more than an unmarked list of equal options. [L0435, undated]
- Highlight the middle bundle tier with a most popular label and add free shipping to it. This steers most buyers toward the bundle with the higher order value and better margin for the store. [L0434, undated]
- Badge sets seen in the sources:
  - Most Popular on the 2-pack, Best Value on the 3-pack. [L0860, 2026]
  - Most Popular on Buy 2, Best Offer on Buy 3, with Buy 2 at Save 50% and Buy 3 at Save 55%. [L1013, 2026]
  - Best Offer on the top bundle at $89.99. [L0560, 2026]
  - Best Seller on the two pack in the sock ladder. [L0347, 2026]
  - Most Popular on the 60-day tier and Best Deal on the 90-day tier. [L1164, 2026]
- A badge sits on the bundle that tests as best (margin or volume), not on the bundle you want to push by default. Competitors' badge placement is a signal, not a rule. [L0600, 2026]
- Show the regular price next to the bundle price to make the saving clear. [L0871, 2021+]

### Naming tiers and bundles

- Creative names create psychological appeal and justify premium pricing. Examples: "Elite Growth Kit," "Stylist Bundle," "The Complete Acne Killer," "Twin Pack." Generic names such as "Bundle 2" leave perceived value on the table. [L0232, 2026]
- Example: a luxury hair laser at $1,999 alone can be repackaged as an "Elite Growth Kit" with a 5-month supply and extended warranty, justifying approximately $300 additional cost through distinctive naming. [L0232, 2026]
- Generic bundle names fail to create perceived value or justify premium pricing compared with names that suggest benefits, status or use cases. [L0232, 2026]
- Name bundle tiers to reflect shared use cases or paired scenarios, such as "Twin Pack," "Room for Two," "Apartment Bundle," "Partner Kit." This appeals to customers buying for multiple users and increases AOV through contextual appeal. [L0232, 2026]
- Give each bundle a name that describes the outcome, such as a "sleep bundle" or "back and neck bundle," and show the regular price next to the bundle price. Combined bundles with a shared problem (for example neck and back pain) sell well. Sleep bundle example: priced at $79.95 against a regular combined price of $126, shown clearly on the landing page. [L0871, 2021+]
- Label each bundle by the length of supply it covers, such as 10-day, 30-day, 60-day, 120-day or one-year supply, not only by quantity. This lets buyers compare the cost per day across tiers. [L0600, 2026]
- Tier labels for a three-tier pack: 30-day, 60-day, 90-day supply. [L1164, 2026]

### Pricing each tier from its own cost

#### Rules

- Before launching a bundled offer, calculate the per-unit costs for all items combined and verify that the bundled price still allows healthy margins. Bundling power collapses if unit economics do not support profitability. [L0233, undated]
- Set bundle pricing from cost of goods and margin: first calculate cost and margin for each option, then ensure the best bundle still gives a healthy margin even when a second unit is sold at 50 percent off. [L0789, 2022]
- Each bundle quantity must be priced on its own supplier cost, not as a simple multiple of the single-unit price. A two-piece bundle is priced from the two-piece cost, which is why $45.95 for two is not double the single price. [L1182, 2026]
- Use a fixed bundle total instead of a percentage discount when setting bundle prices, so the customer sees a clear per-unit price and the seller avoids discount math. Pick a total where the per-unit price drops clearly from the single price to make the saving obvious. [L0861, 2026]
- Check the bundle saving against the sum of the single prices. Saving percentage = (combined value minus bundle price) divided by bundle price. Example: a bundle priced at 500 with a combined value of 680 gives (680 minus 500) divided by 500 = 36 percent. [L1088, 2020]
- A bundle at 29.99 for two items against 59.99 full price gives a $10 saving on the pair, about 16.7% off the full price. [L0436, undated]

Example: a single item at $29.99 and two items for $49.99 instead of $59.99. Before launching a bundle like this, check that the bundle price still covers product cost, acquisition cost and fees. [L0436, undated]

- A bundle with a total price up to about 40 dollars can make sense if a cheap add-on has a 40 to 50 percent take rate (see the add-on check above). [L0708, undated]
- Profit check: a buy-4-get-4-free offer on a home decor product left about $30 margin per order for one student. [L0650, undated]
- If the profit is too thin at the current price, improve the offer rather than only changing ads, for example by giving two items at a discounted total price while keeping a larger margin. The offer change must still leave enough margin after the cost of the extra item. [L0661, undated]

#### Pricing procedure: Buy-One-Get-One-Free bundle

1. Ask the supplier for the cost of two pieces. In the example, one piece costs $8 and the second unit adds $4, so the total for two pieces is $12.
2. In the bundle row of the sheet, check that the market label reads US and not UK.
3. Enter the total product cost for two pieces ($12) and set the quantity to 2.
4. Raise the selling price until the margin reaches 70%; in the example this gives $45.95 for two pieces.
5. Check that the margin is at least 67% before using the price.

#### Pricing procedure: Buy-Two-Get-One-Free bundle

1. Ask the supplier for the cost of three pieces, or add one more unit to the two-piece cost. In the example, two pieces cost $12 and the third unit adds $4, so the total for three pieces is $16.
2. In the sheet, change the quantity to 3 in the bundle row and enter the total product cost of $16.
3. Check that the market label reads US.
4. Raise the selling price until the margin reaches 70%; in the example the final price is $64.95, after first trying a higher price of about $69.95.
5. Check that the margin is at least 67% before using the price.

#### Pricing procedure: full sequence for a single product with bundles

1. Price the product at roughly the same level as competitors.
2. If the margin is at least 70%, price slightly lower to undercut them; if the margin is strong but tighter, price slightly higher to protect profitability.
3. Replace any Buy-one-get-one offer with Buy 1, Buy 2 and Buy 3 bundles that carry progressive discounts.
4. Add a free gift to the Buy 2 and Buy 3 bundles to raise perceived value.

[L1149, 2026]

- Bundle pricing with a margin reference: "Bundle pricing sells two or more units for less than the sum of single prices, so the customer gets an automatic saving... Calculate that the bundle price still leaves a margin that covers all costs." [L0436, undated]
- Price of a low-cost add-on: if a cheap product (about $5 to $10) is competing in paid ads, a bundle can raise AOV to a level that covers ad costs. The bundle must still leave a margin of about $20 to $25 per customer. [L1174, undated]

### Price-raising before a BOGO offer

- BOGO works when margins allow it. If margins are thin, raise the product price by $5 to $10 first and then test BOGO, provided branding is strong enough to justify the higher price. [L1260, undated; L1345, 2021+]
- Procedure: set up a buy-one-get-one-free offer when margins are thin:
  1. Raise the product price by 5 to 10 USD.
  2. Strengthen branding so the higher price is credible.
  3. Offer buy-one-get-one-free on the higher price.

  In niches such as jewelry, good product images can justify a higher perceived value (example: a necklace going from 12.99 USD to 19.99 USD with BOGO). [L1259, undated]
- In niches such as jewelry, strong images can raise perceived value, so a necklace listed at 12.99 could be shown at 19.99 and then offered as buy-one-get-one-free. Results depend on niche and margin, so test. [L1260, undated]
- Raise the price before the bundle is made: a single product priced too low can be lifted by bundles and upsells (see the first-step bundle section above). [L0645, 2023]
- The price should also be set so the single-unit price is raised before the offer and not tested for margin only: "Test which offer type fits the margin of your products." [L0476, undated]
- Test offers for thin margins: see the offer change above. [L0661, undated]

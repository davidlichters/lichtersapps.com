## Scarcity, urgency and price framing

### Stock-based scarcity

- Show limited inventory on product pages and in ads, for example "only 4 units in stock" or "limited supply." Scarcity triggers fear of missing out and raises perceived value and purchase urgency. [L0233, undated]
- Scarcity works by making a product look hard to get and more desirable. Phrases such as one-day sale, limited offer, while supplies last, and first come first served signal limited supply. Limited availability also suggests exclusivity. [L0340, undated]
- Scarcity means limiting the quantity of an offer. A fixed stock, such as only five units left, creates pressure because the value disappears once the stock is gone. [L0683, undated]
- Anything that looks limited or rare is seen as more valuable, so limited-stock or limited-availability wording increases demand. [L0516, 2023]
- Show remaining stock next to each variant as plain text, such as "only four left." The lesson recommends this over installing an app for it. [L0502, 2020+]
- State the exact number left, such as 790 coats, 137 sets or 46 sets, instead of saying stock is limited. Specific figures are more believable than vague scarcity claims. [L1369, undated]
- Scarcity can be communicated in three ways, each stated plainly on the store:
  1. Limited product supply.
  2. Limited number of bonuses, for example free shipping only for the next 20 buyers.
  3. A bonus available only until a set date and never again afterwards. [L0683, undated]
- Use these announcement templates in store copy and email campaigns, adapted to your offer. Send them within a limited-time special-offer email to remind customers of the limit:

```
Scarcity announcement templates to use in store copy and email campaigns, adapted to your offer: "Only a few items left in stock." "Only 20 free necklaces available. After that, no more free necklaces." "A free book, only available this week, and after that you won't get it anymore." Send these within a limited-time special-offer email to remind customers of the limit.
```
[L0683, undated]

### Deadlines and time-limited offers

- Urgency comes from a time limit, not a quantity limit. A defined deadline for buying or acting makes customers decide faster, because missing the window means losing the offer. [L0683, undated]
- Urgency comes from a time component, such as a limited drop, a limited edition, or an offer valid only today or this week. The time component triggers fear of missing out and increases perceived value. [L0502, 2020+]
- Time-limited offers, such as limited-time drops, limited editions, 48-hour sales or deadline-based promotions, create urgency. The time restrictions must be clear and genuine. [L0233, undated]
- Limited-time offers create urgency and push customers to buy sooner, which raises conversion rate. Use a clear end date so the urgency is credible. [L0436, undated]
- Limiting an offer to a specific time frame or a specific action creates a need to decide now and overcomes the tendency to delay. Time-limited offers are used in direct response letters to prompt fast action. [L1261, undated]
- Case: one advertiser offered a premium for six packages and proof of purchase, for a single week. Hundreds of thousands of buyers responded, so a short, specific deadline drove fast action. [L1261, undated]
- Flash sales and limited-time discounts tied to a specific date, such as Black Friday and Cyber Monday, create urgency because the discount exists only on that day. Customers feel they cannot buy it earlier or later. [L0683, undated]
- Example: a Posture Pro Corrector launch at 20% off the regular $49.99, marked as a launch offer with a specific end date ("Offer ends Sunday"). The discount is tied to time-limited event positioning to drive immediate purchase rather than comparison shopping. [L0313, 2026]
- Show a visible expiry on the coupon, such as "ending soon," rather than offering only an automatic discount. A visible deadline makes buyers act at the moment they see the offer. [L0655, undated]
- Use a deadline as the most basic urgency builder, alone or combined with other tactics. Make it visible with a handwritten or rubber-stamped date. Printed and simulated handwriting can replace a true stamp on larger runs. [L1370, undated]
  - Case: an insurance agent mailing 100 letters a week to cold prospects offered a free road atlas for a quote. Adding a rubber-stamped response deadline in red ink raised response from 1 to 3 percent to 5 to 8 percent. [L1370, undated]
- Use time-limited urgency offers such as 5% off, 10% off or free shipping. Show a dynamic countdown that ends when the promotion ends. Time-limited offers give buyers a reason to purchase now. [L1346, 2020-26]
- A small urgency added to an offer improves conversion, for example a limited-time free shipping offer or a message that the price returns to the usual level within 24 hours. [L1382, undated]
- Urgency offer pattern: pair a time-limited perk (free shipping or a discount) with a clear statement that the regular price or discount returns after a set period, such as 24 hours.
  - Why it works: the deadline pushes hesitant buyers to decide now.
  - Examples written for this pattern:
    - "Free express shipping today only, then standard shipping returns tomorrow"
    - "Save 40 percent until midnight, then the usual price applies." [L1436, undated]
- Early-bird pricing schedule for events and courses:
  1. A lower price if enrolled by a set date.
  2. A middle price for enrollment after that date but before a later cutoff.
  3. A higher price at the door.

  The same schedule works for advance orders of new products, event passes, subscriptions and renewals. [L1370, undated]
- Example: a 48-hour window, with 20% off shown with a coupon code in the ad, sent to the product page where the viewer can buy. The 48-hour window is generally the time the offer runs for that person. [L0799, 2021-22]

### Limited groups, slots and waiting lists

- Example: a consultant announces a limited number of open client slots and warns that the waiting list will close for years once filled. This combines scarcity with an urgent timing signal, making action feel immediate. [L0340, undated]

### Countdown timers and coupon expiry

#### Setup

- Show a countdown timer with a message such as "this deal ends in." Activate it by adding a product tag that starts with "sale," followed by the end date of the offer. Place it under the buy buttons, and change its background and colors if needed. [L0976, 2024+]
- Add scarcity with a timer in the announcement bar that shows when the sale ends. A discount with no end point looks less credible. [L1194, undated]

#### Keeping timers real

- Keep the countdown timer and the wording in sync with the real expiry time. One email showed a 17-hour timer while the text said the offer ends at midnight. This conflict is noticed by the shopper. [L0567, 2025-26]
- Countdown timers help conversion only when the deadline is real, for example a sale that ends at midnight Eastern time on Black Friday. A timer that resets every day is a gimmick and does not help. [L1092, 2022]

#### Timers versus coupons: two approaches

The sources disagree on whether a timer should restart. Both values apply in different cases:

| Approach | Rule | When it applies | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coupons that expire | Run coupons that expire once or twice a week, not a timer that restarts every three hours. Expiry dates should look like real promotions, not automatic resets. | Default for ongoing countdown urgency. | [L0355, 2020-26] |
| Daily reset during an extended sale | Reset the site countdown timer and email timers every 24 hours. Segment non-openers and non-buyers so they receive the extension message separately. Alternatively, brands can just say "final days" for a week, with no end time. | When extending a sale. | [L1225, 2025] |
| Daily reset at scale | A large brand kept an 18-hour countdown timer on its website during a week-long Cyber Monday sale and renewed it every day, while personalizing messages by customer segment. | A level to grow into, not a starting point. Smaller teams can copy the daily-reset idea without full segmentation. | [L1225, 2025] |

- Note: the older advice (2022) calls a timer that resets every day a gimmick. The newer lesson allows daily resets when a sale is extended. Use the daily reset only when the sale really is being extended, and do not present it as a permanent deadline. [L1092, 2022; L1225, 2025]

### Scarcity must be true

- Use scarcity and urgency only when they are true. False deadlines or invented shortages destroy trust, which is hard to rebuild. [L0378, 2019+]
- Scarcity claims must be true, because false stock messages damage trust. [L0516, 2023]
- Mistake: a discount left without a reason or a deadline marks a random dropshipping store. Always name the sale and state when it ends. [L1194, undated]
- Mistake: a generic countdown with text such as "ending soon" or "limited supply" adds little scarcity. Tie the urgency to the specific problem the product solves. [L1194, undated]

### Highest number first

- When you show numbers, start with the highest figure that is still realistic and defensible. People compare prices automatically, so the first number becomes their reference point. [L0378, 2019+]
- Example: a consultant who once charged $50,000 for a project that generated $7.5 million in sales anchored the first quote at $25,000 upfront. The high number was credible because it referred to a real result from a client. [L0378, 2019+]

### Comparison prices and anchoring

- Showing a comparison price next to your price creates a frame that makes the discount look bigger and shifts perceived value upward. Framing the price against another price changes how the buyer judges value. [L1207, undated; L1209, undated; L1210, undated]
- Raise the compare-at price to make the discount look larger, as part of a small urgency offer. [L1382, undated]
- Pattern: [compare-at price] shown next to [sale price] with a simple discount label.
  - Why it works: the strike-through frame makes the saving obvious at a glance.
  - Examples: "Was 90, now 60: 30 off" and "Was 120, now 60: half off." [L1207, undated]
- Compare your price itemized against what each part would cost separately, showing each item's price and the total. In one set offer, 418 works worth 472.05 dollars at retail were sold for 39 dollars, a saving of 433.05 dollars. [L1369, undated]
- Formula: name a higher price the buyer already knows and show your price below it by a stated amount, then say why your price is available.
  - Why it works: a concrete comparison to a familiar price is easy to check, so the buyer trusts the saving.
  - Example one: "our 25-pound feed sack costs 6 dollars less than the same sack at the regional farm store, which charges 34 dollars."
  - Example two: "our three-piece tool set costs 45 dollars less than the same set at the hardware chain, which charges 189 dollars." [L1369, undated]

### Price against the alternative

- Compare the price to a more expensive alternative that delivers the same result, not to a cheaper product in the same category. [L1370, undated]
- Example: a $95 recorded program was set against at least $195 for live seminar enrollment, plus travel, overnight lodging and days away from work. Standard audio albums sold for about $10 per tape. The buyer must see the total cost of the alternative, because that is what makes the price feel small. [L1370, undated]
- Show a price against the alternative. A chiropractor visit at $300 to $500 per month makes a $50 product look like a much better value. Show the cost and time saved, not only the product price. [L0871, 2021+]
- Compared with a monthly chiropractor cost of $300 to $500, a one-time $50 product costs roughly 6 to 10 times less. Showing this gap in the offer makes the value of a low-priced product easier to see. [L0871, 2021+]
- Show the price against professional treatments in the same category, with a clear price per session or per visit, so the product looks small next to the alternative. Pair it with the own product price. [L0374, 2026]
- Price comparison a competitor used on its landing page to make its product look cheap next to professional treatments: [L0374, 2026]

| Treatment | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Microneedling sessions | $200-$700 per session |
| Laser resurfacing | $1,000-$3,000+ |
| Chemical peels | $150-$400 per peel |
| Dermatologist visits | $100-$300 per visit |
| Brand's serum (landing page) | $39.99 |

- Price is rarely the real decision factor, but it can stop a sale early. Minimise its impact before the reader sees the number. Decide the price strategy before writing the copy, and where price matters a lot, make the comparison work in your favour. [L1370, undated]
- Sort offers into monthly payment bands rather than showing full prices. A car dealer's sale letter grouped vehicles into bands of $199, $249 and $399 per month, so the reader compares small monthly amounts and not the total. Use this grouping only when the monthly figures are accurate and the total cost is disclosed in the offer terms. [L1370, undated]

### Dollar versus percent framing

The sources disagree on the format of a discount. No unit sets a threshold for choosing between them, so each value is listed with the reason its source gives.

| Format | Example wording | Reason given | When it applies | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed dollar amount | "save 7 dollars with a code" | Worked better in the operator's experience. | Savings shown as a dollar amount, including codes. | [L0519, 2020+] |
| Fixed dollar amount | "save $20" | Customers can quantify money more easily than a percentage. | Savings shown as money. | [L1315, 2023] |
| Round, plain figure | "50 off" | Avoids making the shopper calculate the saving; calculating adds cognitive load. | When the discount should be read at a glance. | [L1207, undated; L1210, undated] |
| Exact percentage | "50% off" | Customers do not want mental math; a clear number removes friction. | When a clear percentage is the headline. | [L1209, undated] |
| Percentage under regular or wholesale price | "25 percent under the original price for the same set," or a quarter, a third or half off wholesale | Frames the reduction against a known price. | Only for reductions you can document. | [L1369, undated] |

- Pattern example for the round form: "Was 90, now 60: 30 off" and "Was 120, now 60: half off." [L1207, undated]
- Both values exist in the sources. Choose the format that the buyer can check fastest, and keep the figure consistent across the store and emails.

### Per-unit, daily and ROI framing

#### Per-unit and bulk value

- Cost per unit = album price divided by number of units. Example: a six-cassette album priced at $49.95 to $69.95 works out to about $8.33 to $11.66 per tape. Use the same division to show a buyer that a single item's per-unit price is lower than a bundle's. [L1370, undated]
- Show the price as a bulk of value: list how many pieces of information, items or benefits the product holds, using large, specific numbers such as 1,001 items, so the buyer sees a big bundle for the money. This also applies to physical items, where each nutrient or benefit can be listed to show the volume of value packed into a small product. [L1370, undated]

#### Cost per day

- Express a total price as a small daily cost by dividing it by the number of days the product will be used. For example, a 120-dollar product used for one year costs about 33 cents a day. Keep the period realistic so the figure stays accurate. [L1369, undated]
- Some competitor stores frame price as a cost per day, for example less than 0.60 USD per day, for daily-use products. Use this framing when the product is taken every day and the daily cost is low.  [L0600, 2026]

#### Quantity saved

- For a concentrated product, state the saving in quantity used rather than in price per container. If the product is three times as strong, the buyer needs only one-third of the usual amount. The cost per use falls even if the sticker price is unchanged. [L1369, undated]

#### Return on investment

- Show return on investment, which means presenting the offer as selling money at a discount. Frame the ROI as dollars made, such as a course that produced net gains for many users, or as dollars saved, such as a newsletter that saves more than its cost in taxes. State the figures so the buyer sees the cost as small. [L1370, undated]
- Formula: ROI in a sales letter = gain divided by cost, expressed as a percentage. Example: a $1,000 income gain for a $199 course reads as roughly a 500 percent return.
  - Exaggerate the claim first, then bring it down to a conservative figure, such as saying the claim is only half right. This signals honesty. [L1370, undated]

### Free offers paired with the same product

- Offers framed as free, such as buy-one-get-one or a free gift, tend to convert well when the paired item is the same product as the one being bought. [L1410, 2023]

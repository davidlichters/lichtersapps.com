## Accountants, Lawyers, Bookkeeping and Records

### Scope of Course Advice

- The course material is general and based on experience, not professional advice. Outside the US, personal circumstances such as other businesses, investments or unusual tax situations can change the answer, so an accountant or qualified adviser must be consulted. [L0702, 2021-22]
- Course guidance is not legal or tax advice. Tax and legal matters must be confirmed by a licensed accountant or attorney. [L0969, 2022]
- Do not treat course advice as legal or tax advice. Get guidance from a qualified professional, such as a local accountant, before setting up multiple business names (DBAs). [L1002, 2019]
- Use course material only as a starting point for understanding tax topics. [L0965, 2024]
- Tax questions go to a professional, not to a mentor. [L1469, 2023+]

### Working with Lawyers

- Get legal and tax advice from an attorney who specializes in e-commerce, taxes, and policies, and ask for referrals instead of relying on a general family-friend lawyer. [L0466, 2018]
- Legal advisory subscriptions with unlimited chat are not needed for a typical startup. A business owner may speak to a lawyer only about once every few years. [L0934, 2023]

**Lawyer communication process**

1. Outline your current situation and questions to the lawyer in bullet points.
2. The lawyer will reply via email listing risks and disclaimers, because they hedge via email to protect themselves.
3. If it is unclear after the email, demand clarification and push back on vague language.
4. The lawyer will typically call to give actual advice, because the phone call creates a record of their liability for accuracy.
5. Keep large email chains as documentation. [L0041, 2025-26]

- If a lawyer hedges their email advice and you remain unclear, push back and demand clarity. Vague risk language is not actionable guidance; get a phone call where they state their actual advice. [L0041, 2025-26]
- If you pursue a lawyer for malpractice, they carry professional liability insurance. The insurance claim is your remedy, which makes the written advice important documentation. [L0041, 2025-26]

### Cash vs Accrual Accounting

- Cash-based accounting records income and expenses when money physically enters and leaves your accounts. Accrual-based accounting records income when earned and the corresponding expenses in the same period. [L0231, 2019]

### Separating Entities and Stores

- Keep store income separate from other businesses in a different entity. Mixed bookkeeping makes it hard to show clean numbers when you later sell a store. [L0854, 2022]
- Store owners who plan to sell their stores should keep the company and bookkeeping together if the stores are related. Separate accounting, such as one QuickBooks account per store, only makes sense for stores that would be sold independently. [L1114, 2023]
- Speaking with an accountant before choosing a business structure is advisable, because the best entity type depends on the country and the owner's situation. An accountant can also take over bookkeeping later. [L0698, 2020-26]

### Record-Keeping and Receipts

- Bookkeeping serves the owner first and the tax authority second, so clean records are needed from day one. Records should be kept in a way that makes profit and expenses clear at any time. [L1148, 2021-22]
- Poor bookkeeping from the first sale can lead to large unexpected tax bills later. Keep proper accounting records from the very first sale rather than fixing them later. [L1405, 2021-22]
- Keep the receipts for all business software, courses and tools, so an accountant can assess whether they are deductible in your country. Such costs are often tax-deductible business expenses in most countries. [L0458, 2021-22]

### Reading the Financial Statements

- Clearing accounts from payment providers such as Shopify, Klarna or PayPal hold money that has been earned but has not yet reached the bank. Show them as current assets and track them, because they should turn into cash within days. [L1046, 2021-22]
- Owner draws and profit distributions are financing activities, not operating expenses. They appear in the financing section of the cash flow statement. [L1046, 2021-22]
- Suppliers and advertising are often paid on credit cards, so card balances rise as orders come in and fall when the cards are paid off. The financing section shows this build-up and repayment, and a large positive financing figure means more borrowing than repayment. [L1046, 2021-22]
- A PPP loan is a COVID-era government stimulus loan in the US and appears as a liability on the balance sheet. Any government stimulus or grant funds received should be tracked as liabilities or equity until their treatment is settled. [L1046, 2021-22]
- Interest expense on loans and card balances appears in operating expenses in the example, and should be reviewed alongside the debt balances to understand what is being paid on borrowed money. [L1046, 2021-22]
- In the example store, merchant account (card) fees of 46,733 were about 2.6 percent of gross sales and about 3 percent of total income. Card fees are part of cost of goods sold, so they reduce gross profit alongside product cost and shipping. [L1046, 2021-22]

### Profit First Account Allocation

- Keep a separate bank account for each allocation bucket, so money set aside for suppliers, taxes, owner pay, staff and operating costs cannot be spent elsewhere. A single mixed account defeats the system. Some buckets, such as profit, can be savings accounts instead of checking accounts. [L1200, 2021-22]
- In this cash allocation system, the tax account holds money for the owner's personal income tax on the business's earnings, so the business sets that money aside before it is spent. Set the tax percentage at every allocation so the bill can be paid in full when it is due. [L1200, 2021-22]

**The seven-account Profit First setup**

| Account | Purpose | Money comes in from | Paid out from it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Income | Receives all sales payouts | Shopify, PayPal, Klarna deposits | Transfers to the other accounts only |
| Cost of goods (COGS) | Supplier costs | COGS percentage of income | Supplier invoices and supplier cards |
| Profit | Reward for owning the business | Profit percentage of real revenue | Distributed quarterly |
| Owner pay | Regular salary for the owner | Owner pay percentage of real revenue | Paid to the owner at every allocation |
| Taxes | Tax reserve | Tax percentage of real revenue | Tax payments when due |
| Staff | Payroll | Staff percentage of real revenue | Payroll |
| Operating expenses | All other business costs | Operating percentage of real revenue | Operating cards and bills | [L1200, 2021-22]

**Procedure: beginner setup**

1. Open three accounts: an income account, a COGS account, and a profit account.
2. Send all payouts from Shopify, PayPal, and other payment sources to the income account.
3. Start by moving 1% of income into the profit account, which should be easy to keep without missing it.
4. Move 80% to 90% of income into the COGS account as a starting estimate, and adjust it based on what you observe.
5. Add the owner pay, taxes, staff, and operating expenses accounts as you get comfortable with the system. [L1200, 2021-22]

- A starting profit allocation of 1% should be easy to keep. If it cannot be kept, the business likely has a serious underlying problem. The 80% to 90% COGS starting range is an experiment, not a fixed rule. [L1200, 2021-22]

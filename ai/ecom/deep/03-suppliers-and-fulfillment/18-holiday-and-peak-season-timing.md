- Suppliers do not have a fixed cutoff for accepting new dropshippers before the holidays. They become busier as the season approaches, so applications are put on hold until after the holidays. [L1114, 2023] *(2023)*
- Suppliers may slow approvals during peak holiday sales weeks. Record every response and follow up at a later date. [L0471, 2024] *(2024)*
- Around holiday peaks, wait a few extra days before following up. [L0471, 2024] *(2024)*
- During peak periods such as Black Friday and Cyber Monday, allow a longer grace period and reach out again once the supplier's business is back to normal. [L0469, 2021] **
- Supplier responses slowed during holidays because staff were away and then had a backlog. Follow up with every supplier that did not respond over the holiday period, and treat the earlier contact as a fresh start. [L0801, 2021] **
- Supplier approvals tend to come faster in quiet periods with no holidays or peak season, so use those times to get as many approvals as possible. [L0961, 2020] **
- When supplier onboarding runs through a slow period such as summer, follow up with each supplier daily because staff may respond more slowly. A store can launch on a fixed date with only one approved supplier, if more suppliers are still being onboarded. [L1069, 2019] **
- In Q4, supplier approvals are slower but still possible. [L1112, 2021] **
- Colder periods do not justify dropping suppliers. Use the quiet season to build the list and then the holiday peak to upload approved brands. [L1115, 2024]

**Procedure: Q4 outreach timing (2024)**

1. Contact suppliers during October and the first half of November, when they have the most capacity to approve new retailers. [L1115, 2024]
2. Expect slower replies as Black Friday promotions begin, which often start as early as the third week of November. [L1115, 2024]
3. Keep a steady pace of approvals until about the first half of November, then focus on uploading approved brands to the store. [L1115, 2024]

**Procedure: holiday closure plan (2024)**

1. Send your final round of outreach about a week before the holiday break. [L0471, 2024]
2. Pause during the holiday week. [L0471, 2024]
3. Restart outreach after the break. [L0471, 2024]

**Procedure: holiday follow-up (2019 routine)**

1. Contact the supplier again through the same channel you used first. [L0467, 2019] **
2. Refer to the holiday season and say you want to pick the conversation back up now that the busy period is over. [L0467, 2019] **
3. Ask for, or send, the application or form they need. [L0467, 2019] **
4. Once a supplier responds positively, act immediately: send the documents, sign the MAP agreement, and upload the products to the site. [L0467, 2019] **

**Offline periods**

- If you will be offline for a stretch, do not start an outreach round you cannot follow up on. Use the offline time to build a complete supplier contact list, so you can start calling the moment you are back online. [L0941, 2022]

### Objections and declines

**"We don't work with new online stores"**

- Suppliers often use "we don't work with new online stores" as a standard rejection to test whether you are serious and trustworthy. Push back by asking which thresholds, such as traffic, Alexa score, or revenue, would qualify you for approval. [L0245, 2020] **
- Procedure when a supplier says it does not work with new online stores:
  1. Respond: "I totally understand. What would it take for us to get approved?" [L0245, 2020] **
  2. The supplier may specify traffic per month, an Alexa score above or below a number, or a minimum revenue. [L0245, 2020] **
  3. Work toward those metrics rather than accepting "no" as final. [L0245, 2020] **
- Alexa Score (alexa.com) ranks website popularity. Some suppliers require a minimum Alexa score to approve new retailers, so research and improve yours before approaching premium suppliers. [L0245, 2020] **
- If a supplier says it does not currently work with online retailers, do not drop the outreach. Use a pitch built around how dropshipping increases the supplier's sales, online reach and revenue. Present the arrangement as a benefit for both sides. [L0935, 2022+]
- Supplier unfamiliarity with dropshipping was a bigger obstacle in the past than it is now, because e-commerce has matured. Only spend extra time explaining the model when a supplier actually does not know it. [L0935, 2022+]

**Suppliers that say they are already working with a retailer, or are not taking new retailers**

- Still start the conversation. Explain how selling with you benefits them, and ask to follow up three to six months after going live, once you have traffic and results. [L0470, 2023] *(2023)*
- Many suppliers approve retailers only in a second round, after they see proof of the store's performance. Plan for a group of suppliers that will ask you to prove yourself first, and keep them on your follow-up list. [L0470, 2023] *(2023)*
- When a supplier declines, end the conversation politely with a clear follow-up plan, such as saying you will get back in touch after launch once you have traffic and sales. Ending on a hard no closes off a future approval, whereas a polite follow-up keeps it open. [L0470, 2023] *(2023)*
- If a supplier turns you down because the store is new, you may briefly mention relevant past business experience if the conversation leaves room for it, without giving detailed information. If the answer is a firm no, end politely and do not push further. [L0470, 2023] *(2023)*
- Suppliers that are not accepting new e-commerce accounts are not a dead end. Note the date they gave for a follow-up, and return with sales results or traffic data at that time, since results raise the chance of approval. [L0801, 2021] **
- When a supplier says it will not take new dealers, ask when to follow up and record that time. [L0665, 2022] **

**Capacity-based rejection**

Example: "we are not taking on more internet retailers right now".

1. Do not accept the rejection passively with a polite goodbye, because the supplier will not reach out to you on its own. [L0927, 2020-26]
2. Present a launch strategy that shows what you are doing to start the store: which suppliers you already work with or are onboarding, which marketing channels you will use, and which promotions you will run. [L0927, 2020-26]
3. Use fear of missing out (FOMO) by showing that you are about to build a meaningful position in the market, so the supplier does not want to be left out of your growth. [L0927, 2020-26]
4. Use this approach only if your launch strategy is real and well researched. A supplier that senses a bluff will not be persuaded. [L0927, 2020-26]

**Standards-based rejection**

A supplier may reject you by saying your store does not meet its standards. Treat this as a signal to improve the store and your pitch before calling again, not as a final answer. [L0927, 2020-26]

**MAP policy decline**

```
Hi [Supplier name], thanks for reaching out. Right now we only work with suppliers that enforce their minimum advertised price, so we will not be moving forward with your products at this time. If your MAP policy is enforced across retailers, please let us know and we can revisit this with you.
```
[L0941, 2022]

**Responding during a follow-up call**

1. If they decline, ask what you can do to make it happen, and show genuine interest in their concerns. [L0133, undated]
2. Highlight how this project is already benefiting your company, and explain the growth potential of adding their product line. [L0133, undated]
3. If they continue declining, ask for the real underlying reason, and state that you genuinely want to understand their hesitation. [L0133, undated]
4. On your next follow-up call, address their concerns with specific solutions or workarounds. [L0133, undated]

- Expect rejections from high-ticket dropshipping suppliers, especially domestic manufacturers or distributors. Treat them as a routine part of outreach, not a judgment of you personally. Suppliers often refuse new online retailers for reasons unrelated to your store's quality, such as capacity or company policy. [L0927, 2020-26]
- Some suppliers refuse at first, and that is part of the normal process. Work through the list and keep following up. [L0469, 2021] **
- Some individuals cannot be reasoned with, regardless of your professionalism or preparation. If a supplier unfairly rejects you despite clear, professional communication (for example, dismissing a demo site as a "scam site" after you explained its launch timeline), continue with other suppliers and do not take it personally. This reflects their attitude, not your business model's viability. [L0294, 2025+]
- When a supplier refuses a new or inexperienced dropshipper, accept it as normal and do not force the issue. [L0358, 2020] **
- Procedure for refusal by a new dropshipper:
  1. Record the refusal and the date in your supplier spreadsheet. [L0358, 2020] **
  2. Once you have traffic and sales from other brands, reapproach the supplier, which may take three to six months. [L0358, 2020] **
  3. Show your results and request approval again. [L0358, 2020] **
- Early refusals from brands to a brand-new store are normal. The minimum of 20 suppliers provides enough chances for approvals. [L0971, 2024]
- Handling a refusal by an online-only reseller: [L1116, 2025] *(2025)*
  1. Ask what would change their mind, such as a traffic level, a sales level, or a business history requirement. [L1116, 2025]
  2. Record the answer as a note on the supplier tracking sheet. [L1116, 2025]
  3. Ask whether you may follow up after a set period, for example three months after launch, to share results and check whether their policy has changed. [L1116, 2025]
  - Treat the refusal as "not yet" rather than "no". [L1116, 2025]
- Newer stores are often rejected or told to return once they have more traffic or more time in business. Keep applying to other suppliers, because approval rates vary and early rejection is normal. [L1094, 2023] *(2023)*
- A supplier may postpone approval until a new store reaches a set level of sales or traffic. This is a normal outcome. Ask for the exact threshold and keep pursuing approvals with other suppliers in the meantime. [L0359, 2020]
- Some suppliers approve only after the store has sales, traffic and business history. Follow those up later rather than dropping them, and ask for the reason for each rejection. [L0967, 2020] **
- Suppliers that decline early often approve later, once the store shows more traffic or revenue. Re-contact them with proof. [L0966, 2025] *(2025)*

### Reasons for rejection and the next step

**Next step by reason (2020 routine)**

1. If the supplier is busy (for example during the holiday season), call back after that period. [L0664, 2020] **
2. If the supplier requires proof of revenue or traffic, follow up once you reach those thresholds. [L0664, 2020] **
3. If the supplier says it is not opening new accounts, follow up every month, ask for another contact, restate your interest, and repeat the original pitch. [L0664, 2020] **
4. Keep detailed notes of every supplier conversation, so the follow-up is well timed and consistent. [L0664, 2020] **

**Minimum requirements**

- A common reason for rejection is a minimum requirement, such as an Alexa score above 100,000, or around $20,000 in expected monthly business. A new store usually cannot meet these, and a large company will rarely make exceptions, however well you pitch. [L0927, 2020-26]
- Some suppliers only authorize stores with at least 10,000 monthly visitors. Others require at least 20,000 euros in monthly revenue. Record these requirements in the supplier notes, so you can follow up when the store reaches them. [L0938, 2020-26]
- Reference point from 2025: about 20,000 monthly visitors and around 200,000 USD monthly revenue as proof when re-contacting. [L0966, 2025]
- If a supplier requires a minimum Alexa ranking under 100,000 or a marketing plan, note the requirement, respond that the threshold will be met, and reapply once the store reaches that traffic level. [L0967, 2020] **
- Some suppliers approve only at a set level of sales or traffic. For example, the approval threshold can be 10,000 monthly visitors or $50,000 in revenue. [L0278, 2025] *(2025)*
- A supplier that imposes minimums can still become a customer later. Mark it as a follow-up target and return once you have proof of the required numbers, rather than dropping it. [L0927, 2020-26]

**Procedure: handling minimum requirements**

1. Ask each supplier that gives a minimum requirement exactly what it needs: analytics access, screenshots, Alexa score, or monthly sales volume. [L0927, 2020-26]
2. Record these requirements in your supplier spreadsheet next to that supplier's name. [L0927, 2020-26]
3. Keep working on the other suppliers on your list while you build toward the stated numbers. [L0927, 2020-26]
4. When you reach the required numbers, contact the same person you spoke to before, remind them of the earlier conversation, send proof such as screenshots, and ask who to speak with about setting up your account. [L0927, 2020-26]

**Procedure: convert a rejection into an approval (2025)**

1. Prepare a list of 20 or more brand suppliers during niche selection, to account for rejections. [L0278, 2025]
2. When a supplier says no or claims it does not work with online stores, understand that this means it lacks trust in the store yet. [L0278, 2025]
3. Ask: "What would it take for us to eventually sell for you? Do you require a certain sales volume or monthly traffic?" [L0278, 2025]
4. Record the supplier's specific approval threshold, for example 10,000 monthly visitors or $50,000 in revenue. [L0278, 2025]
5. When that threshold is reached, follow up with the original contact person, referencing the previous conversation and the new metrics. [L0278, 2025]
6. Use this systematic follow-up to convert rejections into approvals. [L0278, 2025]

**Procedure: handling a first rejection caused by being unprepared**

1. Note the supplier in your supplier list, along with the reason for the rejection. [L0927, 2020-26]
2. Prepare properly, including research and a clear pitch. [L0927, 2020-26]
3. Call again roughly one month later. [L0927, 2020-26]

**Procedure: refusal with no reason given (2024)**

1. Note the reason for the refusal and when to follow up. [L0971, 2024]
2. Keep working the rest of the supplier list. [L0971, 2024]
3. Follow up after three months, and again three months later if the supplier still refuses. The goal is approval from every supplier. [L0971, 2024]

- A rejection in a new niche often just means the supplier wants to see proof that you are a real store. [L1113, 2022] **
- Most supplier rejections come from too little follow-up, not from the supplier's answer. Keep following up until a supplier gives a clear yes or no, or ask it to recontact you once you reach a set traffic or sales level. [L0808, 2026] *(2026)*

- A supplier may name a window to reconsider a new retailer: three months, six months, or a year. Record it and schedule the follow-up for that date instead of leaving the supplier without a next step. [L0927, 2020-26]

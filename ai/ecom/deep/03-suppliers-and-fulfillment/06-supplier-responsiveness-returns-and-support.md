### Supplier responsiveness, returns and support

- Before approval, make sure a supplier answers the phone and emails. A brand that never answers may not support products after approval. [L1111, 2020]
- If a supplier cannot answer technical product questions such as steel type or weight capacity, treat that as a red flag for working with the supplier. If a question is about product selection (such as which barbell suits a given body weight and lift), create a buying guide on your site rather than dismiss the question. [L0429, 2022]
- A supplier that offers no return policy at all is a warning sign, and it is more worrying when every approved supplier has the same policy. Investigate those suppliers before building products on them. [L0853, 2021]
- Do not buy an expensive supplier demo product just to learn their customer support, because such requests are unusual. Only consider it when there is a strong resale market, and check first how quickly the product can be resold. [L0356, 2019]
- Avoid suppliers that expect the retailer to provide local support, repairs or solutions to customers' technical problems. These demands move a heavy support burden onto the store. [L0247, 2021]
- Some suppliers request an opening order before granting catalog access. Respond that you need assurance you can sell their products at the required price, and keep contacting other suppliers in the meantime. [L0247, 2021]
- A store that says products from certain brands may not ship the same day is usually dropshipping those items from third-party warehouses it does not control. [L0459, 2020-26]

### Choosing a supplier by motivation

- Choose a supplier based on your motivation: speed, quality or price, because these three priorities lead to very different supplier choices. [L0780, 2024+]
- Shipping time is a factor to review before choosing a supplier, weighed against product, price and margin. [L0440, 2023+]
- Partner with US-based suppliers achieving 2 to 3 day delivery instead of international dropshippers with 21 to 30 day turnaround. Faster fulfillment reduces perceived time delay and effort/sacrifice, dramatically multiplying the value of the same product. [L0233, undated]

### Where sources disagree

- **Overseas sourcing.** The strict position is no dropshipping from China or from abroad by default [L0359, 2020; L0671, 2021; L0963, 2022; L0469, 2021; L0834, 2021]. The exceptions are a custom, high-demand single overseas maker where delivery and margin work [L0358, 2020], a nearby country such as Europe where shipping is affordable and no high import taxes apply [L0962, 2021], an outside-US supplier whose product is unique and of good quality [L1086, 2019], and a UK supplier only when no US source exists [L0467, 2019]. The 2019 position that long delivery can be a benefit if the margin still works is older and applies only when the margin holds after shipping [L0357, 2019].
- **Origin versus stock location.** A product made in China is not a problem when a brand or importer holds it locally [L0852, 2020; L0941, 2022; L1085, 2018; L0466, 2018]. The problem is shipping from overseas. Use stock location as the test, not the country of manufacture. [L0963, 2022; L0941, 2022]
- **Delivery numbers.** The 2 to 3 day figure for US suppliers comes from one source [L0233, undated]. The 2 to 7 day figure for a high-ticket US manufacturer comes from one source to 2022, older [L0458, 2021-22]. Both describe US stock. Use the number from your own supplier's stated process.
- **Non-MAP suppliers.** Red flags are an approval with no MAP policy to sign and a supplier that has no control over MAP [L1094, 2023; L0429, 2022]. Non-MAP suppliers remain workable as a first step, when competitor prices support the margin, when a small share of brands lacks MAP, or in markets where MAP cannot be enforced [L0671, 2021; L0471, 2024; L1069, 2019; L0472, 2025; L1051, 2020-26]. The distinction is whether the supplier enforces MAP, not whether it has a policy on paper.
- **Distributors.** The default is direct brand approval first [L0962, 2021; L0359, 2020; L0665, 2022; L0855, 2023]. Distributors are accepted when they enforce MAP and leave enough margin [L0962, 2021; L0835, 2023; L0429, 2022], when they are the only route to the brand [L0468, 2020], or as a fallback that is not the goal [L1069, 2019]. The 2019 statement that distributors often have no MAP is older and still applies as a check [L0357, 2019].
- **Direct-to-consumer.** The 2021 position is that a strong supplier store that would match your price is not worth the time [L0247, 2021]. The 2023 position is that omnichannel suppliers are viable unless specific margin or MAP issues appear [L0251, 2023]. Both apply: check the pricing on competitor stores and Amazon, and decline a strong direct store that would match your price [L0247, 2021; L0251, 2023; L0804, 2023].
- **Showrooms.** A supplier showroom selling at MSRP is acceptable when you can still sell at MAP [L0853, 2021]. The same chapter advises choosing suppliers whose competitors have no retail presence, because retail presence often leads to refusals [L0671, 2021]. Treat showroom presence as acceptable only after you have checked whether the supplier approves drop shipping.
- **Distributors for region versus MAP.** A regional distributor requirement is not a barrier if the supplier does not enforce MAP [L1090, 2021]. Distributors that do enforce MAP are the exception that justifies working through them [L0962, 2021; L1094, 2023].

### Supplier Location and Domestic Stock

#### Start Local

- At the start, work only with suppliers based in the country where you sell, who already stock the products there, either directly or through a local distributor or subsidiary of an overseas parent company. For the EU, the products should ideally already be in the EU, possibly in several EU countries. [L1480, 2021-22]
- Do not start by shipping products across borders, for example from China into your sales market. Cross-border shipping can come later, but it is not the starting model. [L1480, 2021-22]
- Work only with local suppliers in the same country as the target customers, not with overseas manufacturers, so the product ships domestically. [L1481, 2021-22]
- Local, independent, established suppliers make operations much easier in many ways. Suppliers are usually experienced and open to working with new sellers. [L1480, 2021-22]
- Local suppliers in a market with a strong e-commerce and retail industry often have established, tested, high-quality products and are looking for online retail partners to sell them. [L1481, 2021-22]

#### Overseas Manufacturing and US Stock

- Products may be manufactured overseas, for example in Italy, as long as the supplier keeps the stock in a US warehouse that the orders ship from; the headquarters location does not matter. [L1113, 2022]
- Products from suppliers are usually from US-based brands that hold stock in the US, not products made in the US. Brands can be from the same country as the store's shipping base, and the store's suppliers do not need to manufacture locally. [L1114, 2023]
- Source from suppliers with stock or a warehouse in the United States. Dropshipping from Europe to the U.S. is generally too expensive to be workable. [L1274, 2018]

#### Checking Stock Readiness

- When a supplier cannot ship a product from U.S. stock right away, check whether it has a set of bestselling products that are always kept in its U.S. warehouse. Only list those in-stock bestsellers as U.S.-ready products. [L1115, 2024]

### Brand, Distributor and Importer

#### Source Direct

- Source directly from the brand rather than from wholesalers, because direct margins are much better. [L1113, 2022]
- Work directly with manufacturers rather than distributors or middlemen. Manufacturers usually carry several sub-categories of products that you can select from. [L1274, 2018]
- Buy from the brand itself rather than from a large retailer that resells its products. The closer you are to the actual brand, the better. [L1275, 2019]
- A brand that sells direct to consumers will often refuse a dropshipper at first, which is normal. Use the same approval steps and expect the relationship to be slightly different. [L1275, 2019]

#### When a Distributor Is Acceptable

- Use a distributor only when the supplier sells exclusively through that distributor. Also confirm the distributor enforces price policies; a middleman that adds a markup and has no pricing controls should be avoided when the supplier can be contacted directly. [L1283, 2023]
- Only work with a distributor if it enforces MAP. Brands that sell outside the distributor can often be approached directly. [L1275, 2019]
- In a niche where most brands only sell through a distributor, the rest of the brands (around 5 to 10 percent) may still be available for direct approval. Check MAP enforcement before signing up with the distributor. [L1275, 2019]
- Suppliers that sell direct to consumers and use distributors in the US may raise prices or limit stock for resellers in shortages. Distributors are therefore not a preferred source. [L1112, 2021]

#### Conditions for Online Sellers

- Some suppliers only approve stores that can show they sell online. Suppliers that also sell to brick-and-mortar retailers are acceptable if you found them through online-only retailers. [L1285, 2024]

### Direct-to-Consumer Conflict

- A supplier that already sells direct to consumers on Google Shopping is only worth approaching if it sells at MSRP and allows you to sell at MAP, or if its own product pages are weak enough that you can beat them. Otherwise, skip it. [L1113, 2022]

### MAP Policy and Enforcement

#### What MAP Is

- MAP stands for minimum advertised price, the lowest price a retailer may advertise for a product. After approval, the supplier gives you a MAP price list, and you may never list a product below its MAP. [L1422, 2020-26]
- Example: if supplier ABC sets the MAP at $1,000, no retailer may advertise its products below $1,000, and that includes you. [L1422, 2020-26]
- Track supplier price lists and MAP policies in writing from the supplier. Verify them before listing products because enforcement varies by supplier. [L1281, 2022]

#### Why MAP Matters

- A supplier often already has about five other retailers selling its products. Those retailers depend on stable prices to keep their margins, so MAP violations hurt all of them. [L1422, 2020-26]
- Undercutting MAP, such as selling a product with a $1,000 MAP for $700, may take all the sales from competing retailers. Those retailers then lose margin, get upset, and may stop carrying the brand, which damages the supplier's market. [L1422, 2020-26]
- MAP protects the profit margins of retailers, so good retailers keep stocking the brand. Suppliers want as many quality retailers as possible and will act against anyone who breaks the pricing policy. [L1422, 2020-26]

#### Checking Coupons and Discounts

- Each supplier's MAP agreement should be checked for whether discounts or coupons are allowed. If coupons are not allowed, the agreement will say so, and MAP software usually checks the displayed price, so a discount shown on the page is what gets flagged. [L1114, 2023]

#### Testing Enforcement in MAP Markets

- MAP rules are country-specific, but most countries outside the United States have no MAP. In those markets, you should apply for approval with every supplier you find, not only with suppliers who enforce MAP. [L1146, 2020-26]
- In MAP markets such as the United States, test whether a supplier enforces MAP by checking whether its retailers all sell at the same price, not below the minimum threshold. This test does not apply in non-MAP markets. [L1146, 2020-26]

**Procedure: Check MAP enforcement before contacting a supplier**

1. Search the brand names from your niche research on Google.
2. Find each brand's best-selling products on the websites of the sellers that appear.
3. Take the product names and SKUs of those best sellers and search them on Google Shopping.
4. Compare the prices that different sellers list.
5. Consistent prices suggest MAP is enforced. Widely varying prices, such as $500, $550 and $800 for the same product, suggest it is probably not enforced, even if the supplier has a MAP policy. [L1274, 2018]

- If a product sells for $1,000 at five stores while one store sells it for $600, treat this as a red flag. Avoid advertising that supplier because its prices are not controlled. [L1274, 2018]

#### Regions with Restricted MAP

- In regions where MAP policies are restricted, such as parts of Europe and possibly Australia according to the call, drop the price match policy. Screen every supplier more carefully to ensure no one is selling their products at a loss. [L1274, 2018]

### Marketplace Restrictions

- A brand that is already sold on large online-only retailers such as Wayfair can still be considered a dropship-friendly supplier. Being listed on a big marketplace does not on its own disqualify a brand. [L1223, 2024+]
- Products sold on AliExpress may be near-copies of a legitimate brand's product, which can lead to a supplier being shut down and to legal trouble. A product that is suddenly removed from AliExpress may have been taken down after a rights holder complaint. [L1440, 2021+]

### Drop-Ship Friendliness Checks

#### Quotes, Pricing and Fulfillment

- Source and validate the supplier before selling: get proper quotes, confirm pricing and confirm that the supplier can fulfill the product. [L1259, undated]
- Get proper quotes, confirm prices and confirm the supplier can fulfill the item before selling. Contact several suppliers as backups to compare prices and quality and to ensure after-sale support and return policy. [L1260, undated]

#### Pre-Upload Quality Check

- Before uploading a supplier's products, search the brand online for reviews and product quality. Check that the listing is worth the effort, since you will rely on organic traffic. [L1277, 2020]

## Scaling Supplier Base and Business Continuity

### Continuous Supplier Outreach After Launch

- Going live is not the finish line. Keep reaching out to new suppliers rather than stopping at the first handful. A store with only five authorized suppliers is a starting point, not the target. [L0191, undated]
- Outreach is an ongoing growth activity. Stopping it once the store is live is a mistake, because a single new supplier can change the business significantly. [L0191, undated]
- Settling for a small set of authorized suppliers because the store already works is a mistake. Continued expansion is what lifts revenue. [L0191, undated]
- Stopping outreach after reaching the first handful of authorized brands and getting traffic is a common mistake. Continued outreach turns a small catalog into a scalable one, so keep the weekly routine running. [L0938, 2020-26]
- Letting product collections stop growing after launch keeps traffic low and limits sales. Treat supplier outreach and product additions as an ongoing task. ** [L1275, 2019]
- Do not wait until every approval in a niche is secured before going live. Contact new suppliers continuously after launch, because outreach, follow-ups and product uploads never end. [L1446, 2020-26]
- The supplier search is never finished. Keep looking for new suppliers and keep applying to the remaining ones even after the first five approvals. [L0582, 2020-26]
- Ongoing supplier work never ends. Add every new product a supplier launches to your store as well, since supplier catalog maintenance is a continuous process. ** [L1109, 2018]
- Continue adding new suppliers in the background during the ad phase, even after launching ads with initial suppliers. [L0278, 2025]
- The long-term goal is authorized retailer status with every supplier. In the early stage, the goal is to be approved by as many suppliers as possible. [L0966, 2025]
- Supplier outreach never ends. Aim to be authorized with every supplier in your niche. [L1407, 2023+]

#### Recurring Outreach Routine

Schedule outreach as a recurring calendar block: once per week, 30 minutes to 2 hours, Monday through Friday. [L0191, undated]

Run the routine in this order: [L0191, undated]

1. Contact suppliers on the master list who have not been reached yet.
2. Follow up with suppliers who did not respond.
3. Follow up with suppliers who said "not right now."
4. Record in the supplier notes: follow-up timelines, traffic or revenue requirements, and any special conditions.
5. When a supplier approves, complete their authorization paperwork.
6. Upload that supplier's products to the store.
7. Return to the master supplier list and continue outreach.

### Expanding the Supplier List

- Expanding the catalog with new suppliers is one of the fastest ways to grow a store. A single strong supplier with many in-demand products can change sales significantly. Before starting from scratch in a new niche, first add more products from suppliers you already have. [L1441, 2018]
- Check whether competitors have uploaded new collections or products from your suppliers. Uploading new products before a competitor does lets you capture those sales first. [L0894, 2020-26]
- Upload first the suppliers whose products are most similar to those already on your store, so you have replacement options when one supplier fails. [L1407, 2023+]

#### Expanding a US Manufacturer into a Smaller European Market

To expand a US manufacturer's product into Europe from a small country: [L0359, 2020]

1. Calculate whether shipping directly from the US to the target market is cost-effective, including product cost, shipping and duties.
2. If it is not, research local fulfillment centers in the target country and get their rates for storing and shipping products.
3. Contact the manufacturer with a volume estimate and the fulfillment rates, and offer to ship from the local center.
4. If talks go well, ask for exclusive distribution in the region.

### Supplier Risk During Disruptions

#### Diversification

- Work with several suppliers rather than only one, because a single supplier can become unavailable through illness, closure or other disruption. Diversifying protects the business from losing its whole catalog. ** [L0467, 2019]
- Depending on only a few suppliers is risky in a shortage. If half of 20 suppliers have stock problems, the remaining ones absorb all demand. Build a broader supplier base before a crisis, including suppliers you have not yet pushed sales for. [L0942, 2022]
- Suppliers that were weak before a supply crisis can become top suppliers afterward. Form supplier relationships early rather than writing off suppliers you have not yet pushed sales for. [L0942, 2022]
- Keep multiple backup suppliers or fulfillment providers. This protects against supplier failure, lets you compare prices and quality, and ensures after-sales support such as return and refund policies. [L1259, undated]
- Keep a backup manufacturer so a single supplier failure does not stop production. This matters most for regulated products such as skincare that need certifications. [L1225, 2025]
- Do not rely on a single traffic source such as Google Shopping. If Google policies change, Merchant Center access is lost, or costs rise, a single-source business can lose revenue overnight. Build a network of profitable traffic sources so the loss of one is absorbable. ** [L0414, 2021-22]
- Relying on one supplier for most of a store's sales is a concentration risk, because that supplier's silence stops the whole order flow. Get authorized with several suppliers in the niche early, even while one supplier is carrying the sales. [L1407, 2023+]

#### Supply Shortages and Market Conditions

- Chinese COVID-related lockdowns in early 2023 slowed shipping, which a seller cannot control. Reducing this risk means working with suppliers in Europe, the US or other regions outside China. [L1214, 2023+]
- In early 2023, US-China trade tensions had not yet affected drop shipping. Experienced suppliers keep sellers informed about freight and shipping-policy changes in China, so ask your supplier for these updates rather than relying on general news. [L1217, 2023+]
- During the April 2020 COVID-19 period, supplier warehouses in closed states operated with severe restrictions (e.g., 5 people maximum per building, working every other day). This caused email response delays but not cessation of communication. ** [L0245, 2020]
- In 2022, fitness niches were the most affected by supply chain shortages. The expectation given was that supply would level out within several quarters. ** [L0963, 2022]

#### Supplier Goes Silent or Stops Shipping

Handle a supplier who has gone silent or is not shipping as follows: [L1407, 2023+]

1. Email the supplier and check whether they still answer and process orders, since a dead phone line alone does not prove they have stopped.
2. If they still respond and ship, keep selling their products.
3. If they do not respond or ship orders weeks late, stop selling their products, even when those products are profitable.
4. Log every order with the date it was sent to the supplier and whether it has shipped, and follow up with the supplier on unshipped orders.
5. For open orders the supplier cannot fill, message the customer and offer 2 or 3 in-stock alternatives (see the customer message template).
6. Stop all ad spend on the affected products immediately.
7. Turn on inventory tracking on the Shopify product pages of those products.
8. Keep the products live instead of deleting them, and add the out-of-stock note above the description with links to 1 to 3 similar in-stock products.
9. Email your customers about the change.
10. Keep onboarding new suppliers, starting with those whose products are most similar to the ones already on your store.

- A supplier whose phone number is out of service can still be reliable if they answer email and keep processing orders. Check this by email before deciding to stop selling their products. [L1407, 2023+]
- Stop selling a supplier's products once they stop responding or ship orders weeks late. Repeated delays damage the business even if each sale is profitable. [L1407, 2023+]

#### Case: Single-Supplier Store Hit by Silence

A store open for about one month had close to 6,000 dollars in sales, all from a single supplier. The supplier then stopped responding for about two weeks, leaving four customer orders unprocessed. The supplier's phone number no longer worked and only email could reach them. Attempts to authorize other suppliers to fill the gap did not produce a replacement quickly. The owner had to choose between absorbing the lost orders and offering customers alternatives. [L1407, 2023+]

#### Closing a Supplier That Goes Out of Business or Stops Responding

When a supplier closes or stops responding: [L0852, 2020]

1. Keep the products that still sell and are still available for a short time.
2. Stop using the products whose supplier is no longer responding, and phase them out slowly as new supplier approvals come in.
3. Keep images that are used on the blog, collections or homepage sliders if the supplier is closing, but do not sell products that are no longer available.

### Tariffs and Cost Changes

#### Tariff Rates and Timing

Proposed US tariffs at the time of the lesson were up to 60% on goods with China as country of origin and 10 to 20% on goods from other countries. These were planned changes, not yet in effect. [L0626, 2025+]

| Country of origin | Proposed rate (at time of lesson) | Status |
|---|---|---|
| China | Up to 60% | Planned, not yet in effect [L0626, 2025+] |
| Other countries | 10 to 20% | Planned, not yet in effect [L0626, 2025+] |
| Made in USA | Not tariffed under the planned rules | [L0626, 2025+] |

#### Managing Exposure

- Record the country of origin of every product you sell, even when the supplier is US-based, because many US-supplied products are made overseas. This determines the tariff exposure of each product. [L0626, 2025+]
- When tariffs are expected, move ad budget toward products whose country of origin is not China. Look for more suppliers with Made in USA products, which the planned rules did not tariff. Suppliers with China-origin products are not cut off, but their ad spend is reduced relative to other products. [L0626, 2025+]
- To reduce exposure to tariffs, search for "[niche] made in USA" to find domestic brands. These are usually more expensive, but their prices are less affected by import tariffs. [L0972, 2025]

#### Price Pass-Through

- Tariffs mainly affect suppliers who import from China, and they often pass added costs on by raising wholesale prices. When wholesale rises, MAP rises too, so the new MAP is set to the new cost structure. [L0966, 2025]
- Tariff-driven supplier surcharges usually pass through to the advertised price, because the minimum advertised price is typically a multiple of wholesale cost. Price increases affect all sellers of the same brand equally. [L0972, 2025]
- Keep operating as usual until a supplier actually announces a price increase. [L0966, 2025]

#### Supplier Price Increases and Margin Pressure

- Supplier price increases can come with a MAP increase and a cut in the retailer's margin, for example from about 20 percent to about 10 percent. Watch for suppliers who both raise cost and sell directly to consumers, since that signals reduced dependence on resellers. ** [L1456, 2021-22]
- Case: a store about one and a half years old had built almost all of its traffic through Google Ads and had only started organic traffic that year. Its largest supplier accounted for roughly 80 percent of sales. The supplier raised sale prices because of higher shipping costs, raised the MAP and increased the store's cost at the same time, so the store's margin dropped from about 20 percent to about 10 percent. The supplier also started selling directly on its own website and on Amazon, reducing its need for resellers. The response discussed was threefold: keep adding suppliers over time; lower Google Ads bids as an interim measure while still aiming for at least break-even sales; and build bundles on the low-margin products where a supporting product is naturally bought alongside the main one, even if the bundle sells to only part of the buyers. ** [L1456, 2021-22]

### Selling or Transferring a Store with Supplier Dependency

#### Reducing Dependency Before a Sale

- Avoid a single-supplier dependency before selling. A store that relies on one exclusive supplier is seen as high-risk by buyers. Set up a second supplier, place a few real orders with them at similar prices, and transfer that backup relationship to the buyer as part of the sale. [L0553, 2018]
- Keep at least two alternate suppliers available before a sale, even if you do not use them. Buyers dislike single-supplier risk, which could come from a supplier raising prices or cutting you off. [L1468, 2014]
- Supplier relationships that take time to build and negotiate act as a moat that protects a store from being copied quickly. A store that can be set up in minutes by picking a product from AliExpress has little moat. [L0554, 2019]
- Spread revenue across several suppliers to reduce risk. A store relying on one supplier is less attractive because a supplier problem can halt its earnings. [L0554, 2019]

#### Conflicts of Interest

- If a store's supplier is also a seller of the same products or is connected to the seller, buyers see conflict-of-interest risk and may pay less or walk away. A wholesale operation and a retail operation supplied by the same owner should be separated into two businesses. [L0553, 2018]
- Owning both the supplier and the store, and giving yourself a sweetheart price, is a risky pattern. The owner can raise prices after the sale, which buyers watch for. [L1468, 2014]

#### Contracts, Transferability and Due Diligence

- Buyers should only buy a store that depends on a single supplier if the supplier agrees in a contract to keep selling to the new owner. [L0553, 2018]
- Supplier relationships are a key part of due diligence. The buyer carries the responsibility for checking that the supplier relationship will continue after the sale. [L0553, 2018]
- Special supplier or affiliate deals must be transferable to the new owner. If a new owner starts with a new account and gets worse rates, the site's value falls. [L1468, 2014]
- Exclusive supplier relationships are a major benefit for valuation when the store has them. A store with 18 suppliers, some with exclusive relationships, was a strong example of diversification. [L0554, 2019]
- A supplier may stop working with a store on the day the store changes owners. Before paying for an existing store, confirm in writing that supplier accounts and agreements transfer to the new owner. ** [L0949, 2021-22]

#### Briefing Suppliers Before a Sale

- Brief suppliers about the sale early, since they know the relationship best. A supplier who refuses to deal with a new owner can block the migration. [L0554, 2019]
- Ask suppliers whether they will keep the same terms for the buyer or require cash payment for the first six months. [L0554, 2019]

### Delegating Non-Relationship Tasks

#### What to Hand Off and When

- An unimportant task does not have to be avoided, but it does not need to be done by the owner. When it is costly or time-consuming, either shorten the time allotted in the daily to-do list or outsource it. Outsourcing only makes sense when it is financially viable at that stage. ** [L1514, 2021-22]
- Treat outsourced fulfillment (ordering products from suppliers and sending orders) as a task to hand off only once the store has a track record. In the beginning, one person can often handle both support and order processing. ** [L0834, 2021]

#### Team Structure by Revenue Stage

- A small high-ticket dropshipping store typically needs at least two regular VAs: one for customer service and phone calls, and one for order processing and supplier management. In a very small business these can be combined into one person. A store in the seven-figure annual revenue range (about $1 million or more per year) usually has two to three full-time VAs plus freelancers for specific tasks. ** [L0721, 2021-22]

| Stage | Typical team | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Very small business | One person for support and order processing | [L0721, 2021-22] |
| Small store | At least two regular VAs: customer service and calls; order processing and supplier management | [L0721, 2021-22] |
| About $1M+ per year | Two to three full-time VAs plus freelancers for specific tasks | [L0721, 2021-22] |

#### Cost and Tools

- Outsourcing a virtual assistant who manages customer service and order fulfillment costs around 500 extra per month. [L0780, 2024+]
- OnlineJobs.ph is a marketplace for hiring remote virtual assistants, often from the Philippines, to handle recurring store tasks at lower cost. [L0817, 2026]
- At $100k-500k per month, a supply chain manager calculates how many units of each SKU to order, based on current and forecast sales volume. Expect to pay about $6-15 per hour on Upwork. [L1129, undated]

#### Delegating Research Without Delegating Brand Choice

For high-ticket dropshipping, the owner picks the brands to sell and delegates contact lookup, application forms and similar manual research to someone else. Define the research need in advance. [L1247, 2021]

#### Further details

- Adding one new supplier can raise store revenue by roughly 30-50% and opens demand that the existing catalog cannot reach. More suppliers means more products, and more products means more chances to sell. [L0191, undated]

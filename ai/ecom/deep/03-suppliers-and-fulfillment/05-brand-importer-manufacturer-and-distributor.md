### Brand, importer, manufacturer and distributor

#### Who to contact

| Supplier type | Default | Use when | Ids |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brand that owns the brand name, or its importer | Preferred | Brand sells direct to end customers at MSRP and allows resale at MAP | [L0665, 2022; L1086, 2019] |
| Manufacturer selling to you | Preferred | Brand or manufacturer approves you directly | [L0855, 2023; L0962, 2021] |
| Holding company that owns several brands | Acceptable | Owns the brands it sells | [L1069, 2019] |
| Distributor, wholesaler or clearinghouse | Fallback | Only when no direct brand approval is possible, and after checks | [L0665, 2022; L0962, 2021] |
| Website selling several brands at wholesale | Check first | Acceptable only if it is a distributor that enforces MAP | [L0964, 2023; L0970, 2023] |

- Always start by seeking direct relationships with brands. Use importers, wholesalers or manufacturers that carry other brands only when you cannot get approved by enough direct suppliers. [L0962, 2021]
- Going directly to the supplier is preferred over a distributor. Only use a distributor after an extra layer of research, not by default. [L0359, 2020]
- Going straight to the brand gives you a direct contact, current product information, pricing control, and authorized retailer status. Direct brand relationships are the basis for profitable dropshipping. [L0466, 2018]
- Contact brands directly, not distributors or wholesalers. If an overseas supplier has US warehouse presence and carries legitimate branded products (verifiable through competing stores), contact them. Never lead with general distributors as primary suppliers. [L0276, 2024]
- Prefer approval directly with the brand (the importer that owns the brand name) over a distributor, because distributors control MAP enforcement, pricing and relationship terms. Use a distributor only when no direct brand approval is possible. [L0665, 2022]
- Distributors do not own the brands they sell and rarely control prices, so margins are lower. Avoid distributors when a direct supplier approval is possible. [L0855, 2023]
- Use dropship suppliers that own their brand and sell under it, not intermediaries, supplier directories, or dropshipping from China. The store sells the supplier's brand to the customer. [L0759, 2020-26]
- Work directly with brands or their authorized local importers, and avoid supplier directories and middlemen. Middlemen add cost and make it harder to get approved. [L0852, 2020]
- Distributors that resell many brands as middlemen usually add a markup and often do not enforce MAP pricing. Buy directly from the brand whenever possible. [L0970, 2023]
- Do not contact brick-and-mortar retailers that also sell online for wholesale, because they have access to brands that only work with physical stores. Focus supplier contacts on brands that work with online-only stores, so approval is possible across them. [L0968, 2021]
- Work with the brand, since the brand name on the product is what matters. A brand may manufacture its own products or use one or several third-party manufacturers, so the setup behind it does not change which supplier you contract with. [L1086, 2019]
- An importer that owns the brand name is the supplier to approach for an imported product, because it is the company that brings in and sells the product. [L0665, 2022]
- A holding company that owns several brands is an acceptable supplier, while a middleman distributor that does not own the brands should be replaced by direct brand relationships. [L1069, 2019]
- A supplier is any company you can be approved to sell for directly. Manufacturers can also be suppliers, but some suppliers do not manufacture their own products. [L0855, 2023]
- Verify the supplier's business model with these checks:
  - A company whose site looks like an agency or marketing firm can still be a wholesale distributor of brands. Check whether it lists brands and wholesale distribution before adding its contact details. [L0940, 2020-26]
  - A website selling several brands is not always a distributor, since it can be the parent company that owns those brands. Identify which model the seller follows before deciding whether to work with it. [L0970, 2023]
  - A third-party website that sells a supplier's products at wholesale is only acceptable if it is a distributor that still enforces MAP. If it is a company that buys from the supplier and resells, do not list those products. [L0964, 2023]
  - When the same company name appears on two supplier lists with different wholesale prices, it may be a distributor rather than the brand itself. Ask the supplier to explain the difference and keep a screenshot as evidence. [L0852, 2020]

#### When a distributor is acceptable

- When a brand routes business only through a distributor, it is acceptable to work with that distributor. Before doing so, ask the distributor whether it enforces MAP policies and whether its markup leaves enough margin for the store. [L0429, 2022]
- A distributor is acceptable only if it is the brand's wholesale arm that manages everything, enforces MAP, and gives you a margin you can compete with. Before deciding, request price lists, check whether MAP is enforced, and check the margin and competition. [L0835, 2023]
- Distributors are acceptable only if they enforce MAP pricing and the product margins are significant after their markup. Compare distributor price lists before committing. [L0962, 2021]
- Example: in medical equipment for home use, many manufacturers all pointed to one distributor that carried their full range. The distributor enforced MAP pricing and the margins were good, so that route was viable. [L0962, 2021]
- A supplier that sells through a distributor or dealer network may not offer a clean drop-ship account. Only work with it if it is the only way to win the brand, which happens for a very small share of suppliers. [L0468, 2020]
- Working through a distributor is a fallback when a brand sells only through distributors. It is worth doing if the products sell, but direct relationships with the brand are the goal. Distributors often have no MAP policy, so exclude their products from the price match policy. [L1069, 2019]
- A seller in a country should prefer brands or importers that hold stock locally. A distributor is acceptable only when no direct brand link exists. [L0852, 2020]
- In one past case about 90 percent of brands in a niche sold through distributors, and the distributor enforced MAP policies. Such cases are rare, so contacting the distributor can still be worth it when a store is already built around that niche. [L1094, 2023]
- Some suppliers claim they must work through distributors by region because of non-compete clauses. First confirm whether they actually enforce MAP; if they do not, the distributor requirement is not a real barrier and the niche can still be pursued. [L1090, 2021]
- Distributors can be harder to get authorized, so mention the name of the person or company that referred you when you contact them. Ask early whether they enforce pricing policies, since not all distributors do. [L0470, 2023]

#### Distributor checks

- Some brands only sell through a distributor or clearinghouse. If that is the case, vet the intermediary on MAP enforcement, pricing policy, policy toward competitors, how hard approval is, and shipping speed before listing its products. [L0359, 2020]
- A distributor that is not a manufacturer usually makes profit harder to earn, and distributors often have no MAP policy. Contact the individual brands the distributor sells, because direct relationships give better pricing. [L0357, 2019]
- For supplier distributors such as TD Synnex or Ingram Micro, check whether they have a MAP policy. If none is enforced, do not use a price match guarantee and confirm the margins still work. [L0963, 2022]
- Do not work with a supplier that has granted sole distributorship for a market to a physical plus online store there. Pass on that supplier instead of competing with the exclusive partner. [L0962, 2021]
- Some suppliers route new retailers to third-party distribution centers, and those centers may refuse new accounts or ask for a large first order. Ask each center directly whether it opens accounts for new retailers before counting that supplier as available. Treat a refusal from a distribution center as a supplier-level blocker to work around, not as a final answer for the whole niche. [L0966, 2025]
- A distributor that bans returns while competitors and the manufacturer offer 30-day returns is a warning sign. Move on to other suppliers and reapply directly after three to six months of established business. [L0429, 2022]
- Brands that manufacture outside the US often work only through approved master distributors. [L1068, 2019]

### MAP policy

#### Definition and purpose

- Minimum Advertised Price (MAP) is a pricing floor set by suppliers that prevents authorized retailers from advertising products below that price, protecting fair margins for all sellers and preventing destructive price competition. [L0110, undated]
- Minimum advertised price (MAP) is the lowest price a retailer may advertise a supplier's product for. When a retailer lists below it, a supplier that enforces MAP contacts that retailer, who must raise the price or lose its authorization to sell. [L0711, 2020-26]
- MAP is a contractual floor: an authorized retailer signs an agreement not to advertise the product below a set price, usually calculated as wholesale cost multiplied by a stated factor. Some suppliers have no MAP at all, and in some countries MAP policies cannot be enforced by law. Check whether a supplier enforces MAP before judging it. [L1051, 2020-26]
- Brands use MAP to protect their brand value and their retail partners: if one retailer sells below the floor, others lose sales and the perceived value of the product drops. MAP therefore keeps prices stable across all sellers. [L1051, 2020-26]
- MSRP is the manufacturer's suggested retail price and MAP is the minimum advertised price. A supplier that sells online at MSRP while letting you sell at MAP gives you a price advantage over the supplier's own website. [L1085, 2018]
- Silver and gold suppliers expect you not to list their products below their Minimum Advertised Price (MAP). Undercutting their pricing breaks the supplier trust relationship. [L0121, undated]
- As an Authorized Retailer you represent the supplier's brand. Poor customer experiences with your business create negative associations that damage the supplier's brand reputation, not just your own. [L0121, undated]

#### Testing MAP enforcement

- Search for a supplier's products on Google Shopping before calling. Widely varying prices for the same product suggest that MAP is not enforced, which is a red flag. [L1068, 2019]
- Procedure to check whether a supplier enforces MAP:
  1. Search Google for the supplier's company name. [L0853, 2021]
  2. Look at the supplier's most popular products in Google Shopping. [L0853, 2021]
  3. Compare the prices sellers list for the same product. [L0853, 2021]
  4. If prices vary widely, the supplier probably does not enforce MAP. [L0853, 2021]
- Procedure for a brand that unauthorized sellers list at MAP:
  1. Keep advertising your own authorized status and the brand's warranty. [L0970, 2023]
  2. Ask the brand to contact the unauthorized sellers and have their listings removed. [L0970, 2023]
  3. If that does not work, the brand can file a DMCA notice with the host company of the listing site. [L0970, 2023]
- Sellers should check a supplier's MAP enforcement before approval. Being approved without a MAP policy to sign is a red flag that the supplier does not enforce MAP. [L1094, 2023]
- A supplier that approves a retailer without sending a MAP policy to sign is a red flag, since it likely has no MAP policy at all. Check whether a supplier enforces MAP before relying on it. [L1094, 2023]
- A supplier that says it has no control over MAP prices is not a true MAP supplier. For such suppliers, keep selling only if the products sell at MAP and traffic is profitable. [L0429, 2022]
- When a supplier with a MAP policy does not enforce it, it is usually not worth staying with them. First contact them directly and make the problem clear before deciding to drop them. [L0665, 2022]
- Competing stores should not be allowed to disrupt MAP through discounts. A non-competing store that advertises your brand keywords and sells other brands at low prices can be addressed by contacting the brand that you sell for, which can ask Google to block the advertiser from bidding on its brand name. [L0665, 2022]
- Ideally every supplier carries a MAP policy. A small number without one, such as two out of twenty, can be accepted. Those brands must be excluded from the price match policy, and their competitor prices should be checked before any ads run. [L1069, 2019]
- Suppliers that enforce MAP and ship on your own account are preferable to non-MAP suppliers. Shipping on your own account can feel intimidating at first, but it becomes routine once you work with several suppliers. [L0835, 2023]

#### Non-MAP suppliers

- A supplier without MAP pricing is not an automatic rejection. MAP suppliers are preferred, but the decision depends on what competitors charge for the same products. [L0671, 2021]
- Supplier MAP policies are enforced, but many MAP suppliers are workable. Non-MAP suppliers can also be profitable, though some countries do not allow MAP, so the decision depends on the market and what products are available. [L0472, 2025]
- Non-MAP suppliers can be a valid first step. Having live products on your store increases trust, which can help you get approved by other suppliers. [L0471, 2024]
- Non-MAP supplier research, in order:
  1. Apply to every brand in your niche that you can get approved for (example: 50 brands means contacting all 50), and collect their price lists. Being an authorized retailer does not oblige you to sell their products. [L1051, 2020-26]
  2. Identify which suppliers are MAP and which are not from the price lists. [L1051, 2020-26]
  3. For each non-MAP supplier, build your own price list with columns for Wholesale, MSRP (manufacturer suggested retail price) and Competition. [L1051, 2020-26]
  4. Search each product name on Google Shopping as a customer would, and record the average competitor price in the Competition column. Use established, legitimate-looking stores for the average. [L1051, 2020-26]
  5. Check each supplier's average competitor price against its wholesale cost using the 2x rule (see next units). [L1051, 2020-26]
  6. Only upload products that pass the 2x rule; skip the rest without regret. [L1051, 2020-26]
  7. Monitor KPIs in the Google Ads account on a fixed schedule (see below). [L1051, 2020-26]

### Direct-to-consumer conflict

#### How common and how to judge it

- Roughly 20% of suppliers also sell directly to consumers on their own website, and most do not. A supplier's direct website only leaves room for profitable drop shipping when the site is weak, outdated, slow, has poor images or poor product descriptions. [L0664, 2020]
- If a supplier's own site is polished and offers too little discount for you to afford paid traffic, skip that supplier. If the site is weak, you can still compete with a bonus gift, expiring coupon codes or by matching the price. [L0664, 2020]
- A supplier's own online store is not a red flag if it sells at MSRP and allows you to sell at MAP. Weak supplier websites (one photo, poor descriptions, missing features) are an opening to take sales with a better product page. [L1085, 2018]
- A manufacturer that sells direct to consumers is not automatically a problem. It is harder to compete with when its site uses advanced pixel tracking and is clearly built for online selling, but it is fine when online sales are only an add-on to a brand website. [L0965, 2024]
- Meta Pixel Helper is a free Chrome extension that lights up with the number of Meta pixels on any site you visit. Several pixels, such as a page view plus an AMP page view, show that a supplier or competitor is investing in direct online sales. [L0965, 2024]
- Check whether a supplier advertises directly to consumers:
  1. Search the brand name on Google. [L0804, 2023]
  2. Check Google Shopping for the brand's listings. [L0804, 2023]
  3. Check the search text ads at the top of Google. [L0804, 2023]
  4. Check whether you see their Facebook remarketing ads. [L0804, 2023]

#### Conditions for selling a brand that sells direct

- Sell for suppliers who sell direct to consumers only if: (1) they sell at MSRP and allow retailers to sell at MAP, or (2) their direct-to-consumer experience is poor and you can provide superior UX, more information, and an easier buying process. [L0249, 2022]
- A supplier that sells direct to the public can still be acceptable if its direct price equals MSRP and it lets retailers sell at MAP. Example: MSRP of 1,500 USD for direct sales and MAP of 1,200 USD for retailers. [L1090, 2021]
- A supplier that sells direct can also be acceptable if its own online store shows clear neglect, such as outdated design, slow loading, broken links, poor product images or weak information. In that case a retailer can provide a better shopping experience. [L1090, 2021]
- Suppliers that sell direct to the public at MSRP while allowing retailers to sell at MAP are workable. Avoid suppliers with strong direct-to-consumer websites that compete with your store, unless their site looks outdated and shows little effort. [L0968, 2021]
- Suppliers selling directly to the public at MSRP that allow retailers to sell at MAP are acceptable. Suppliers selling direct where e-commerce is weak can still be profitable. [L0969, 2022]
- Supplier with a sale on its own website: add products only if it sells at MSRP and allows retailers to sell at MAP, or sells direct but shows little investment in its e-commerce site. [L0969, 2022]
- A supplier who sells direct to consumers is acceptable if they sell at MSRP while retailers sell at MAP, or if their direct site is weak and unmarketed. If they run a strong direct-to-consumer business with ads and remarketing, expect your margin to be eroded. [L0804, 2023]
- Sell a brand that also sells direct to consumers only if you gain an advantage: either the supplier sells at MSRP while you sell at MAP, or the supplier's site looks outdated so you look more trustworthy. Otherwise do not sell that brand. [L0803, 2022]
- Sell a brand that sells direct only if the supplier sells at MSRP while you sell at MAP, or if it sells direct from a weak site. Avoid suppliers with strong direct-to-consumer websites that compete with your store. [L0247, 2021]
- Suppliers that sell both online and through physical retail locations are still viable. Cross-check their pricing on competitor stores and Amazon to ensure they are not undercutting your pricing significantly. Allow them through your approval process unless you identify specific margin or MAP policy issues. [L0251, 2023]
- A supplier that sells direct to consumers at MSRP, with a physical showroom, is acceptable if you can still sell at MAP. Supplier retail pricing should give you room to sell below it. [L0853, 2021]
- Choose suppliers whose competitors have no retail store, showroom or warehouse presence. Check competitor websites for this before approaching suppliers, because suppliers with retail presence often refuse drop shipping. [L0671, 2021]

#### Undercutting risk

- Suppliers can sell to the public directly and undercut retailers, which is a warning sign. If a supplier's recommended retail prices or its own site sell below what you can offer, do not list that brand or negotiate price parity before advertising it. [L0801, 2021]
- Avoid suppliers that sell directly to the public and would undercut your MAP price. Prefer suppliers who sell only to businesses and let you sell at MAP. [L0469, 2021]
- If a supplier sells directly to the public at a lower price than your planned selling price, do not upload that supplier's products when you know this in advance. If you find out later, keep the products on the store but do not include them in Google Ads. [L0551, 2020]

### Marketplace and channel restrictions

- If a supplier forbids selling on Google, Amazon, eBay, Facebook or Bing, ask for clarification, because most suppliers only restrict marketplaces. Ask how the store is expected to advertise before deciding to work with them. [L0967, 2020]
- Suppliers that sell on Wayfair are not a problem, because large marketplaces often deliver poorly and treat their suppliers badly. Suppliers may then prefer to send business directly to the seller. [L1085, 2018]
- If a supplier requires an inquire-only button and no cart, do not list its products, because Google Shopping ads need products that can be bought directly. [L0671, 2021]
- Some suppliers require that orders are shipped using the retailer's own shipping account, and some do not accept customer returns at all. [L0837, 2025]

### Product restrictions that affect supplier choice

- Battery-powered items such as Electric Bicycles, Electric Scooters, Go-Karts, Golf Carts and RC Cars, RC Planes, RC Boats and RC Helicopters can face shipping restrictions for their batteries. Confirm the carrier's and supplier's lithium battery rules before committing to these items. [L0546, undated]
- Products with a built-in battery often cannot be shipped to the US. Confirm with the supplier or carrier that a battery product can reach the target country before sourcing it or running ads. Discovering a shipping restriction after ads are live wastes test budget. [L0552, 2023]
- On AliExpress, a product needs at least one listing with a 4.6+ rating and at least 50 reviews, ideally with all listings at 4.6+. If no seller reaches 4.6+, the product is probably low quality. [L0636, undated]
- Skip AliExpress products with more than 10,000 orders, because high order counts mean the product is already heavily scaled and riskier to sell. [L0636, undated]
- Check consumer reviews for damage before choosing a brand:
  1. Search for the brand's products on Google as a customer would. [L0663, 2019]
  2. Read the consumer reviews. [L0663, 2019]
  3. Look for a repeated pattern of items arriving damaged. [L0663, 2019]
  4. Avoid or adjust products where damage is a trend, especially if you must ship on your own account. [L0663, 2019]

### Supplier vetting procedures

- Procedure to confirm a brand is legitimate:
  1. Research brand by searching on Google. [L0255, 2025]
  2. Search brand name alone. [L0255, 2025]
  3. Search brand name with 'reviews'. [L0255, 2025]
  4. Evaluate search results for supplier reliability and reputation issues. [L0255, 2025]
  5. If problems appear, avoid listing their products. [L0255, 2025]
- Procedure to confirm a brand's own website and independence:
  1. Search Google for each brand name in Column A. [L0056, undated]
  2. Find and visit the brand's official website. [L0056, undated]
  3. If it is a private label or marketplace-exclusive product, delete the row. [L0056, undated]
  4. Confirm the supplier operates independently. [L0056, undated]
- Procedure to confirm a supplier serves only online stores:
  1. Search Google for the niche. [L1069, 2019]
  2. Use only the websites of brands that sell exclusively online, and skip brick-and-mortar retailers. [L1069, 2019]
  3. Confirm the company has no showroom and no warehouse; an office alone is acceptable. [L1069, 2019]
  4. Source supplier names from the websites of your true future competitors, since suppliers found there are more likely to work with online-only stores. [L1069, 2019]
- Supplier traffic rank does not matter for choosing a supplier, since suppliers usually do not try to attract traffic. A competitor's traffic rank is a useful signal instead. [L0551, 2020]
- Search for a supplier's products on Google Shopping before calling, and treat widely varying prices as a red flag. [L1068, 2019]
- Before judging a supplier, check whether it enforces MAP. Some suppliers have no MAP at all. [L1051, 2020-26]

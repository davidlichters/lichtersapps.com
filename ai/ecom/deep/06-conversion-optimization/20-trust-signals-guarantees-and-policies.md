## Trust signals, guarantees and policies

### Trust as the core of conversion

- Trust is the strongest driver of a sale, and most abandonment comes from perceived distrust. Typical causes are unclear shipping times, missing security signals, unclear return rules and a lack of customer reviews. [L1208, 2025-26]
- Modern customers recognise untrustworthy websites, compare prices across competitors and hesitate to share their email address. Trust and retargeting are both needed to turn browsers into buyers. [L0257, 2020-26]
- When a shopper has never heard of the store, trust is the main thing being sold. Every visible signal before checkout either adds or removes trust, and trust earned on the first purchase also decides whether the buyer returns. [L0355, 2020-26]
- Trust comes from professionalism, not quick tricks. It is built through strong design, clear copy and excellent customer service. An online business must look as professional as an offline one to be trusted. [L0423, 2019]
- Design and professionalism are underrated. A store with a clearly better look can win traffic from competitors with poor design. Professionalism covers details such as grammar and a visible phone number, while design covers visual appearance. [L0910, 2015]
- Visitors lose trust when the store looks unhygienic, with errors, bugs, useless plugins or unwanted pop-ups. Trust also fails when branding is weak or design is inconsistent, when no customers or social proof are visible, or when no staff or communication channels are shown. [L0423, 2019]
- A store that does not look trustworthy can lose buyers at checkout even when add-to-carts are fine. When people add to cart but do not buy, review the store's appearance and trust signals. [L0361, 2024]
- In dropshipping, many visitors have not heard of the brand. Trust must therefore be built at once, using social proof (others already buying, reviews) and authority (press mentions, expert or doctor endorsements, notable customers). [L0918, 2023; L1363, 2023]
- Risk matters most when the buyer does not know the seller. A new or unknown shop needs risk-reducing elements earlier and more prominently than an established brand. Place trust signals near the headline and the buy button, not only at the bottom of the page, because shoppers who doubt the seller will not reach the detailed sections. [L1209, undated]
- Trust signals fall into three types:
  - Authority: experts, vets, celebrities, respected magazines.
  - Social proof: large customer counts, ratings, reviews, testimonials showing that many others already buy.
  - Risk-lowering statements.

  Authority and social proof should appear at the top of the page, before product detail. [L0918, 2023]
- Product-page trust elements that lift conversion include active social media accounts, customer reviews, social proof badges, bonus offers on high-traffic products, competitor comparison ("do better than what they do"), financing options, live chat and Lucky Orange session recording. [L0249, 2022]
- Trust signals such as authorised retailer badges do not produce an immediate jump in sales. They raise trust and conversions gradually, so expect a steady gain rather than a dramatic one. [L1050, 2020-26]
- Repeat trust elements in the cart. Shipping, a return policy, a warranty and an active promotion help the shopper decide at checkout, so repeating them there is a cheap test. [L0389, 2025-26]
- Trust elements to place on the page:
  - International delivery information.
  - Secure payment information and secure checkout statements.
  - Payment and security badges.
  - Customer service availability.
  - Easy return terms.

  Each one reduces a specific perceived risk. [L1209, undated]
- Shoppers reduce perceived risk when a page openly states international delivery, secure payment, responsive customer service and easy returns. If these points are missing, buyers fear unanswered problems, damaged goods or being unable to return an item, which lowers purchase likelihood. [L1210, undated]
- Use a trust banner with the usual dropshipping trust signals: international delivery, secure payment, fast customer service and easy returns. [L1363, 2023]
- Trust elements and the risk each one lowers for the shopper:

| Trust element | Risk it lowers for the shopper |
|---|---|
| International delivery | Doubt that the store ships to their country |
| Secure payment | Fear that payment details are not safe |
| Fast customer service | Fear of getting no reply when a problem occurs |
| Easy returns | Fear of keeping an unwanted or damaged product |
| 30 day money back guarantee | Loss of money if the product disappoints (loss aversion) |

  [L1207, undated]
- Before a shopper buys, they often check whether the store is a real, active business. A store that cannot be found looks suspicious, so keep the store's basic footprint visible and consistent. [L0653, 2020-26]

### Reviews and social proof

#### Why social proof works

- Social proof works as a mental shortcut. Shoppers feel safer when they see many other people who are happy with the product, so show happy customers high on the page. [L1207, undated; L1209, undated]
- Customer testimonials and review counts act as social proof. A high star rating shown together with a large number of happy customers builds trust, even if the testimonials are not verified. [L1210, undated]
- Social proof such as reviews, real customer photos and user-generated content should be shown. Even in dropshipping, use a professional product photo shoot and detailed descriptions so visitors can judge the product as they would in person. [L0390, 2019]
- Customer photos and reviews work as social proof on the product page and increase conversion. They should be collected deliberately rather than left to chance. [L0417, 2020-26]
- Before-and-after photos from real customers are a separate form of proof from social proof, because they show how the product works in the customer's favour. Use them alongside testimonials and customer counts. [L0516, 2023]
- Show store reviews on product pages. Buyers spend more when they trust that the store will deliver the product. [L1115, 2024]
- Repeat trust on the page: a star rating in the hero section, expanded with more testimonials and a count of five-star reviews further down. This gives the social proof more weight. [L0919, undated]
- Social proof includes reviews, star ratings, verified testimonials and third-party posts. Authority comes from press mentions, research-backed ingredients, expert or doctor endorsements and recognised review platforms such as Trustpilot. [L0919, undated]
- Combine several proof types in the same product copy, such as customer reviews paired with video demonstrations, or reviews paired with third-party testing results, rather than relying on one proof type. [L0295, 2021-22]

#### Where to place reviews and testimonials

- Show a selection of reviews near the top of the homepage, not hidden at the bottom, and add a clear link to read more. Social proof must be seen before the visitor scrolls far. [L0409, 2021-22]
- Show reviews from existing customers on the product page as soon as they exist, presented in an easy-to-read way. A brand-new product has no reviews yet, so this element takes time to build up. [L0396, 2021-22]
- Place a quoted customer testimonial near the top of the landing page. A photo of a happy customer with the product strengthens it but is optional. A short quote alone still works. [L0871, 2021+]
- A landing page must build trust fast, with a testimonial and a clear guarantee, before the visitor reads the full sales copy. Two things drive a first-time purchase: the offer and trust. [L0871, 2021+]
- A short quoted testimonial of one or two lines, with a small image and a five-star rating, works well when placed in the first scroll. It builds trust from the first visit. [L0916, 2022+]
- Social proof sections such as Facebook testimonials, Instagram stories and a Results section should be placed on the page to show real customer feedback. Presenting them in a format familiar to customers builds trust before purchase. [L1330, 2025-26]
- A Results section can present social proof about the product and supports conversion when placed on the page. [L1330, 2025-26]
- Trustpilot widgets can be placed on cart and checkout pages to show your rating as social proof. The lesson's example rating was 4.6 out of 5, rated 'Excellent'. [L0560, 2026]
- Social proof placed next to the product and bundle choices supports quick buying decisions. Example: a fall sock product page showed a 4.8-star rating with over 7,000 reviews, a 'Fall & Winter Must Have' positioning and a visible 'Best Seller' label on the bundle. [L0347, 2026]
- Typical trust elements on a sales page: a Trustpilot rating, a customer count such as 150,000+ satisfied customers, a 'featured in' row of recognised media brands, trust badges, and a line such as 'trusted by thousands' with testimonials beneath it. [L0919, undated]
- Recent-order notices, where a live message names a recent buyer, their state and how long ago they ordered, are social proof. Record them in the proof points column, together with how and where they are displayed. [L0893, 2021-22]

#### Counts, ratings and what the number means

- Use a specific, growing review count in the copy, such as '3,500+ five-star reviews', rather than a vague phrase like 'many happy customers'. Large precise numbers signal active, ongoing social proof. Place the count near the hero section or near the add-to-cart button. [L0919, undated]
- Once a store has a large review count, the exact live total adds little, so a rounded figure such as '10,000+' is enough. [L0409, 2021-22]
- Note the difference between the two count styles: rounded totals ('10,000+') suit a large, settled count, while a precise, growing count ('3,500+ five-star reviews') signals that the social proof is still active. [L0409, 2021-22; L0919, undated]
- Tell visitors in plain words what the review count means, for example 'people love us', instead of leaving them to interpret a number. Repeat a key message in a different format elsewhere on the page, but never repeat the same phrase in the same spot. [L0409, 2021-22]
- A quantitative example: a rating shown as 4.8 out of 5 stars from 1,000 happy customers. Both the average score and the number of reviewers help shoppers trust the product, and specific numbers build more trust than vague praise. [L1207, undated; L1209, undated]
- A rating of 4.8 out of 5 from a thousand happy customers is a strong social proof signal. [L1207, undated]
- Customer totals such as 'over 40,000 customers' or '45,000 happy customers' must come from your own order records, not from a competitor's page. Update the number whenever your count changes so the claim stays accurate. [L0600, 2026]
- A customer-count formula: 'Over [large number] [customers] and counting', placed near the top of the page next to ratings. It works because a big, growing number shows that many people already buy and trust the brand, which lowers the perceived risk for a stranger. Example 1 for a kitchen gadget: 'Over 250,000 home cooks and counting.' Example 2 for a skincare brand: 'Trusted by over 80,000 customers and counting.' [L0918, 2023]

#### Copy patterns and templates

- Social proof pattern: a rounded count with a plus sign, the action 'bought' and a recent timeframe, which reads as popular and current. Two new examples: '250+ people bought this in the past month' and '900+ shoppers bought this in the past year'. [L0389, 2025-26]
- A purchase-count threshold makes the social proof widget appear only when the number is high enough to persuade. A small figure such as '10 bought in the last week' can weaken trust, so set the threshold above that level. The example minimum used in the lesson was 150. [L0389, 2025-26]
- Social proof text block for a product page. Paste the text and keep both variables unchanged. The variable $value is replaced automatically with the purchase count and $timeframe with the chosen period (day, week, month or year). Changing the wording is allowed, but both $value and $timeframe must remain in the text, or the widget breaks. [L0389, 2025-26]

```
$value+ bought in past $timeframe
```

- Social proof pattern for a large number and a rating: one line with the number and rating, followed by a short plain-language sentence that says what it means. It works because a big number alone is not interpreted by the visitor, so the store should state the emotional meaning. Two new examples: '4.8 stars from 3,200+ riders. They ride further, and they tell us so.' and 'Over 5,000 five-star reviews. Customers say it is the best sleep they have had.' [L0409, 2021-22]
- Typical copy of a shipping and social proof block is covered in the guarantee and policy section below. [L1310, 2021-22]

#### Collecting and checking reviews

- Use reviews from real buyers only, even if the store is new. Writing product reviews yourself and presenting them as customer reviews misleads buyers and can violate consumer protection rules on fake reviews. [L1325, 2020-26]
- Reviews that mention the quality of customer service are the most valuable ones to feature on the store. Excellent customer service ranked as the most important factor for consumer trust in a company in a Zendesk survey. [L0908, 2020-26]
- A Zendesk survey reported that 88% of customers trust online reviews as much as personal recommendations from friends or family. Treat this as a vendor-sourced benchmark, not a verified figure. [L0908, 2020-26]
- Testimonials and FAQ answers should come from real customers and real questions, not invented copy. FAQ items usually capture objections, so they are answered with factual reassurance rather than persuasive copy. [L0917, undated]
- Showing a store with few social media followers can still build trust, because visitors expect an active account. A page-likes campaign can help, but it does not replace real customer reviews. (Recorded 2024.) [L0965, 2024]

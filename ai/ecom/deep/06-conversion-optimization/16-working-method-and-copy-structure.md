### Working method and copy structure

- Treat product copy as separate parts that are written and reworked one at a time, then combined into one page. This makes it easier to improve each part without rewriting the whole text. [L1420, 2021-22]
- A complete product copy has six segments: the primary promise or headline, a "paint a picture" section, the unique mechanism, the power benefits, the proof points, and a call to action. Check every product page for all six. [L1420, 2021-22]
- Arrange the segments in a fixed order so the finished page reads as one logical sequence from promise to call to action, following the segment order above. [L1420, 2021-22]
- The segments do not have to be written from top to bottom. Start with the headline, the features and benefits, or the product story, then fill in the other segments afterward. [L1420, 2021-22]
- The "paint a picture" segment helps the customer imagine the life or result they want after buying. It sits between the headline and the product mechanics, so the reader sees the outcome before the details. [L1420, 2021-22]
- The segment method works for a single product page in an ecommerce store and for a sales page inside a sales funnel. It applies to any product type, so the same six segments can be reused in both formats. [L1420, 2021-22]
- Writing product copy as one continuous text from start to finish often leads to weak transitions and missing segments. Mapping the blocks first shows gaps before the copy is finished. [L1420, 2021-22]
- To practise, run the product mapping exercise and write copy with the segment method on the first product. If a team member writes product copy, give them the same method and have them build the copy from the same blocks. [L1420, 2021-22]
- Copy alone does not sell an ecommerce product. It has to work together with product images and video that show the product in use and the benefits it delivers. Strong images without copy and strong copy without images both underperform, so plan them as one set. [L1460, 2021-22]
- Persuasive copy that beats competitors gives an advantage, because many competitors have good images but weak copy. Improving copy that persuades prospects more effectively is expected to raise the conversion rate. [L1460, 2021-22]
- Copying a weak website or landing page and expecting good results does not work. The store page needs its own improvement in copywriting and presentation. [L1469, 2023+]
- Copy is one lever for raising the conversion rate and sales from existing traffic, but it does not work alone. A strong product, good traffic generation and other on-site conversion work are needed as well. [L1493, 2021-22]
- Many small and medium-sized ecommerce stores put far less time and effort into their website copy than it deserves. This leaves sales on the table even when traffic and products are already in place. [L1493, 2021-22]
- Ecommerce copy does not need to be expert-level to noticeably increase sales. In most cases it only needs to be more persuasive than what competing stores offer the same customers. [L1493, 2021-22]
- Model product and sales copy on the sequence a good in-store salesperson follows, since an online visitor has no one to ask. [L1460, 2021-22]
  1. Identify the buyer's problem.
  2. Clarify the outcome they want.
  3. Give a clear view on how to solve it.
  4. Recommend the specific product.
  5. Ask for the purchase.

  A good product may sell itself, but the copy still has to explain why it fits this buyer. [L1460, 2021-22]
- Use headlines pulled from five-star Amazon reviews to find marketing angles. [L1260, undated]
- Use one benefit-driven headline, one benefit line, and a maximum of two short paragraphs. Use longer copy only for complex products that need education. Otherwise follow with an image or GIF, then benefit bullets. [L1260, undated]
- AI-generated copy can produce very brief micro-story blocks that do not fill a benefit section. Expand each section copy so it matches the image size and explains the feature. [L1331, 2025-26]
- Product descriptions must sell the product with concrete benefits, features and specifications, including dimensions. A description that only names the product does not persuade buyers. [L1346, 2020-26]

### Product mapping and message sheet

- Before writing, run a product mapping exercise for each product. List which of the six segments the available product information can support, and mark the building blocks that are still missing. Then write the missing blocks and assemble the copy. [L1420, 2021-22]
- Once the product map is filled in, pull each answer out of it while assembling the product copy. The primary problem, unique mechanism, feature and benefit pairs, and proof points each go into their matching part of the description, so you refer back to the map instead of writing from memory. [L1160, 2021-22]

### Primary promise and headlines

- Landing page headlines work best as a big, bold claim or benefit that states the desired outcome right away. People move toward pleasurable outcomes and away from painful ones, so surfacing the main benefit first captures attention immediately. [L1210, undated]
- Headlines on the landing page should be emotional and benefit-driven, so the visitor feels that the product solves a current pain point after reading the headline. Visitors first check the main image, the headline, and the product name before they scroll. [L1382, undated]
- Benefit headline formula: [desired outcome] + [a word that signals novelty] + [a word that removes perceived effort or sacrifice]. Novelty signals something different from past failed attempts. The effort-reducing word works because the desired goal is usually perceived as resource-intensive, slow, or hard. [L1210, undated]
  - Meal-replacement brand: "The simple new way to lose weight without giving up dessert."
  - Home-office chair: "The fresh, comfortable way to stop back pain at your desk."
- Benefit headline formula: state the outcome the buyer wants, add an effortless qualifier, then name the everyday result they keep afterwards. Structure: [desired outcome] + [effortless qualifier] + [result they live with]. It works because a visitor understands within seconds what the product does for them and what their life looks like afterwards. [L1363, 2023]
  - "Sleep through the night without waking to a cold room, and start every morning rested."
  - "Keep your kitchen counters spotless with one wipe, and spend your evenings on what you enjoy."
- Semi-headline: place a one-line statement directly under the product name that names the buyer's specific pain or desired result and signals who the product is for. Visitors arriving on mobile decide within seconds whether the product fits their problem, and a precise problem statement makes them feel understood. Formula: [specific pain or outcome] + [short promise of how the product delivers it]. [L1259, undated]
  - "Sore feet after every shift? Cushioned insoles that keep you moving all day."
  - "Frizz ruining your style? A leave-in serum that smooths hair in one pass."
- Test headlines with this procedure: [L1261, undated]
  1. Identify the audience and the one or two claims that matter most to them.
  2. Write a specific headline that addresses that audience and includes a concrete claim or offer.
  3. Write body copy that contains every important claim, with figures, and a clear next step.
  4. Include a coupon or reply device so the reader can act later.
  5. Test the headline against alternatives while keeping the body copy the same, and keep the winner by cost per customer.

### Unique selling points and shorthand

- Unique selling points work as shorthand for visitors who skim or already have high intent. Write them as short benefit lines that make the visitor believe they will reach the desired outcome. [L1207, undated]
- Add short USP lines (unique selling points or benefits) below the headline for visitors who skim or who already have high intent. Each USP should be one quick line that reinforces the desired outcome and lowers effort or sacrifice. [L1209, undated; L1210, undated]
- Shorthand USPs are short, benefit-led phrases placed near the top for skimmers and high-intent visitors who do not read the full body copy. Effective USPs lower effort and sacrifice, for example by stating suitability for all ages or that no experience is required, which lowers the barrier to entry. [L1210, undated; L1209, undated]
- Test every unique selling point by asking whether it lowers effort or sacrifice on the way to the outcome. Points that do not reduce effort, sacrifice, or risk add little to the page. [L1207, undated]
- Remove barriers to entry with USPs such as "suitable for all ages" or "no experience required", because they signal that anyone can get started. Lower the perceived effort and skill needed to buy and use the product. [L1209, undated]
- Pointing out in advance which qualities to notice makes buyers perceive those qualities, whereas identical products presented without guidance are chosen almost at random. Name the features that set the product apart so the buyer sees them. [L1261, undated]

### Unique mechanism and features-to-benefits

- The unique mechanism explains why this product delivers its result differently from alternatives. Power benefits and proof points then back up the main promise, so each claim has support on the page. [L1420, 2021-22]
- Benefit copy should describe what the product does for the customer to reach their goal, not list product features. [L1363, 2023]
- Write product copy around the benefit the buyer gets in daily life, not around a list of features. Explain why the buyer would keep reading and why they would buy. [L1194, undated]
- Benefit line pattern: state the feature, then say what the customer gains from it in their own terms (time, money, health, peace of mind). [L1160, 2021-22]
  - Warranty: a lifetime warranty means the customer never has to pay again if the product fails.
  - Size: a compact unit fits a home, so the customer no longer needs to travel for the same result.
- Product features should be presented as proof that the promised outcome is achievable, not as stand-alone specifications. Each feature should support the desired result, for example by showing that the product fits a wide range of sizes or is comfortable to use, which raises the perceived likelihood of success. [L1210, undated]
- Feature-to-outcome pattern: state a feature with a concrete range or measurement, then link it to a benefit that supports the promised result. [L1210, undated]
  - Backpack: "Fits laptops from 13 to 17 inches, so it works for students and commuters alike."
  - Kitchen knife: "Hand-forged 8-inch blade that stays sharp for months, so prep takes minutes."
- Feature bullets for a fitness hula hoop should cover universal use (fully adjustable design), a wide size range (span 20 to 42 inch for all waist sizes), a soft comfortable fit, and stability (it does not fall down, unlike conventional hula hoops). [L1207, undated]
- A size range of 20 to 42 inch on a fitness hula hoop supports the claim that one product fits all waist sizes. [L1207, undated]
- Feature proof example for a sizing product: adjustable design with a 20 to 42 inch waist range to fit all waist sizes, soft comfortable fit, and a modern design. Each feature answers the question of whether the product will work for this specific buyer. [L1209, undated]

### Proof and credibility

- When a promise sounds big, shoppers want proof. Specific features such as a clinically proven ingredient raise the perceived likelihood that the outcome will be achieved. [L1207, undated]
- Clinically proven ingredients or claims act as proof that increases the perceived likelihood of reaching the outcome, especially when the buyer doubts the promise. Add a verifiable proof element when a benefit sounds too good to be true. [L1209, undated]
- A study of a large ecommerce store that optimized its product titles reported about 20% more traffic, about 70% more ROI and a higher conversion rate. The gain came from appearing for more specific, relevant searches. [L1328, 2015]
- Show a specific count of satisfied results to prove a claim, such as 300 employees who received a raise in one week after studying a course, or 280 new bank depositors in one month from circular letters alone. Counts certified by records carry more weight than adjectives. [L1369, undated]
- A "best choice" badge without any supporting reason means nothing to a shopper. Use badges and claims that describe a concrete benefit the buyer can understand. [L1346, 2020-26]

### Simplicity and three-step setup

- Reduce a process to three simple steps to lower the barrier to entry. People understand threes quickly, which avoids cognitive overload. [L1207, undated]
- Three-step setup format: presenting setup or usage as three simple numbered steps reduces perceived complexity and avoids cognitive overload. Complex processes should be distilled into three clear steps. [L1210, undated]
  - Water filter: "One: fill the jug. Two: insert the filter. Three: start pouring."
- Setup procedure: [L1209, undated]
  1. Show the setup as a simple numbered sequence with exactly three steps (e.g. "one two three let's go").
  2. Keep each step short and concrete.
  3. State that the product is simple to use, so the buyer sees low effort and time required.

### Effort and comparison framing

- Humans conserve energy and look for maximum output for minimum input. Presenting less effort and less time for the same result makes the purchase feel like a quick win. [L1207, undated; L1210, undated; L1209, undated]
- People try to conserve effort and get the maximum result for the minimum input, so promises like "less effort, more results" make a product more attractive. Message reduced effort, sacrifice and time together. [L1209, undated; L1210, undated; L1207, undated]
- Compare your product's cost and results with the alternatives buyers already try, such as supplements or doctor visits. Showing only features does not explain why the product is worth buying. [L1194, undated]
- Framing example: 30 minutes of a workout is presented as equal to a two-mile walk in calories, which connects a new value to a reference the buyer already understands. Choose a familiar reference point (distance, time, cost) that makes the benefit easy to grasp. [L1209, undated]
- Effort-comparison framing: describe the required activity in terms of a familiar everyday reference the buyer already understands, such as relating a short workout to the calories of a known walking distance. This makes an unfamiliar result easier to judge and more believable. [L1210, undated]
  - Supplement: "One scoop gives you the protein of a full chicken breast, without the cooking."
  - Language app: "Ten minutes a day gets you the vocabulary of a semester-long class."
- Cost-calculation formula: ask the reader to add up what his current method costs in money and time, so he reaches the gap himself before you present your product. A figure the buyer calculates himself is more convincing than one the seller states, and he feels the cost now. [L1369, undated]
  - Delivery van: add up each month's fuel, insurance, repairs and driver hours for your current delivery van, then compare that total with the monthly cost of the service offered.
  - Data entry: total what your team spends each month on manual data entry and rework, then compare that total with the software subscription.

### Similarity and social proof

- Shoppers quickly check whether others are similar to them as a threat test. Wording such as "customers like you" signals that the product is meant for people like the visitor. [L1207, undated; L1209, undated]
- Buyers judge whether someone is a threat by checking similarity, so describing reviewers as "customers like you" signals that the shop's customers are like the visitor. Use the similarity principle in testimonial and review wording. [L1209, undated; L1207, undated]
- Similarity pattern: [customers like you] + [specific result or time]. The similarity cue lowers the feeling of threat and makes the result feel reachable. Referring to the buyer as part of a group signals that the buyer is not a threat and that others like them succeeded. Pairing this with a count of satisfied buyers strengthens trust. [L1207, undated; L1210, undated]
  - "Busy parents like you can finish this routine in 10 minutes."
  - "Beginners like you see results in the first week."
  - Skincare brand: "Customers like you saw clearer skin in 14 days."
  - Running shoe: "Runners like you finish their first 10K in 60 days."

### Problem, agitation and solution

- Start with a pain that the audience already agrees with, such as a universal complaint, then introduce the product as the fix. Shoppers are more ready to buy when the problem feels familiar. [L1207, undated]
- Problem-then-solution structure: state a universal pain point in a short, easily agreed sentence, then introduce the product as the fix. Dramatizing the result with a vivid metaphor adds emotional weight. [L1210, undated]
  - Bathroom cleaner: "Scrubbing tile is exhausting, so we made cleaning take seconds."
- Problem-solution copy works well below the hero: name a frustrating problem, then present the product as the fix. Keep this section simple. [L1363, 2023]
- Problem-agitation-solution formula: state the buyer's problem in clear terms they already agree with, stir up the emotions the problem causes such as anger, fear, guilt or embarrassment, then reveal the product as the answer with its benefits. People act more readily to avoid pain than to gain something. [L1370, undated]
  - Backup service: "Your laptop can die tonight and take five years of family photos with it. Every hour without a backup is a gamble, and the losses hit hardest when you least expect them. Our service copies everything every night so a broken drive costs you a coffee, not your memories."

### Objection handling

- Address objections such as cheap knockoffs before the buyer raises them, for example by stating that imitations are not the same product. Objections such as cheap knockoffs or many competitors should be addressed on the page before the buyer raises them. Framing the brand favourably against competitors, for example by contrasting results, makes the brand look better in comparison. Handling the objection on the page removes a reason to leave. [L1209, undated; L1210, undated]
- Objection-handling pattern: name the most likely doubt in a short statement that answers it before the buyer asks. [L1210, undated]
  - Leather wallet: "Cheap wallets crack in months; ours is built to last ten years."
  - Electric toothbrush: "Most timers are inaccurate; ours buzzes exactly every 30 seconds."
- Make the buyer feel the offer is fair and not too good to be true by answering the doubts up front. Skepticism often stops buyers who otherwise have the money and interest, so an unaddressed objection costs response even when the offer is solid. [L1370, undated]
- Answer objections in order: [L1370, undated]
  1. State the direct answer clearly.
  2. Add a supporting testimonial, case history or short story that proves the answer.
  3. Restate or reference the guarantee or free trial offer.

  Use a FAQ page with the objections phrased as questions when that suits the format.
- Objection-framing opener: admit that the offer converts only a small percentage of recipients, express personal concern that someone might miss out because of an explanation gap, mention how many customers praised the product, and then say you held a brainstorm to list the reasons someone would say no. Then list each reason and answer it. This turns objections into a sincere, helpful presentation. [L1370, undated]
- Guarantee terms should spell out what happens: the buyer returns the item at the seller's expense and receives a full refund, with no obligation during the trial period. Stating the terms clearly removes doubt better than general reassurance. [L1369, undated]
- Guaranteed letter: offer a small payment, such as $10 to $50, to any recipient who reads the entire letter and feels their time was wasted. The payout should be affordable, the message strong enough that the reader will want to read, and the total cost measured against the profit from the campaign. [L1370, undated]

### Emotional and logical reasons

- Motive selection checklist before writing a sales letter: [L1369, undated]
  1. Ask whether the product would make the buyer richer, healthier or happier.
  2. Ask whether it would raise his standing with the people he respects.
  3. Ask whether it would let him do, write or say something better than before.
  4. Ask whether it would protect his money, time or reputation from loss.
  5. Ask whether it would help the people he loves.
  6. Choose the strongest answer as the main motive and present it only from the buyer's point of view.
- Only-some-can-qualify approach: present membership or access as an exclusive group to appeal to the reader's desire for recognition and status. This works especially well for premium memberships and premium card-style offers. [L1370, undated]
- Ego appeal: present the product or service as a status symbol by asking what it says about the reader if they lack it, for example asking why they have no fax number when asked for one. Ego drives purchase decisions alongside practical benefits, and the same appeal can be reused for new technology such as cell phones or websites. [L1370, undated]
- Lead with the buyer's pain and the promise second: a computer-seller sales letter opened by asking whether the reader hated their computer, listed the frustrations, then made concrete promises such as plain-English talk and a free thirty-minute consultation. [L1370, undated]

### Calls to action and reply devices

- Tell readers what action to take and make it easy by providing a coupon, phone number or simple reply method. Readers forget decisions quickly, so reminders built into the ad improve follow-through. [L1261, undated]
- Set the offer's terms in the ad itself, such as the free sample, the deadline or the store where it can be picked up. Terms that are vague reduce response. [L1261, undated]
- Include a reply card with spaces for name, address and the item wanted, plus a stamped return envelope, so that ordering takes one action. Make the card easy to fill in and clearly marked as the way to order. [L1369, undated]
- Include a prepaid return card or envelope with each mailing and tell the reader to tuck a check or dollar bill into the letter for a fast order. Make it easy to pay or order with the same enclosure. [L1369, undated]
- Many business readers skip to the last paragraph of a letter to learn what it costs and what to do, so the hook that tells them the action must sit in that paragraph. A close needs two parts: the persuasion that shows the gain, the risk of delay and the guarantee, and then a hook that tells the reader what to do. [L1369, undated]
- Write the close in this order: [L1369, undated]
  1. Tell the reader the exact action to take, using the reply card, the envelope or a specific phrase to send.
  2. Say what the reader gets by acting now.
  3. State the specific penalty for not acting, such as a lost price, lost stock or a lost opportunity, with a date.
  4. Give the means to act at once, such as a stamped envelope or a prepaid card, so no step is left to the reader.
- Letting a prospect say "tomorrow" or "later" is one of the largest causes of lost sales, because a postponed buyer rarely returns. Give a concrete reason to decide now, such as a deadline or a limited quantity. [L1369, undated]

## Valuing and selling a store: numbers buyers check

### Valuation basis and formula

Store valuation is monthly net profit times a multiple, plus the value of qualifying assets.

```
Store valuation    = Monthly Net Profit x Multiple + Assets
Monthly Net Profit = Revenue - (COGS + Expenses)
COGS               = product cost + shipping + extra costs
Expenses           = marketing + software + other business costs
Monthly Net Profit is averaged over the last 12 months.
```
[L0553, 2018; L0858, 2018]

- The version without an asset line is Valuation = (Income - Expenses) × Multiple, with the multiple based on monthly net profit. [L0231, 2019]
- Assets are added only when they qualify (see Assets in the sale). [L0553, 2018]
- Net profit per month is what determines a business's value, and a business making no net profit is worth nothing in a sale. [L1111, 2020; older]
- Net profit is what buyers price; a valuation built on revenue is not used. [L1111, 2020]

### Multiples: ranges and what moves them

Sources give different ranges. Use the one that matches your store's profile and the date of the source.

| Source | Range or value | Basis | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| one source | 21x to 35x | Monthly net profit, dropshipping | Current [L0231, 2019] |
| one source | 22x to 30x typical in last 12 months; 20x to 50x general range | Monthly net profit | Current [L0553, 2018] |
| one source | 22x to 30x | Monthly net profit | Current [L0858, 2018] |
| one source | About 30x | Monthly net profit | Older: 2022 [L0854, 2022] |
| one source | About 30x | Average monthly net profit | Older: 2020 [L1111, 2020] |
| one source | 2.5x to 3.5x annual profit (single traffic source, sold to a broker) | Annual profit | Older: 2021-22 [L0511, 2021-22] |

- The 2.5x to 3.5x figure uses annual profit, so it is not directly comparable with the monthly multiples. For reference, 30x monthly equals 2.5x annual. [L0511, 2021-22; L0553, 2018]
- Diversified traffic from Google, email sequences, SEO and a stable TikTok or Snapchat flywheel pushes the broker multiple higher than the single-source range. [L0511, 2021-22]
- The multiple rises with track record length, store size and diversification, and falls for short history, a single product, a single traffic source, or a six-month-only average. [L0553, 2018; L0858, 2018]
- A store bought and resold every 12 to 18 months lands at the lower end of the range. [L0553, 2018]
- Buyers treat the multiple as a discount for risk. Concentration on one product, one traffic source (for example only Facebook or only Google Ads), one supplier, or a declining profit trend lowers it. Twelve months of stable or growing profit raises it. [L0553, 2018]

Factors that move the multiple:

| Factor | Larger multiple | Smaller multiple |
|---|---|---|
| Track record | Longer | Shorter |
| Profit average used | 12-month average | 6-month average |
| Store size | Bigger store | Smaller store |
| Product range | More diversification | Single product |

[L0858, 2018]

- Sites are sometimes valued on the average net profit of the previous three months, so changes need time to show in the figures before a sale. [L1468, 2014]
- Post-sale support can change the price: a 90-day post-sale period with two calls per month and unlimited email support can add roughly $50,000 to the sale price, because new buyers overvalue the support. [L0553, 2018]

Worked examples at different multiples:

- $5,000 monthly net profit at 21x is $105,000; at 35x the same store is $175,000. [L0554, 2019]
- $5,000 monthly net profit at 30x is about $150,000. [L0854, 2022; older]
- $10,000 monthly net profit at 22x to 30x gives about $220,000 to $300,000. [L0553, 2018]
- $25,000 monthly net profit sold for about $750,000 through a broker (about 30x). [L1111, 2020; older]

Broker estimate from margin:

- EBITDA equals profit margin times revenue. A 30 percent margin on $1,000,000 in sales gives EBITDA of about $200,000, which implies roughly $450,000 to $700,000 at broker multiples. Higher margins raise the valuation sharply. [L0511, 2021-22; older]

| Earnings trend | Decreasing | Steady |

[L0858, 2018]

- A 12-month-old apparel store on AliExpress with Facebook-only traffic and decreasing earnings made $8.7K net profit per month. It was listed at $183,817 (21X) and sold for $120,000 (13X) after 126 days on the market. [L0858, 2018]
- An 18-month-old home and garden store with Google Ads, organic traffic and Facebook retargeting, steady earnings and $5K net profit per month was listed at $141,957 (28X) and sold for $133,000 (26X) within 10 days. [L0858, 2018]
- The second store sold for more despite lower monthly profit because its quality factors were stronger. [L0858, 2018]

### Net profit averaging

- Use a 12-month average of net profit as the default valuation basis, because it covers seasonality. [L0553, 2018]
- Shift to a 3- or 6-month average only when a major change makes the 12-month figure unrepresentative of current performance, for example losing organic traffic to a Google update or a sudden viral rise. [L0553, 2018]
- Conflict: [L1468, 2014] says sites are usually valued on the previous three months' average, while [L1111, 2020] uses the trailing 12 months together with the average of the last three months. Apply the 12-month default unless a major change applies. Where a source uses the three-month figure, changes made now need time to appear in it. [L0553, 2018; L1468, 2014; L1111, 2020]
- Track revenue, costs and owner time separately for each store from the start, because many sellers below $100,000 in revenue cannot say how many hours a specific store needs. [L1468, 2014]
- Buyers use a 12-month average, so a store that sold badly in the period before listing is valued low. [L0553, 2018; L0834, 2021]

### Records and bookkeeping

- A buyer will not trust a P&L built from manually typed figures in a spreadsheet. To sell a business, you must prove your P&L is correct with records. [L0632, 2021-22; older]
- When a business sells for six or seven figures or more, buyers scrutinize the numbers strictly. A small sale may pass with little checking, but larger deals will not. [L0632, 2021-22; older]
- Keep accurate financial records with all expenses documented; accounting errors destroy buyer trust and reduce the final sale price. [L0231, 2019]
- Keep accurate financial records from the start, because reconstructing 4- or 5-year-old records is a large amount of work. Accurate records also support decisions such as return on ad spend and profit per product. [L0554, 2019]
- Track return on ad spend and profit per product, since these are needed for smart business decisions and buyers look for them in financial records. [L0554, 2019]
- Mistakes in the numbers quickly erode buyer trust, and buyers may suspect that information is hidden. Keep records as you go, including historical data for suppliers and VAs, even when changing work accounts. [L0554, 2019]
- Use QuickBooks, Xero or FreshBooks, or a spreadsheet, to track expenses. For multiple stores, keep each store's books separate so the buyer can see each one's profit. [L1468, 2014]
- Keep supplier invoices and bills in one Google Drive organized by month, year, or supplier. This archive makes it easier to pull numbers into the P&L when selling. [L0554, 2019]
- A broker's vetting includes checking that the Shopify sales numbers are genuine, so buyers of a listed store do not need to verify them on their own. Buyers still run their own due diligence on the overall deal. [L0554, 2019]
- Keep separate, clear financials for each network of related stores. [L1111, 2020; older]

### Metrics to gather before listing

Gather these before selling:

1. Hours worked per week
2. Company start date
3. Average monthly revenue
4. Average monthly expenses
5. Number of products
6. Unique monthly visitors
7. Email subscriber count
8. Number of social media channels with follower counts

[L0231, 2019]

- A business sells best with 18 to 24 months of history, because buyers look at the trailing 12 months of net profit. [L1111, 2020; older]

### Add-backs

- An add-back is an expense that is valid for tax purposes but not for valuing the business, such as a conference trip or a one-off redesign. [L0554, 2019]
- List it as an expense on the P&L and add it back to net profit to be transparent with buyers. [L0554, 2019]
- Buyers often negotiate add-backs. A conference trip is an easy add-back. [L0554, 2019]
- A site redesign can be argued as a one-off, but buyers may treat it as a recurring cost if they expect redesigns every couple of years. [L0554, 2019]

### Assets in the sale

- Assets that can be added to a store sale are inventory (valued at wholesale), equipment such as laptops or photography gear, and software. [L0553, 2018]
- An asset counts only if it is included in the sale and necessary to run the business. [L0553, 2018]
- Discontinued stock and personal costs such as travel are not recoverable. [L0553, 2018]

### Google Analytics and due diligence

- Google Analytics is essential for verifying traffic sources and visitor trends. Buyers require access to confirm these metrics during due diligence. [L0231, 2019]
- Install Google Analytics about six months before the sale. Buyers rely on it to detect bot or fake traffic and to see traffic sources, and it is the de facto standard. [L1468, 2014]
- Preparing Google Analytics access for buyer due diligence takes active time: you must grant the necessary permissions, provide documentation of historical traffic sources, and remain available to verify visitor data accuracy and trends. [L0231, 2019]
- In Google Analytics, be an admin or know the admin username, and be ready to add buyers as users so they can run their own reports. Screenshots of Analytics can be enough for early review. [L1468, 2014]
- If a seller refuses Google products, Clicky is an accepted alternative to Google Analytics for buyers' due diligence. [L1468, 2014]
- Server log statistics such as AWStats, or stats you simply post without a real analytics account, are not accepted for due diligence. Buyers will not trust them. [L1468, 2014]
- Screenshots of earnings and traffic are easy to fake, while video is harder to fake and more trusted. Record a screencast of earnings and traffic, especially for public auction listings. [L1468, 2014]
- Every financial figure in a listing must match the others. State the exact date range for earnings and traffic, and say whether traffic is sessions or page views. [L1468, 2014]
- Brokers verify earnings by logging into the account and checking screenshots themselves, or by having the seller show the data live. Expect this step before a listing goes live. [L1468, 2014]

### Seasonality evidence

- Buyers of seasonal niches want at least two years of sales data before trusting a spike, because one year cannot show whether the spike repeats in the same period. [L0554, 2019]
- If the second year shows the spike in the same place as the first, the store is much easier to value. [L0554, 2019]
- The 12-month average covers seasonality, which is why it is the default basis. [L0553, 2018]
- Do not sell the store while sales are down, because the price will be lower than what it is worth. [L0834, 2021; older]

### Profit-growth levers before sale

Three levers raise store value:

- Raise revenue with profitable products, suppliers and traffic streams.
- Hold or improve margins.
- Lower buyer risk to raise the multiple.

[L0553, 2018; L0858, 2018]

Ways to grow profit:

- To grow profit, improve ad ROI and cut unprofitable ads, renegotiate product costs or move to a new supplier, and build relationships that turn customers into repeat buyers. [L0858, 2018]
- Email list growth, higher average order value, higher customer lifetime value, and better return on ad spend are revenue and margin levers. [L0553, 2018]
- Adding email marketing to a store can lift revenue by roughly 10 to 20 percent, according to the lesson's estimate. [L0553, 2018]
- A long-running database of outreach contacts and earned backlinks can add resale value, since a buyer may value it much like an email list. [L0422, 2021-22; older]
- A sustainable cost per acquisition, built through upsells, pre-sells and reaching customers before the purchase, raises valuation along with defensibility, brand awareness and profit margin. [L0511, 2021-22; older]

Improve margins before selling in four steps:

1. Stop exploratory ad spend that does not return a positive ROI, especially in the 12 months before a sale.
2. Renegotiate product costs by asking the supplier for lower prices on best-selling products, or source an alternative supplier with the same quality.
3. Build customer relationships and retention, since keeping existing customers is cheaper than acquiring new ones.
4. Improve ad return on ad spend.

[L0553, 2018]

- Eliminate ad spend that is exploratory and does not currently return ROI during the 12 months leading up to a sale, because buyers value proven profit rather than tests. [L0553, 2018]

### Sale timing and format

- There are three ways to increase store value: grow revenue while keeping margins, grow profit by cutting spending, or improve the multiple the store can sell for. [L0858, 2018]
- Sell a store as an asset sale instead of the business entity. Stores profitable for at least 12 months can be listed on Empire Flippers, while newer stores can be posted on the Fast Business Forum. [L0854, 2022; older]
- A store bought and resold every 12 to 18 months lands at the lower end of the multiple range. [L0553, 2018]

#### Further details

- Annual return on buying a website equals monthly net profit multiplied by 12, divided by the purchase price. For example, a site priced at $100,000 that earns $5,000 net profit per month returns 60% per year if profit stays flat. The result is a best-case figure because it assumes no growth and no decline. [L1468, 2014]

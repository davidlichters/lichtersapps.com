## Cash management, Profit First, taxes and budgeting

### Cash flow in dropshipping

- A customer order is charged to a card, the payment processor pays out, the supplier is ordered and usually paid at shipment, and the supplier is often paid on a credit card that settles about 30 days later. Cash comes in before it goes out, but money must still be set aside to pay suppliers later. ** [L0484, 2021-22]
- Inventory-based dropshipping generally has a positive cash flow pattern. Service businesses that pay their team before receiving payment, and often wait around 30 days for payment, have a negative pattern. ** [L0484, 2021-22]
- COGS is recorded when the supplier is paid, so businesses with long lead times show large profit months when income arrives before supplier payments, followed by lower-profit or loss months. This pattern is normal and does not indicate a problem. ** [L0484, 2021-22]
- Dropshipping cash moves in a wave. In a rising season the store collects customer cash faster than it pays suppliers. When demand falls, it pays suppliers for earlier orders. Supplier charges can lag orders by weeks or months, so cash can look worse than the sales trend. ** [L1046, 2021-22]
- In a seasonal store, income often rises before expenses because supplier payments lag sales by several weeks (for example 4 to 6 weeks for made-to-order products). Cash builds early and is spent later in the season even as income falls. Some periods show profit while cash decreases, and one profitable month does not mean cash is available to spend. ** [L0484, 2021-22]
- Treating a strong profit month as available cash leads to overspending and a sudden drop in the bank balance when supplier invoices are paid. A loss month after a profit month can be a normal timing effect, so decisions should rest on reports and cash flow, not on single-month reactions. ** [L0484, 2021-22]
- Save cash from profitable periods to pay suppliers later, because supplier payments may come due after the income has already been received and spent. ** [L0484, 2021-22]
- The smooth seasonal profit and cash curve in the source is idealized. Real dropshipping businesses are far more volatile month to month, so plan reserves for sharp swings rather than the smooth average. ** [L0484, 2021-22]
- A surprise supplier bill means you did not track what you owe, and it can turn an apparent profit into a serious loss. ** [L0632, 2021-22]
- A bank balance higher than expected is not automatically free profit, because unpaid supplier invoices may still be owed from that money. Compare the bank balance against what you owe suppliers before deciding how much you can spend or hire. ** [L0632, 2021-22]
- High-ticket dropshipping is a cash flow business with constant money moving through sales and supplier payments. Keep cash flow positive, because spending customer money on other expenses leads to owing suppliers quickly. ** [L1480, 2021-22]
- A growing debt balance is a warning sign even when cash looks healthy, because liabilities that exceed assets leave the business exposed. Track card and loan balances every month and aim to pay them down over time. ** [L1046, 2021-22]

### Cash planning and startup funding

- Bookkeeping records what already happened to your money. Cash planning decides in advance what each incoming dollar will do. Bookkeeping alone does not prevent cash shortages, so both are needed. ** [L1200, 2021-22]
- Cash flow management means knowing how much you can safely take out of the business without harming it. Without a planned allocation, the owner cannot answer that question from the bank balance alone. ** [L1200, 2021-22]
- Cash flow is named as the biggest struggle for entrepreneurs. Forecast it realistically, do not assume early sales will last, and keep reserves so that a slow month does not threaten personal finances. [L0832, 2019]
- You will need to front cash to buy inventory and get it into your warehouse, 3PL, or Amazon until sales profit can cover future orders. Plan cash for this entire period, which can last a while depending on lead times and origin. ** [L1154, 2021-22]
- A common mistake is planning the budget around the first order only, such as the cash currently in the bank, without planning for the next orders. Keep a cash plan that covers the whole cycle until the business generates positive cash flow. ** [L1154, 2021-22]
- Early ad spend is usually funded from a credit card. The aim is to turn that spend into sales quickly, but the full daily budget does not have to be paid out of pocket every day. ** [L0438, 2021-22]
- Plan about $1,000 in marketing spend for the first few months, about $30 per day. Sales should cover much of this spend as the store starts earning, so little of your own money is needed. ** [L0458, 2021-22; L0771, 2021-22]
- Plan for a first-month budget of about 1,000 dollars for collecting data and generating sales. You can start with less, but you will collect data more slowly. ** [L0551, 2020]
- Keep the starting budget balanced between marketing and inventory. Too much inventory without demand leaves stock stuck, while too much demand without enough product means orders cannot be fulfilled. [L1438, 2023+]
- Professional videos and photos for a clothing brand can cost about $1,000 per month, a workable starting budget for content that drives early sales. [L1438, 2023+]
- Total investment plus advertising spend since the start was estimated at over a quarter million, up to half a million, with the currency not stated. Plan total funding needs with this scale of spend in mind. [L1438, 2023+]
- A US company can unlock funding options such as credit lines from Shopify Capital, 8fig and Sellers Funding. These can finance inventory or scale ad spend. [L1512, 2023]

### Inventory forecasting and stock tracking

- Inventory forecasting is non-negotiable. Spend at least one hour per week on it, with half a day with your team as the optimum. It is the founder/CEO's direct responsibility and is not delegated alone to an inventory planner. [L0041, 2025-26]
- Inventory forecasting directly prevents bankruptcy. It is the single most important operational task for founders because it drives everything else. [L0041, 2025-26]
- Inventory forecast process:
  1. Review what inventory and sales happened in the same period last year.
  2. Create predictions for the coming period.
  3. Write the reasoning behind each prediction.
  4. Adjust forecasts weekly based on actual results.

  [L0041, 2025-26]
- Days of stock:

  ```
  Days of stock = units on hand ÷ average units sold per day
  Example: 300 units ÷ 10 units sold per day = 30 days, about one month of stock
  ```

  [L1154, 2021-22]
- The 80/20 rule for inventory: 20% of products generate 80% of sales. Update inventory in real time only for the top 20% of top 20%, not for all products. ** [L0268, 2020]
- Track each store's stock daily per supplier. A rapid drop in monthly gross sales, for example from $50,000 to $20,000, signals out-of-stock problems that need new supplier approvals. ** [L0834, 2021]

### Tax obligations and deductions

- Financial forecasting is essential because tax authorities will not wait for payment. Schedule and predict sales tax and income tax obligations monthly or quarterly. [L0041, 2025-26]
- If you cannot forecast inventory and revenue accurately, you cannot forecast the cash available for tax payments. Government can seize products and inventory for unpaid taxes, which forces business failure. [L0041, 2025-26]
- In the US, shipping costs, Shopify fees, transaction fees (2.9%), ad spend and product cost are all tax-deductible business expenses. You pay income tax on revenue minus total expenses. ** [L0272, 2022]
- Net profit, and what is taxed:

  ```
  Net profit = gross revenue − deductions (COGS, shipping, ad spend, merchant fees, other expenses)
  Example: 1,000,000 gross − about 800,000 deductions = 200,000 net profit
  Only the net profit is taxed.
  ```

  [L1344, 2020-26]
- Taxes are not included in the ROAS or ad budget calculation, because they are paid out of profit. [L1115, 2024]
- In the Profit First allocation, taxes take a set share of real revenue (see Profit First allocations). [L1200, 2021-22; L1334, 2021-22]

- Prepaying Google Ads is an optional billing method, not the default. For a new store it is generally not advisable, because the normal billing-threshold setup keeps cash free until spend builds up. ** [L1068, 2019]

### Profit First allocations

- Setting spending as percentages of real revenue creates a budget limit, because money not allocated to an account should not be spent. This counters the tendency for spending to grow to match whatever cash is available (Parkinson's law). ** [L1200, 2021-22]
- Real revenue is monthly revenue minus cost of goods sold. With COGS at 80% of revenue, real revenue is 20% of revenue, and the profit, owner salary, taxes, staff and operating expense allocations are all percentages of real revenue that must total 100%. ** [L1334, 2021-22]
- COGS and real revenue formulas:

  ```
  COGS amount = income in the period × COGS percentage
  Real revenue = income − COGS amount = income × (1 − COGS percentage)
  Example: income of 100,000 per month at 80% COGS gives 80,000 COGS and 20,000 real revenue.
  Example: income of 25,000 per week at 80% COGS gives 20,000 COGS and 5,000 real revenue.
  ```

  ** [L1200, 2021-22]
- The percentages assigned to real revenue accounts must add up to 100%, so every dollar of real revenue has a job. Each account amount equals real revenue multiplied by that account's percentage. ** [L1200, 2021-22]
- Example allocation of real revenue. The percentages are examples, not fixed rules. ** [L1200, 2021-22]

  | Account | Share of real revenue | Example: real revenue 20,000 per month | Example: real revenue 5,000 per week |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Profit | 10% | 2,000 | 500 |
  | Owner pay | 20% | 4,000 | 1,000 |
  | Taxes | 10% | 2,000 | 500 |
  | Staff | 30% | 6,000 | 1,500 |
  | Operating expenses | 30% | 6,000 | 1,500 |
  | Total | 100% | 20,000 | 5,000 |

- Profit First example, monthly, COGS at 80% of revenue. The source's own allocation differs from the table above: owner salary is 10% and operating expenses 40% here, against owner pay 20% and operating expenses 30% in the example above. Both total 100%. Use the allocation that a Profit First assessment gives for your business. ** [L1334, 2021-22]

  | Line | Share | Example at $20,000 revenue | Example at $40,000 revenue |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Revenue | 100% | $20,000 | $40,000 |
  | Cost of goods sold | 80% of revenue | $16,000 | $32,000 |
  | Real revenue | 20% of revenue | $4,000 | $8,000 |
  | Profit | 10% of real revenue | $400 | $800 |
  | Owner salary | 10% of real revenue | $400 | $800 |
  | Taxes | 10% of real revenue | $400 | $800 |
  | Staff | 30% of real revenue | $1,200 | $2,400 |
  | Operating expenses | 40% of real revenue | $1,600 | $3,200 |
  | Total allocation | 100% | $4,000 | $8,000 |

- Allocation percentages depend on the business, and no universal set of numbers can be given. Use a Profit First assessment to set them before implementing the plan. ** [L1334, 2021-22]
- Weekly example with 45,000 in sales and 80% COGS. ** [L1200, 2021-22]

  | Item | Amount |
  |---|---|
  | Sales for the week | 45,000 |
  | COGS (80%) | 36,000 |
  | Real revenue | 9,000 |
  | Profit (10%) | 900 |
  | Owner pay (20%) | 1,800 |
  | Taxes (10%) | 900 |
  | Staff (30%) | 2,700 |
  | Operating expenses (30%) | 2,700 |

- Lowering the COGS percentage from 80% to 75% on 45,000 in weekly sales raises real revenue to 11,250 and lowers the COGS amount to 33,750. Lowering operating expenses from 30% to 20% frees 10% of real revenue to allocate elsewhere, for example to profit. ** [L1200, 2021-22]
- Regular allocation routine. ** [L1200, 2021-22]
  1. Check how much income has arrived in the income account since the last allocation.
  2. Multiply that income by your COGS percentage and transfer that amount to the COGS account.
  3. The remaining money is real revenue; calculate each other account's share from it.
  4. Transfer the profit share to the profit account, the owner pay share to the owner pay account, the tax share to the taxes account, the staff share to the staff account, and the operating share to the operating expenses account.
  5. Pay suppliers only from the COGS account, staff only from the staff account, and other business costs only from the operating expenses account.
  6. Pay the owner's salary from the owner pay account at every allocation, and leave profit untouched until the quarterly distribution.
  7. Repeat the allocation on a fixed rhythm, either twice a month or weekly, and review the percentages once a quarter.
- Allocations can run twice a month, as the book suggests, or weekly, as the presenter's company does. Choose one fixed rhythm and keep it, because balances are checked and split at that interval. ** [L1200, 2021-22]
- For planning, use a monthly rather than weekly or fortnightly calculation, based on the whole month's income. Start from a long-term target such as a lump-sum exit or a revenue and profit goal, then work backwards to a monthly revenue or profit target. ** [L1334, 2021-22]
- Review allocation percentages once a quarter, and adjust them when a balance keeps building up unused or when margins change. Surplus from an account that is not being used can move to profit or owner pay. ** [L1200, 2021-22]
- Keep allocating in this way even when the business is young. Keep profit low during startup to reinvest in the business, but never take zero profit; owners should always pay themselves some profit. ** [L1334, 2021-22]
- Operating expenses in the Profit First plan must include advertising and PPC costs. Make sure the operating expense budget can cover paid ads before increasing spend. ** [L1334, 2021-22]
- Using accounting software to separate money without real bank accounts is a mistake. Software only shows numbers and does not hold the money apart, so use separate accounts in addition to bookkeeping. ** [L1200, 2021-22]
- Taking unplanned, irregular withdrawals, such as a large payout one month and nothing for several months, creates instability and burnout risk. Set a fixed owner pay percentage instead. ** [L1200, 2021-22]
- Staff budget formula and example. **

  ```
  Staff budget = real revenue × staff allocation percentage
  Remaining budget = staff budget − total current staff pay
  Example: 30% of 4,000 real revenue = 1,200 budget; a 1,000 customer service hire leaves 200.
  ```

  [L1334, 2021-22]
- Profit First planning steps. ** [L1334, 2021-22]
  1. Write down the long-term vision, such as target revenue, target profit, or a lump-sum exit amount.
  2. Work backwards from that target to a monthly revenue or profit goal.
  3. Enter current monthly income and the cost of goods sold percentage to calculate real revenue.
  4. Set the allocation percentages for profit, owner salary, taxes, staff and operating expenses, with a Profit First assessment, so they total 100% of real revenue.
  5. Calculate the monthly staff budget from the staff allocation.
  6. Enter the current team and total staff pay, then read the remaining budget.
  7. Enter the monthly cost of the next planned hire, and make the hire only when the remaining budget covers it.
  8. Enter the maximum revenue capacity the current team can handle and the target capacity the owner is aiming for.
  9. Each month, check remaining budget and capacity, then hire when the budget and team allow it.
  10. When the staff budget keeps building up without need, lower the staff allocation (the lesson used 20%) and move more into profit and owner salary.

### Owner pay, distributions and draws

- Pay yourself a fixed salary, for example 2,000 per month in two installments. Set monthly sales or profit goals that trigger a larger distribution when reached, such as an 8,000 bonus after 10,000 in profit, and keep the remaining profit in the business. ** [L0247, 2021]
- Owner pay is transferred at every allocation, while profit is distributed to the owner only once a quarter. Keeping profit in its account until the quarterly payout builds reserves before any profit is taken. ** [L1200, 2021-22]
- Early-stage profit distributions (2020 source): take distributions immediately after generating net profit instead of waiting for a set accounting period. Example: after several weeks of sales generate $5,000 net profit, take $3,000 as a distribution and reinvest $2,000 into growth. This is the older approach; the 2021-22 Profit First method above holds profit until the quarterly payout. ** [L0268, 2020]
- Owner withdrawals and profit distributions do not reduce the profit shown on the P&L. They appear on the balance sheet through equity and in the cash flow statement under financing, so an owner can take money out and still see a profit for that month. ** [L0484, 2021-22]
- Owner contributions increase equity, while owner draws reduce equity and show up as financing outflows in the cash flow statement. Keep these separate from operating expenses so that the profit and loss still shows true business performance. ** [L1046, 2021-22]
- Pay yourself first from the business. Know what your household costs per year, including children's education, food and clothing, and take that amount out as income. Reinvest the rest of the profit into the business, because taking out all profit leads to heavy taxes and slower growth. ** [L0960, 2019]
- Reinvestment amount:

  ```
  Reinvestment amount = annual business profit − annual personal living costs
  Example: 300,000 profit − 100,000 yearly living costs = about 200,000 reinvested
  ```

  ** [L0960, 2019]

### Business money versus personal money

- Couples or partners running a store together should set a separate budget for the business and keep savings for emergencies and family needs out of it. Plan the money before starting, not after the first sales come in. [L0832, 2019]
- Partners should openly discuss how much financial risk each is willing to take before spending money, because tolerance for risk often differs. Agree on spending limits for each risk level. [L0832, 2019]
- Track profit and expenses in dedicated business accounts so that the store's performance can be measured cleanly over time. ** [L1069, 2019]
- Do not run a skeleton-budget business that spends every dollar and leaves one dollar after taxes. Lawsuits require cash reserves for legal defense and settlement, so maintain financial buffers. [L0041, 2025-26]
- Create a budget and live within your means. Saving what you keep matters more than the amount you earn. Limit small recurring and impulse purchases, such as takeout, daily coffee or replacing equipment that still works, to build savings. [L1343, undated]
- For small accounts in a mail business, the difference between profit and loss depends more on how promptly payments are collected than on any other single factor. Set a clear timeline for following up on overdue accounts. [L1369, undated]

### Reinvestment and budgeting

- Money is a tool in business, not an emotional object. Spend it when it reliably generates more money. For example, if each dollar of ad spend returns 1.50 dollars, spending more on that channel is a way to reach more customers and grow sales. Limit spending only when returns do not justify it. [L1513, 2019]
- Some offers pay back immediately, while others take months to recover costs. Know the payback period before spending, fund a campaign according to how long the payback takes, and only take on the cash risk you can carry. [L1261, undated]
- With Brex, about 10,000 USD of Facebook ad spend returned roughly 100 to 300 USD in rewards. The reported rate varies with the card and the type of spending. [L1512, 2023]
- Set a minimum monthly savings amount (3,000 USD per month in the example, with better months saving up to 8,000 USD) and direct it into income-producing assets before discretionary spending. [L1515, 2019]

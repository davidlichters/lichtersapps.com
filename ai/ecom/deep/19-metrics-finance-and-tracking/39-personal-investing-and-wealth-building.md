### Personal investing and wealth building

**Context and order of priorities**

- US figures cited in the lesson: the average American has under $1,000 net worth, and about 85% of people have under $10,000 in net worth even when many earn $100,000 or more. Treat these as rough US-based context, not current statistics. *(Source undated.)* [L1126, 2018]
- Order for turning income into wealth:
  1. Cut monthly expenses as far as possible.
  2. Calculate your monthly living expenses and multiply by six.
  3. Keep that amount as an emergency fund in a liquid, no-fee online savings account.
  4. Once the emergency fund is full, send a fixed amount every month to investments instead of the savings account.

  [L1126, 2018]
- Keep an emergency fund of six months of living costs in liquid savings before investing. Without it, a forced sale of investments during a downturn or early withdrawal penalties can wipe out gains. [L1126, 2018]
- Store income is semi-passive because automation still needs oversight, while invested capital can grow without monitoring. Investment income is therefore the most truly passive income stream. [L1515, 2019]

**Compounding and entry methods**

- Compound growth of $1,000 invested every month at an assumed 8% annual return, compounded monthly (lesson calculator output, rounded). The growth comes from compounding, and each year's contribution is added on top of prior growth. Results change with the actual return, so re-run the numbers with a current calculator. [L1126, 2018]

  | Years | Total contributed | Approximate total value | Approximate interest earned |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 10 | $120,000 | $186,000 | $65,000 |
  | 20 | $240,000 | $600,000 | about $360,000 |
  | 30 | $360,000 | $1,500,000 | about $1,140,000 |

- Dollar-cost averaging: instead of investing a lump sum at once, invest a fixed amount on a regular schedule to reduce the risk of entering at a bad price. Lesson example: $100,000 invested as $10,000 per month over 10 months. [L1126, 2018]
- Check the arithmetic of any plan that spreads a lump sum over months. $5,000 per month for six months only covers $30,000, so a $60,000 sum at $5,000 per month takes 12 months. Work out the number of months from the total and the monthly amount before committing. [L1126, 2018]
- Modern online platforms let small monthly amounts (200 USD or 1,000 USD in the examples) enter assets that once required large sums, such as apartment buildings shared with many co-investors. A large starting capital is no longer required. [L1515, 2019]
- Real estate investing in income buildings no longer requires large capital. The lesson states that monthly contributions of $200 to $1,000 can be enough to start. The average expected return cited for such investments is 9-10% per year. [L0827, 2019]

**Index funds, fees and not timing the market**

- Do not try to time the market. Holding large amounts in cash while waiting for a dip can cost more than the dip would have saved, since the lesson describes missing a year with about a 22% market gain by staying in cash. [L1126, 2018]
- A low-cost index fund holds a small share of the whole publicly traded stock market. Vanguard is cited as one provider, and the strategy is to hold it long term rather than trade it. [L1126, 2018]
- Over long periods, most highly paid financial advisors, mutual fund managers and hedge fund managers do not beat the total stock market, and fees reduce returns further. Low-fee index investing is therefore the default option the lesson recommends. [L1126, 2018]
- Low-cost index funds that buy the whole stock market (Vanguard was named) avoid the 2-4% annual fees some financial advisors charge, and fees reduce long-term returns. [L1515, 2019]
- Selling during a downturn turns a paper loss into a real loss. If you own 100 shares of an index fund and the market falls 50%, you have lost nothing until you sell, so hold unless you need the money. [L1126, 2018]
- Investment platforms named as examples include the robo-advisor Betterment and the real estate investment platform Fundrise. Check fees, minimum deposits and regulation in your own country before using any platform. [L1126, 2018]

**Expected returns (sources differ)**

- Long-term expected return on diversified investments such as stocks, real estate or funds is cited as about 10% per year, while the compound interest example assumes 8%. Use a conservative rate when planning. [L1126, 2018]
- Real estate income buildings: 9-10% per year (see Compounding and entry methods). [L0827, 2019]
- PeerStreet, a property-backed loan platform, reported rates of about 7% to 8%. Loans carry default risk, and the lesson's claim of never losing money should not be treated as a guarantee. [L1126, 2018]
- Pure Street, secured hard-money loans: average return given as 7-9% per year, while some banks paid around 2% at the time. *(Source undated; bank rate is a historical comparison.)* [L1515, 2019]

**Risk limits and speculation**

- Limit speculative investments such as Bitcoin, cryptocurrency and individual stocks to 0% to 5% of total net worth. Never put more into speculation than that, and avoid anything risky you cannot afford to lose. [L1126, 2018]
- Speculative assets such as cryptocurrencies and newer platforms can lose the entire amount invested, and some were lost entirely when the platforms failed. Limit these holdings to an amount you can afford to lose completely. [L0863, 2022]

**Property and lending**

- Turnkey rental properties are often the most expensive house on the block, which raises the risk of losing value and tenants in a downturn. A larger apartment building of 40 or more units with a full-time property manager spreads that risk, and the lesson assumes about 95% occupancy even if a few tenants leave. [L1126, 2018]
- Property-backed loan platforms such as PeerStreet let investors lend against real estate. If a borrower defaults, the lender can take the collateral property and sell it to recover the money. Returns of about 7% to 8% were reported, but this is not guaranteed and depends on the loan and the quality of the collateral. [L1126, 2018]
- Unsecured peer-to-peer microloans can lose the full amount if the borrower stops paying, while loans secured by property let the lender recover funds by selling the property. Spreading a loan across many investors (about 1,000 USD each) further reduces risk. [L1515, 2019]
- Pure Street is a platform where many investors each fund a small slice of a secured hard-money loan backed by property. If the borrower defaults, the platform handles eviction and court steps and sends monthly progress updates. [L1515, 2019]

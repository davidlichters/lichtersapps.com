## Customer research and avatars

### Research before the store and the copy

- Understanding an audience means looking past demographics (age, gender, socioeconomic status) to behaviour and the underlying motivations, meaning the reasons why people act. This distinction separates effective advertisers from those who work by trial and error. [L0258, undated]
- Before branding, the store owner must know what the product is, what it does and how it benefits customers. The owner must also know who the customers are, how they behave, and what their needs, goals and motivations are. [L1044, undated]
- Understand the customer before designing the store. Review a customer Facebook group, the photos members post and what they watch online. This removes guesswork about layout, imagery and messaging. [L0709, 2017+]
- Before writing any copy, define the target market: the problems, desires and other facts about prospective customers. Copy works as a conversation with those people, so it only persuades when you know them well. [L0907, 2021-22]
- Keep the customer avatar sheet as a one-page reference that anyone can check before writing copy for a single product, email or ad. [L0907, 2021-22]
- Customers are defined as the group of people who experience the problem your product solves, so customer research must confirm that this group exists. Knowing the ideal customer in detail is required before you can communicate the problem and solution effectively. [L0872, 2021-22]
- Empathy and the ability to see your business from the customer's perspective are core marketing skills. The more you put yourself in the customer's position, the better the marketing becomes, especially for fields that must be estimated by intuition. [L0872, 2021-22]
- Customer research is not a one-time task. Review the customer research document at least once a year and update it whenever you learn something new, because the customer base can shift over time. [L0872, 2021-22]
- Completing the avatar profile before writing copy shortens the copywriting process. Treat the avatar as a living document and update it as you learn more about the market. [L0907, 2021-22]
- Audience research should cover four pillars:

  | Pillar | What it describes |
  |---|---|
  | Demographics | Who they are |
  | Psychographics | What they value |
  | Behaviours | How they act: research habits, loyalty, online communities |
  | Goals, needs and motivations | What they want and what belonging they seek |

  Use these pillars so that all brand messaging speaks to a defined group rather than everyone. [L1071, undated]
- Before filling in the audience section of a brand document, prepare two earlier research outputs: a detailed customer research spreadsheet and a shorter summary document. Base demographics, psychographics, behaviours and goals on these sources rather than on assumptions. [L1071, undated]
- For performance-oriented products, audience psychographics typically include a desire for quality and results, a strong interest in the activity the product supports, and appreciation of brand status and reputation. The brand image and product descriptions should reflect these values directly. [L1071, undated]
- When defining a brand's target audience, the overall group can be broad, spanning both genders, several age groups and low to high income levels. Name one focused core age range where the product category is most active, such as 15 to 45 for a sports brand, and let that core guide the messaging. [L1071, undated]
- Enter the research findings into the research form to sharpen the ICP and USP before any creative is produced. [L1019, 2026]

### The four layers: demographics, psychographics, behaviour and motivation

- The four segmentation components:

  | Component | What it covers | Examples |
  |---|---|---|
  | Demographics | Static attributes of a person | Age, gender, income level, education, marital status |
  | Psychographics | Personality, values, beliefs and attitudes | Openness, conscientiousness, level of organization, tendency to worry; values such as control or belonging; beliefs about the world, religion, opinions |
  | Behavior | What people do, their context and where the problem fits into their daily life or browsing situation | Using a product in a kitchen, scrolling a feed while queuing in a store |
  | Goals, needs and motivations | The underlying why behind what people do | Status, power, security, freedom, belonging |

  [L0993, undated]
- Demographics are the most surface-level layer and should be considered last when defining a target audience. They matter when they change whether the product fits. Example: a menstruation product, since people over 50 generally no longer menstruate, as the average age of stopping is above 50. [L0993, undated]
- Demographics alone often fail to explain buying. Buyers of one jewelry piece can range from 20 to 50 years old. The underlying need for status is usually a stronger basis for targeting than age or income. [L0993, undated]
- Psychographics cover how a person is wired and what they believe: personality traits, values such as control or belonging, beliefs about the world, and attitudes and opinions. Use them to choose the tone and angle of the message. [L0993, undated]
- For a viewer persona, needs and motivations matter more than demographics. Capture what the person desperately wants, what they fear not getting, what stands in their way and what outcome they want. Age, income and education serve only as background. [L1427, 2023+]
- Behavioural segmentation asks what the person is doing and in which context the problem appears. Place the product at the point in the daily routine where the problem or trigger occurs, not only where they browse. [L0993, undated]
- Example: a vegetable slicer's trigger is the moment someone cuts vegetables by hand, finds it tough and annoying, and gets an uneven cut. Describing the product through that exact moment makes the problem and solution immediately recognizable. [L0993, undated]
- Sharpen the buyer persona with concrete details such as reading habits, where they shop, income level, occupation and how often they buy. For example, a buyer who purchases four times a year as a gift for friends. These details guide ad and website messaging. [L1016, 2026]
- Premium beauty-tool example: the buyer invests in appearance and wellness, buys from Instagram and Facebook ads, and trusts beauty influencers and stylists. Describing the target customer and their psychology this concretely gives the audit enough to write relevant messaging. [L0886, 2025-26]
- The persona does not need every field completed. Fill in as much as you can to get a usable picture of the customer. Demographics are static facts, psychographics cover values and personality, behaviour covers what they do and the context of their problem, and motivations cover the deeper needs and feelings. Desire, feature and persona together are enough to start writing a decent headline. [L0516, 2023]

### The iceberg model

- Use the iceberg model to understand the customer in layers. Demographics (age, gender, location) are the visible surface, behaviour is what they do, the need is the reason behind the behaviour, and the motivation is the deep human driver. Marketing that reaches the deeper layers converts better and builds a sustainable brand. [L1426, undated; L0993, undated; L0998, undated]
- Goals, needs and motivations explain why people act. Demographics and behaviour are what you usually observe, while the reasons behind them make a message powerful. They usually drive purchases more strongly than demographics or behaviour do. [L0998, undated; L0993, undated]
- Think of an iceberg: demographics and behaviour form the visible tip, while needs, tensions, fears and motivations sit underneath. Your task is to uncover the layer below what people do. [L0998, undated]
- Motivation chain, from visible purchase to underlying need:
  - A commuter buys coffee and a bagel on the way to work. The visible behaviour is the purchase.
  - The need is that they have no time to make breakfast.
  - The fear is being late, which could damage their reputation at work or cost them a promotion.
  - The motivation is avoiding shame, losing control of their life and running short of money.

  Use this chain to move from the visible purchase to the underlying need and motive when building a buyer profile. [L0993, undated; L0998, undated; L0644, undated]
- Status can be a hidden buying driver even when convenience appears to be the reason. A buyer may choose a premium café because its setting makes them look successful while working, not only because it is close by. [L0998, undated]
- Coffee-chain example: people do not buy mainly for the coffee quality. They buy for a pleasant social environment and the status of looking successful while working with a laptop, and proximity is secondary. The deeper status and environment need keeps them coming back. [L0993, undated]
- Ask why the buyer really buys and how the product fits into their life. The surface action, such as buying a quick snack on the way to work, often hides a deeper motive like protecting their reputation or sense of control. [L0644, undated]
- Deeper motivations matter more than surface demographics. A buyer often wants to avoid a deeper consequence, such as missing work and losing a promotion, not just to remove a single pain. Writing copy about that underlying need speaks more intimately. [L0999, 2023]
- Ask why repeatedly to move from a visible behaviour to the need behind it. Keep asking until you reach a need such as security, status, freedom or belonging. [L0998, undated]
- Procedure for finding the real motive behind a purchase, asking why at each layer:
  1. What does the customer do?
  2. What need does that action meet?
  3. Why does that need matter to them, for example to protect their reputation or control at work?
  4. What deeper motivation sits underneath, such as identity, control, belonging or status?

  [L1426, undated]
- Procedure for working the iceberg with an audience:
  1. Record the surface facts about the audience: demographics and observable behaviour.
  2. Ask why they do this behaviour and identify the need it serves.
  3. Ask what they fear will happen if the need is not met.
  4. Ask what deeper motivation drives them, such as control, status, security or growth.
  5. Use the deepest layer to shape the message, without necessarily stating every motive openly in the ad.

  [L0993, undated]
- The deepest motives do not always need to appear in ads, but you must understand the audience closely enough to speak to them at that level. Ads that touch the motive often outperform ads that only describe the behaviour. Knowing these motives is what makes a message feel relevant. [L0993, undated; L0998, undated]

### Human drivers and motivations

- Nine core human drivers motivate purchasing: power and control over outcomes, freedom from pain, conserving or gaining resources (time, effort, money, physical), status and superiority, connection and belonging, sexual selection, social conformity (keeping up with peers), personal growth, and providing for loved ones. Map the product to one or more of these drivers. [L0258, undated]
- Base human needs to check against an audience include: power, control over outcomes, freedom from pain, gaining or conserving resources (time, energy, money), status and superiority, sexual selection, survival, connection, love and belonging, keeping up with peers and not falling behind, growth, and caring for loved ones or having a purpose bigger than oneself. Each need is felt to a different degree depending on the person. [L0993, undated; L0998, undated; L0997, undated]
- Strong messages tie a product to a fundamental human need instead of just describing its function. Common drivers include power, control over outcomes, freedom from pain, and gaining or conserving resources such as time, energy and money. [L0997, undated]
- These needs are present in every audience but weighted differently depending on the person. Other core needs include status and superiority, connection, love and belonging, keeping up with peers, growth, looking after loved ones, and having a purpose bigger than oneself. [L0997, undated; L0998, undated]
- Buyer motivations often sit in opposing pairs, such as control versus enjoyment on the personal side and power versus belonging on the social side. Decide which pair the product speaks to before writing the message. [L0644, undated]
- Opposing motivations create tension that good marketing can address: control versus enjoyment, power versus belonging. Identify which side a product serves and speak to that need in the copy. [L1426, undated]
- Map both directions for each segment. Buyers move toward a goal they want or away from a pain they want to escape. Ads and offers work better when they name the specific pain to remove or the specific goal to reach. [L0497, undated]
- Negative emotions such as fear, shame, guilt, anger, helplessness and grief narrow attention onto one point, which makes them useful for hooks and problem agitation. Positive emotions such as hope, relief, pride, belonging and love broaden attention, which makes them useful for presenting the solution and the transformation. [L0613, 2026]

### Status, identity and social comparison

- People compare themselves to others and feel envy toward those who have something they lack, while they scorn those who lack something they already have. Brands can use this status comparison dynamic to position a product as the visible sign of being ahead of others. [L0497, undated]
- Status and belonging are powerful because standing within a group affects access to resources and opportunities. Today that means career progression, social acceptance and keeping pace with peers rather than physical survival. [L0997, undated]
- Status, attractiveness and social standing are sensitive needs. Blunt claims about them can backfire, so phrase them carefully and indirectly rather than shaming the viewer. The need should be understood without being stated in a crude way. [L0993, undated; L0998, undated; L0997, undated]
- Status, comparison and social claims should be articulated in a way the customer can accept about themselves, because blunt wording can insult or alienate the audience. [L0997, undated]
- Desires can include social status, such as wanting others to see the buyer as cool. Status is a strong angle for copy and should be recorded next to practical desires. [L0907, 2021-22]
- Stopping at the product as the only desire misses the status, identity and pain angles that drive buying. Always list what the customer wants beyond the product. [L0907, 2021-22]
- Record the identity the customer wants, meaning who they want to be. For example, a parent may want to be a reliable parent, and a host may want to be a relaxed host. [L0907, 2021-22]
- A customer who wants to be seen as successful by friends may buy products to project that identity. A parent buying a new bed for a child may want to be seen as providing the right things and being a good parent. [L0872, 2021-22]
- Complete the motivation fields by asking what the customer wants to gain, what they want to be, what they want to do, and what they want to avoid. The avoid field captures the negative experiences the problem causes, which the purchase helps the customer escape. [L0872, 2021-22]

### Customer avatars: worksheet and fields

- Define the ideal customer avatar with a worksheet before setting up the store's structure:
  1. Open a customer avatar worksheet built for your niche.
  2. Fill in every field for the person who should land on the site and buy.
  3. Use the answers to decide which visitors to attract and how to convert each of them into a buyer.

  [L0355, 2020-26]
- Define one dream customer with a name, age, relationship status and income level, and choose products that fit her daily life and needs. Check every new product against this customer profile before testing. [L0388, 2024+]
- Example: a dream customer described as a 40-year-old single woman with money maps to different products by life situation: home decor for a home-improvement-minded person, stretch bands for someone who goes to the gym, and a relaxed hoodie for evenings at home. [L0388, 2024+]
- Segment customers by use case or buyer type (avatars) rather than by job title, because the best customers may come from very different professions. In the example, the top customers spent over $1,000 per year and included people from very different fields, so avatars built on use cases were more useful for targeting. Build a bundle or promotion for each main avatar. [L0334, 2017]
- A business with one customer type may fill in only one avatar column. Businesses with different groups of buyers, such as B2B and B2C or customers of different genders, should keep a separate avatar for each because their needs can differ greatly. [L0872, 2021-22]
- The first pass at the avatar document should contain only what you already reasonably know. Accuracy is not required at this stage, because the document is a living resource that grows with each review. [L0872, 2021-22]
- Give each customer avatar a name, because team members and agencies can then refer to it quickly, for example when briefing an ad for a specific avatar. The name can be anything. [L0872, 2021-22; L0907, 2021-22]
- The customer avatar document is not confidential. Share it with anyone who creates work for you or interacts with your customers. [L0872, 2021-22]
- A one-page customer research document can be handed to agencies, freelancers, consultants, and new employees or customer service staff so they understand who the customers are without lengthy briefings. Agencies, especially traffic agencies, will ask about target market, customer attributes, and what customers look like. [L0872, 2021-22]
- Customer avatar tool: a spreadsheet, either a Numbers or Excel file on Apple or Windows, or a Google Sheets copy made in your own Google Drive that you can share with a team. Spreadsheets are easier to customize and expand than PDFs, whose formatting is hard to manage. [L0907, 2021-22]
- Avatar sheet fields (three avatar slots, one column each):

  | Field | What to record |
  |---|---|
  | Customer Avatar 1 / 2 / 3 | One column per avatar; the sheet has three avatar slots |
  | Avatar Name | Any memorable name, used as shorthand in team discussions |
  | Age Range | Basic demographic data |
  | Gender | Basic demographic data |
  | Average Income | Basic demographic data |
  | Experts Followed | Bloggers, YouTubers, authors, TV personalities, athletes and influencers the customer follows |
  | Favorite Online Spaces | Facebook groups, online forums, Reddit communities, Quora, fan pages |
  | Prior Relevant Purchases | Other versions of your product, supporting products, accessories, and products bought alongside yours |
  | Primary Problem(s) | The main problems the avatar wants to solve by buying your product; one or several |
  | Wants / Desires | Desires that follow from the primary problems and relate to your product |
  | Emotions Currently Feeling | How the customer feels now about the problem, e.g. frustrated, sad, excited |
  | Beliefs | What they believe will happen if they buy, what they believe about your product, and what they believe about themselves |
  | What do they want to gain? | Outcomes beyond the physical product, such as pleasure or status |
  | What do they want to be? | The identity they aim for |
  | What do they want to do? | The actions they want to take, as specific as possible |
  | What do they want to avoid (pain)? | The pains they are trying to escape | [L0907, 2021-22]
- Start the profile with basic demographics (age range, gender and average income), then move to the deeper fields such as problems, desires, beliefs and emotions. The basic demographic fields to record are age range, gender and average income target. Record gender separately for each avatar if customer groups differ in ways that matter. [L0907, 2021-22; L0872, 2021-22]
- Procedure for building or checking avatars:
  1. Check whether you already have customer profiles. If they are current, review them for accuracy and stop there; if not, fill in the full sheet.
  2. Decide how many avatars you need. One avatar is enough if one product solves one problem for one type of buyer; otherwise make one per distinct buyer type, such as B2B and retail, or different experience levels within retail.
  3. Give each avatar a short, memorable name.
  4. Record age range, gender and average income.
  5. List the experts, bloggers, YouTubers, authors, TV personalities, athletes or influencers this avatar follows.
  6. List prior relevant purchases: other product versions, supporting products, accessories and products bought alongside yours.
  7. List favourite online spaces: Facebook groups, forums, Reddit communities, Quora and fan pages.
  8. Brainstorm the primary problems the avatar wants solved by your product; several are fine.
  9. Turn each problem into specific wants and desires that relate to your product.
  10. Record the emotions the avatar feels right now.
  11. Record beliefs about what happens after buying, about the product, and about themselves.
  12. Record what they want to gain beyond the physical product.
  13. Record who they want to be.
  14. Record what they want to do, as specifically as possible.
  15. Record what they want to avoid (pain).
  16. Research anything you do not know, and keep the sheet updated as your knowledge grows.

  [L0907, 2021-22]
- Procedure for customer research:
  1. Start with your own knowledge and experience from serving customers, and the signals customers have already given you.
  2. Open the customer avatar spreadsheet and fill in every field you already know or can reasonably estimate, without aiming for perfect answers.
  3. Give each avatar a name so it can be referenced quickly in team and agency work.
  4. Use Google to research the fields you do not know yet, such as experts, books, magazines, movies and TV, and online spaces.
  5. Fill in the remaining fields: prior purchases, primary problems, wants, emotions, beliefs, and the gain, be, do and avoid questions.
  6. Carry out the exercise within the current month, then keep updating it as you learn more.

  [L0872, 2021-22]
- Record prior relevant purchases, such as other product versions, supporting products and accessories. If your product is an accessory, note which main products your customers already own, for example which phone models buyers of a phone case use. [L0907, 2021-22; L0872, 2021-22]
- Fields you cannot answer yet can be left empty and completed later. For passion-based niches, find relevant books and magazines by searching Google for titles about that passion. [L0872, 2021-22]
- A half-hearted profile leads to half-hearted marketing and weak results. Treat the exercise as serious research and look up anything you do not know instead of guessing. [L0907, 2021-22]
- Describing the customer as a broad group such as 'everyone interested in the activity' is the lazy approach. Dig into sub-groups, since their knowledge, needs and problems usually differ. [L0907, 2021-22]

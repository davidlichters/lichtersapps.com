## Brand voice and messaging

### Choose the brand voice before writing

- Pick adjectives that describe how the brand should be perceived, such as trustworthy, reliable, powerful, friendly, inspirational, bold or empowering, and use the same tone in all copy, social posts and comment replies. [L1044, undated; L1071, undated]
- Decide how the brand should be perceived (for example trustworthy and reliable, powerful, or friendly) and make every message match those choices. [L1071, undated]
- Choose the tone before launch to fit the niche: empathetic, empowering or sweet for a parenting store, or cool and slightly cheeky for a younger audience. Refine it later as real customer responses show what resonates. [L1044, undated]
- Brand messaging, including the tagline, copy style and product descriptions, comes from the brand personality and tone. Product descriptions emphasise the benefits and features that support the brand's reason for existing. [L1044, undated]
- The customer is the hero of the story and the brand is the guide that helps them reach their goal. If the owner does not feel this way, the store must at least appear caring and focused on the customer. [L1044, undated]
- Speak to the audience like a friend, not like a brand speaking to a consumer. Corporate jargon and glossy, over-polished messaging are quickly seen through, especially by younger audiences who use TikTok. [L0576, 2025-26]
- All website copy should read as a conversation with a friend, aimed at connecting with customers. Keep the tone conversational but avoid slang and colloquial expressions. [L1467, undated]
- Use a personal, first-person voice (I, you, me): the copy should read as one person speaking directly to one reader. A byline with a real person's name strengthens this, and the writer's honesty and personality should show through. [L1366, undated]
- Use the wording the target customer uses and relates to, so the reader feels the writer speaks their language. [L1366, undated]
- Write business-to-customer copy as if speaking to one person standing in front of you, like a good salesperson in a physical store, in a conversational or fun tone if that fits the brand. Business-to-business copy can be more formal. [L0396, 2021-22]
- Use emojis in announcement bars and email subject lines only when the brand is playful and the audience expects that tone. Keep professional stores free of emojis. [L0853, 2021]
- Do not use the word *dropshipping* in consumer-facing brand copy. [L0989, 2025-26]
- Be careful with the word *luxury* in headlines. A quieter premium tone can present price and quality clearly without making the first impression feel too upfront. [L1016, 2026]
- A premium brand can keep its brand equity and still test sales-focused creatives: run distinctive on-brand creatives, such as a signature sound, ASMR-style audio or a recognisable visual style, alongside the direct-response ads. [L1016, 2026]
- Copywriting is the core skill for selling digital products: the right words name the customer's problem and create the need to buy. Strong copy also improves results for physical dropshipping, shapes brand image, signals company values, and balances information with relatability. [L0513, undated]

### Rewrite existing copy in brand voice

- Rewriting website copy to match the brand voice multiplies the impact of a visual rebrand. Without changing the site structure, updating headlines and body text to match brand personality makes the site feel more authentic, human and trustworthy. [L0317, 2025-26]
- Generate on-brand headings with this prompt, then pick from the several options ChatGPT returns. [L0317, 2025-26]

```

```

```

```

[L0317, 2025-26]

- Small punctuation and word-order changes shift perceived tone. Changing *Wash, wear and repeat* to *Wash. Wear. Repeat.* creates a punchier, more confident, more authentic tone. [L0317, 2025-26]
- Before generating a voice file, collect 2-3 email or product description examples from the brand. They teach the AI the real tone, language patterns and style the brand uses. [L0322, 2025-26]
- Validate the extracted voice with Apify: download the brand's Instagram feed and analyse caption language, product descriptions and engagement tone. Compare the result with Brand Fetch's voice analysis to confirm the actual voice matches the strategic definition. [L0318, 2025-26]
- Supplier feature lists describe what a product has, but buyers respond to what the feature does for them. Turn each feature into a benefit that explains why it matters, for example *stable* becomes *perfect for beginners*. [L1130, 2020-26]
- Feature-to-benefit examples: *inflatable design* becomes *easy storage and transportation*, and *durable construction* becomes a reason why durability matters to the buyer. Add a zoomed-in image of key construction details next to the text. [L1130, 2020-26]
- Copying supplier descriptions word for word is a quick start but leaves the page weak. Rewrite features as benefits and add images and examples as early as possible. [L1130, 2020-26]
- Product descriptions must be written for the customer, not copied from the manufacturer. Manufacturer copy is technical and generic, and visitors leave a product page when the text is unclear or identical to competitors. [L1293, 2014]
- Check how top competitors name a product before changing your title format. If they do not use a hyphen or lead with the brand name, consider removing the hyphen and the brand from the title. [L0963, 2022]
- Supplier product information supplies most of the store's content. Write a direct-response style store and shape the copy around what buyers need to read in order to buy, even if you are not the product expert. [L0663, 2019]
- Use a clear brand story framework, such as the StoryBrand approach by Donald Miller, to guide content ideas and messaging. Tactic-specific social media books date quickly, so prefer strategy-level books. [L0664, 2020]

### Product naming

- Name products after the dream outcome, not only the item. *Stylish Africa map necklace* sells appearance and self-expression, while *Africa map necklace* only describes the item. Benefit-focused naming directly increases perceived value. [L0233, undated]
- Use the words of the dream outcome in product names, such as *stylish africa map necklace* instead of *africa necklace*, so the value is clear at first glance. [L0502, 2020+]
- Name the product after one clear benefit. Mixing two benefits in one name, such as sleep improvement and compression, confuses buyers about what they are getting. [L1194, undated]
- A name should communicate a concrete benefit within a defined timeframe. A clear timeframe and outcome make a name more attractive than a technical or vague description, and the same product can sell better under a benefit-led name. [L0683, undated]
- Benefit-led naming pattern:

  **[Specific result] + [defined timeframe] + [product type]**

  The timeframe makes the outcome measurable, and the result tells the customer what they gain. Test several names against each other and keep the best performer. [L0683, undated]

- Name examples:

  | Name | Assessment | Reason |
  |---|---|---|
  | *Float Tank Center Session* | Weak | Describes the service but gives no outcome [L0683, undated] |
  | *Free 6 Week Stress Release Challenge* | Stronger | States a time interval and the result customers get after it [L0683, undated] |
  | *48 Hour Neck Ease Massager* | Pattern example for a neck massager | Names a specific result and a defined period, which makes the promise concrete [L0683, undated] |
  | *Sleep Through the Night Pillow in 7 Days* | Pattern example | Specific result within a defined period [L0683, undated] |
  | *Back Pain Relief Stretch Kit in 14 Days* | Pattern example | Specific result within a defined period [L0683, undated] |

- Use this prompt in AI tools to generate product names and a description:

```
Use the Magic Headline Formula to write 10 product name options for [PRODUCT]. Each name should state a specific result and a defined timeframe in which the customer gets it. Then write a product description for the best option in the same formula, aimed at [TARGET CUSTOMER] who has [PROBLEM]. Keep the language plain and concrete.
```

[L0683, undated]

- A name can carry the whole concept when it describes the buyer's situation or payoff in a few words. A name that expresses a use or a feeling does part of the selling before the copy starts. [L1366, undated]
- Name variants with a story or setting rather than plain descriptors, so the product sells a feeling and a picture of how the buyer will feel. A plain scent name becomes a named outdoor or evocative variant with a short descriptive label. [L0524, 2023]
- Use plain, descriptive words that state what the item actually is, instead of clever brand names, for products and funnel stages. Buyers scan for their exact problem, so a precise name often converts better than a creative one. [L1016, 2026]
- Where the two naming approaches differ: concept or story names fit products sold on a feeling or payoff [L1366, undated; L0524, 2023], while plain names fit products and funnel stages where buyers scan for the exact problem they have [L1016, 2026].
- A unique product name is less competitive in Google search than a generic product name, so buyers can find the product again by searching for it. Use a distinctive name even when the market uses generic terms. Custom wording can also be less competitive than generic supplier product titles. [L1209, undated; L1210, undated]

### Product descriptions

- Short, simple and attractive descriptions often convert better than long technical ones because they intrigue the buyer more. [L0513, undated]
- Feature-only copy can work in a crowded market when the feature proves superior quality or performance against competitors. The copy has to show why the product is better than alternatives, not just list specifications. [L0919, undated]
- Emphasise, in order of clarity: the features, the benefits for the customer, and the technology or method behind the product. It works because customers buy outcomes, and the technology explains why the product delivers them. [L1071, undated]
  - Example: a water bottle description names its insulation feature, what it means for daily use, and how it keeps drinks cold for hours. [L1071, undated]
  - Example: a kitchen knife description names its steel, the cutting benefit, and the sharpening method. [L1071, undated]
- Turn a feature into a benefit by naming the good quality and the drawback it avoids, in plain words rather than technical terms. Examples: *Easy movement without the drag* and *Keep-you-moving support, nothing stiff about it*. [L0884, 2025-26]
- Explain the materials and why they cause comfort or recovery, so the buyer understands a higher price. Buyers pay more when they can see the reason for the value. [L1194, undated]
- Every product is two products: the physical product (materials and construction) and the functional product (what it does for the buyer). Buyers pay for the functional product; the physical product matters because it makes the functional product possible. [L0330, undated]
- Use physical features in copy in four ways:
  1. To justify the price.
  2. To document the quality of the performance.
  3. To assure the performance will last over years.
  4. To sharpen the buyer's mental picture of the performance.

  The most important use is to give a new claim fresh believability, by introducing a new mechanism, quality or freedom from an old limitation that others lack. [L0330, undated]
- For high page-count books, state the page count in the short product description next to the bundle name and price. The page count justifies a higher price to the buyer. [L0354, 2026]
- Choosing the description length:

  | Situation | Approach | Source |
  |---|---|---|
  | General case | Short, simple, attractive; often converts better | [L0513, undated] |
  | Crowded market, feature is measurably superior | Prove the feature against alternatives | [L0919, undated] |
  | Technology or method explains the result | Features, then benefits, then the method | [L1071, undated] |
  | Product needs education | Up to three or four lines of copy | [L1345, 2021+] |

### Headlines, subheadlines and taglines

- Two-part tagline: an action followed by a reassuring outcome, kept to four words or fewer. The contrast gives the reader a feeling and a reason in one line, which makes it memorable and easy to repeat. Examples: *Train hard, recover faster.* and *Dig deep, sleep well.* [L0524, 2023]
- Product page headline pattern: name a hidden root cause, promise one simple daily action that addresses it, then list three to four outcomes the buyer feels. It gives the visitor a reason the problem persists and a single easy step to fix it. [L1164, 2026]
  - Example: *Cuts the hidden late-day slump that drains your focus, so afternoons feel sharp with one stick pack a day.* [L1164, 2026]
  - Example: *Eases the hidden tension that builds up at a desk, so you can work comfortably with one daily routine.* [L1164, 2026]
- Subheadline formula: product name, then a one-line subheadline that names the exact problem the buyer has and the result the product delivers. Mobile visitors decide within seconds whether a page is meant for them, and a specific pain point signals that it is. [L1260, undated]
  - Example: *Sore feet after a 10-hour shift? Cushioned insoles built for all-day standing.* [L1260, undated]
  - Example: *Winter-dry skin that cracks by Tuesday? A rich hand cream for daily use.* [L1260, undated]
- Semi-headline pattern: a one-line, benefit-driven line placed directly under the product name, naming the exact pain point of the target customer and what the product does about it. It tells a visitor who has just landed what the product is. [L1345, 2021+]
  - Example: *Tired of cold feet on winter mornings? Heated insoles that warm up in 30 seconds.* [L1345, 2021+]
  - Example: *Losing sleep to snoring? A chin strap that keeps your airway open all night.* [L1345, 2021+]
- Copy structure: one benefit-driven headline line, then a paragraph of no more than two lines. Long paragraphs make visitors leave, so only complicated products that need education can use three or four lines. [L1345, 2021+]
- Promise line under the product name, with the result, the time it takes and the guarantee in one short line. It states the value, sets the buyer's expectation and removes risk. Use this structure for any problem-solving product. [L0871, 2021+]
  - Example: *Relieve back tension in 10 minutes a day or your money back.* [L0871, 2021+]
  - Example: *Sleep through the night with less neck pain in 7 days, or get a full refund.* [L0871, 2021+]
- Opening line formula for a subject line or first line: **[Activity or task] has never been this easy** (or *has never been easier*). It makes the reader curious about how much effort is saved and speaks to the use case rather than pushing a sale. [L0605, undated]
  - Example for a fitness product: *Your evening workout has never been this easy.* [L0605, undated]
  - Example for a kitchen product: *Breakfast prep has never been this quick.* [L0605, undated]
- Welcome email subject lines should combine a benefit of joining with an offer, and use curiosity-driven lines that speak to the use case. [L0605, undated]

### Problem, agitate, solution and differentiation

- Problem-agitate-solution pattern:
  1. State the customer's problem.
  2. Make the pain of that problem vivid.
  3. Present the product as the solution.

  [L0552, 2023]
  - Example: *Tired of cold coffee by the time you reach your desk? Our insulated mug keeps drinks hot for hours, so every sip is still warm.* [L0552, 2023]
  - Example: *Sick of tangled cables on your desk? The velcro cable organiser keeps every charger in place, so your workspace looks clean in seconds.* [L0552, 2023]
- Escalate everyday pain with concrete, sensory scenes, such as hair found in breakfast, in coffee or in the mouth. Specific, vivid moments make the problem feel personal and push the customer toward a solution. [L0450, 2022+]
- The satisfying feel of the action, which buyers mention in reviews, is an emotional benefit. Use it in copy alongside the functional benefit. Describing how the product feels to use can differentiate it from competitors who only list functions. [L0450, 2022+]
- For a contractor or DIY audience, name the pain points directly: planning takes too long, generic guides skip real steps, and sourcing materials decides success. Then promise verified steps, specific sourcing and a faster start. [L0794, 2026]
- Before writing direct-response landing page copy, list every problem the customer goes through, including the tension and the emotional motivation behind each one. For example, a dirty house matters because it feels unhygienic and embarrassing. [L0917, undated]
- Differentiation pattern: when a product competes with similar tools, lead with the unique feature and the measurable benefit, not the generic job the product does.
  - Example: *Our kitchen knife stays sharp for 3 times longer than standard steel blades, so you cut less and spend less time sharpening.* [L0552, 2023]
  - Example: *Our running shoe weighs 180 grams, half the weight of typical trainers, so every stride feels lighter.* [L0552, 2023]

### Concept-led selling and emotional journeys

- Build every sales letter around one core belief with three parts: a new opportunity, the reader's desire it unlocks, and a new mechanism that only you can provide. Write the belief down before drafting, because it becomes the mission and common thread that keeps all copy focused. [L1365, 2019+]

  | Part | Role in the belief |
  |---|---|
  | New opportunity | The thing that is new for the reader |
  | Desire | What the reader wants it to unlock |
  | New mechanism | The way only you can deliver it |

  [L1365, 2019+]

- Belief formula:

  ```
  One belief = [New opportunity] is the key to [prospect's desire] and it's attainable only through [new mechanism].
  ```

  Example: *Fixing evening light exposure is the key to falling asleep faster and waking rested, and it's attainable only through our timed light-and-temperature routine.* [L1365, 2019+]

- The desire must be tied to a benefit that lets the prospect feel more significant, respected or valued, improves their mental, physical, social, emotional, sexual or financial well-being, or reduces the risk of events that would harm these areas. A new opportunity only works as the key to a desire when it delivers one of these benefits. [L1365, 2019+]
- Typical desires by niche:
  - Health: losing weight, lowering blood pressure, building muscle.
  - Business opportunity: working from home, quitting a job.
  - Financial: a secure retirement, taking care of family. [L1365, 2019+]
- Emotional journey positioning, in six steps:
  1. State what the product is not, to challenge market assumptions.
  2. State what it is, as an emotional metaphor or transformation.
  3. Describe the journey it enables between emotional states.
  4. Name it after the emotional state it creates, not its function.
  5. Connect that journey to how the user aspires to move through their own life.
  6. End with the emotional destination the buyer will reach.

  It works because it bypasses rational objection and engages the emotional core of all purchase decisions. [L0196, 2020+]
  - Example for an email management tool: *This isn't another inbox. It's your return to focus. It carries you from buried to sovereign. We call it Clarity. It moves the way leaders move, with intention. A place where your attention belongs to you again.* [L0196, 2020+]
  - Example for a sleep tracking app: *This isn't just data. It's your body talking back. It journeys with you from exhausted to restored. We call it Rhythm. It guides the way wellness guides, with rhythm. A place where you remember what rest feels like.* [L0196, 2020+]
- Customers buy the outcome they want, and the product is only the vehicle. Ask buyers what result they want, such as less stress, better behaviour, more confidence or better communication, instead of asking only what they think of the product. This outcome language is what sells. [L0906, 2026]
- People buy emotions, identity and vibes, not products. The sales message should make the buyer feel comfort, coziness or belonging. A product that fits a mood sells faster than one described by features. [L0347, 2026]
- Copywriting should communicate how the product improves the customer's life and trigger emotion, not only list features such as quality or shipping time. Emotional, benefit-driven language helps customers connect with the product and lifts add-to-cart and checkout rates. [L0773, 2023]
- A useful copywriting framework from *100 Million Offers* by Alex Hormozi: show the customer's problem, the perceived likelihood of success, and the value delivered, including how quickly and with how little effort the customer reaches the goal. [L0773, 2023]
- Products sell more easily when they create an emotional connection or trigger a reaction in the buyer. A random product with no story or meaning is harder to sell. [L1219, undated]
- Not every product needs an emotional angle. If the product has no emotional meaning, put the emphasis on the specific problem it solves and how it solves it. [L1219, undated]
- Feature-led copy and emotional copy meet in one rule: the product is only worth the emotion if the copy proves the result. [L0906, 2026; L1219, undated]
- Give a gift angle, such as a perfect holiday gift, to give buyers a second reason to purchase. It works best with products that suit the holiday season. [L1194, undated]

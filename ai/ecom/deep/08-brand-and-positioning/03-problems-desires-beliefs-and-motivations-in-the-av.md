### Problems, desires, beliefs and motivations in the avatar

- Primary problems are the problems the customer experiences that the product or business solves, and there can be several per avatar. Note every problem you can identify, even if the list grows over time. [L0872, 2021-22]
- Define primary problems as the main problems the product helps solve. There may be one or several, and there is no single right answer. [L0907, 2021-22]
- Ask why the customer wants the product to find the deeper problem behind it. A person who wants a rowing machine may really want to feel fitter after a stressful year, and that underlying problem drives the purchase more than the item itself. [L0872, 2021-22]
- Wants and needs are different. A customer can want a product without needing it, and the want usually comes from an underlying problem such as a status concern. Record both the want and the problem behind it. [L0872, 2021-22]
- Turn each problem into a desire tied to how the product is used, not just the product itself. For example, a first-time home cook's problem is making a meal quickly, so the desire is a meal kit that matches their skill level. [L0907, 2021-22]
- Record beliefs in three areas: what the customer thinks will happen if they buy, what they believe about the product, and what they believe about themselves. [L0907, 2021-22]
- Customers buy for more than the physical product, so list what they want to gain, such as the pleasure of use or social status. Each gain should connect back to their original desires. [L0907, 2021-22]
- Record what the customer wants to do, and make it as specific as possible. A precise action, such as mastering one particular technique, gives sharper copy than a vague goal. [L0907, 2021-22]
- Core problem excavation, for each avatar:
  1. Surface problem: how people describe it publicly (at least 10 quotes).
  2. Real problem: what they think at 2 a.m. and never say aloud (at least 15 quotes).
  3. Identity problem: the negative labels and self-concept the problem created (at least 10 quotes).
  4. Relational problem: strain on partners, family, friends, and avoided social situations (at least 10 quotes).
  5. Trajectory problem: what they fear becoming and the worst case they imagine (at least 10 quotes).

  [L0613, 2026]
- Target avatar research inputs to request before starting:
  1. The brand website or key product information.
  2. Facebook ad account data: gender split with spend and performance by gender, performance by age cohort, time-of-day and day-of-week spend patterns, top-performing creative themes, and geographic concentration if relevant.

### Desires, emotions and distress

- A persona is the same as an avatar or an ideal customer profile (ICP). The most attributed persona in a competitor's data is the audience its ads target most, so study that persona first. [L0374, 2026]
- A desire is the result the customer wants, such as fading acne scars, even skin tone or smoother skin texture. Desires work better when an emotion is attached to them. [L0374, 2026]
- Emotions attached to a desire make the message more effective. For an acne product, love or attraction can be the emotion: the acne stops a person from being seen as a partner, and the product is the fix. Broad, non-targeted love messaging can also be strong for acne products. [L0374, 2026]
- Top emotions in a competitor's data, such as hope and relief, are a data-backed signal of what to target. Use the emotion with the most attributed ads as the starting point for your own messaging. [L0374, 2026]
- Distress segments:

  | Segment | Intensity range | Baseline state | Attention | Message receptivity |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Low (curious or preventive) | 1-4 | Mild curiosity, optimization focus | Broad, can process positive and negative cues | Responds to aspirational and lifestyle messaging |
  | Moderate (problem-aware) | 5-7 | Frustration, concern, mixed hope and skepticism | Beginning to narrow | Needs problem acknowledgment first; wants proof; distrusts overpromising |
  | High (desperate) | 8-10 | Chronic shame, fear, desperation, anger, helplessness | Severely narrowed | Weak to positive cues; needs extensive validation; responds to 'finally, someone who understands' |

  [L0613, 2026]
- Label-emotion map, one entry for each of at least 10 significant labels:
  - Label.
  - Primary emotion.
  - Secondary emotions.
  - Attention state while the label is active.
  - Behavioural consequences (what they do or avoid).
  - Which messages can reach them in that state.
  - The aspirational label that could replace it.

  Also map the transition path from each of the top 5 negative labels: trigger, required proof, and typical duration of the change. [L0613, 2026]

### Interviews and surveys

- In the early stage of a new product, run customer interviews yourself instead of delegating them, even when your time is expensive. Raw answers in the customer's own words are the source for headlines and copy, and a summary removes the phrasing you need. [L0378, 2019+]
- Before any content work, build a customer profile with a questionnaire of about 15 questions covering at least these areas:
  1. Demographics: age, sex, race.
  2. What a typical day in the customer's life looks like.
  3. Their biggest pain points.
  4. What they value most.
  5. Their goals.
  6. The shopping experience they expect.
  7. Which magazines and blogs they read.

  Use the answers for every article, email and image, and phrase content in the customer's own language. [L0380, 2014]
- Survey procedure for current and future customers:
  1. Ask current or future customers what they have at the moment and how they use it.
  2. Ask how your product would help them and what they would need to know before buying.
  3. Run the questions as a survey funnel, so a prospect answers a few questions before getting the offer.
  4. Segment the respondents by their answers (for example colour, resistance level, whether they train with a partner).
  5. Use these segments later: a new product or variant goes only to people who already said they want that feature.

  [L0643, 2015]
- The 'Ask' approach (book 'Ask' by Ryan Levesque) is about asking customers directly instead of guessing what they want. Perceived value matters more than the number of added extras: a message that addresses the buyer's specific problem makes the same offer feel more valuable. [L0643, 2015]
- Survey template question: "What led you to make a purchase with us?" [L0757, 2022]
- Customer research for offers:
  1. Survey new customers with the questions in the survey template.
  2. Read the answers and note what the buyer cared about most when choosing.
  3. Decide what your specific customer would value the most.
  4. Write down your own store ideas as you work through the offer steps, so the list is ready when you need it.

  [L0757, 2022]
- Use the following prompt, unchanged, to pull core mass desire, benefits, pain points, proof and transformation stories from a product description and customer reviews:

  ```
  Act as a senior direct-response researcher who receives only two inputs: (1) a product description covering technical details, features and specifications, and (2) customer reviews containing genuine feedback and testimonials. Working exclusively from these two inputs, pull out the core mass desire, the main benefits and features, the customer pain points, the proof points and testimonials, and the transformation stories.

  Core mass desire:
  1. Study how customers phrase things. Spot emotional themes that recur across the reviews and map the before and after states they describe.
  2. From the feedback, answer: What is the deepest emotional driver? Which transformation appears again and again in the reviews? What do customers say they can now do or feel after using the product? Which pain or frustration are they most relieved to be rid of?
  3. Phrase the core mass desire in one of these two formats: To [emotional benefit] without [pain point], or To finally [desired outcome] despite [previous obstacle].

  Required inputs:
  Product description: [insert the complete product description]
  Customer reviews: [insert 3-5 detailed customer reviews]

  Do not make up any facts that are absent from these inputs.
  ```

### Identity markers and negative labels

- Identity marker research for identity-based products, in order:
  1. Document at least 10 common values of the identity group, with examples.
  2. List visual markers (clothing, accessories, aesthetics).
  3. Collect at least 20 group-specific phrases or terms.
  4. List at least 15-20 influencers and authorities the group respects.
  5. Analyse at least 10 other brands that market to the identity.
  6. Document at least 5-7 shared experiences.
  7. List the podcasts, YouTube channels and publications the group consumes.
  8. Describe their aspirations and fears, how they signal belonging, which opposing identities they define themselves against, and how the identity has changed over the past 5 years.

  [L0613, 2026]
- Negative identity label research: collect labels in five categories (body or health, capability or intelligence, relational or social, financial or success, moral or character), with at least 10 labels per category. Each label needs an exact quote, primary emotion, intensity 1-10, frequency, origin (self-applied, applied by others or both), duration, and source link. Also collect at least 20 aspirational labels, each with the emotion it would trigger, the distance from the current identity on a 1-10 scale, what would make the person feel they had earned it, what blocks them now, and a source link. [L0613, 2026]
- Avatar research counts:
  - At least 20 negative identity labels.
  - 15 aspirational labels.
  - 10 failed solutions, with why they failed and how that felt.
  - 20 failure phrases.
  - 10 skepticism patterns.
  - 10 things the avatar refuses to do, each with the real reason.
  - 25 hurtful quotes across relationship types (5-7 per relationship type: partner, children, parents, friends, coworkers, self).
  - 3-5 secret conversation scenarios.
  - 5 quotes in each post-transformation category (supporters, doubters, self, strangers).
  - Top 10 objections, with the real concern under each.
  - 5 label transition paths.
  - 3-5 complete avatars.

### Complete avatar profile and transition stories

- Complete avatar profile sections, in order:
  1. Demographic snapshot: name, age range validated by ad data, gender validated by ad data, location and situation, income, life stage.
  2. Ad data validation: share of spend, engagement rate, conversion rate, time patterns, resonant creative themes.
  3. Core problem: surface, real and identity.
  4. Emotional architecture: distress level, five ranked emotions with intensity and quotes, attention state, emotional entry point, emotion that shuts them down, transition threshold, and the permission needed to hope.
  5. Identity profile: primary negative label with duration and origin, secondary labels, aspirational label with distance on a 1-10 scale, identity wound summary in 2-3 sentences.
  6. Top 5 fears, with quotes.
  7. Relationship impact on partner, children, parents, friends and self, each with a hurtful quote.
  8. Failed solution history (5 items with result and quote) and refusals (3 items with real reason and inner monologue).
  9. Transformation they want: a 3-5 sentence genie-wish description, how relationships would change, and the identity shift.
  10. Top 3 objections with the real concern under each, skepticism triggers, and what would make them believe.
  11. Purchase psychology: emotional trigger, proof, risk reversal, permission, purchase-trigger emotion, breaking-point scenario.
  12. Messaging strategy: emotional sequence, hook approach (problem, mechanism, identity or aspiration), tone, format, and best time to reach.
  13. A mirror-moment opening line.
  14. A 5-7 sentence raw monologue of what they would say to a close friend after drinks.

  [L0613, 2026]
- Transition story procedure:
  1. Write down the negative label the person started with.
  2. Write down the positive label they now claim.
  3. Note what made the change possible.
  4. Record how long the transition took.
  5. Identify the turning point in the story.
  6. Trace the emotional path from the starting state to the end state.
  7. Compare how they describe themselves before and after.
  8. Attach the exact source quote and its link.

  Collect at least 10 complete transition stories. [L0613, 2026]

### Persona brief before scripting an ad

- Fill in this persona brief before scripting an ad:

  ```
  Persona brief to fill in before scripting an ad:
  - Demographics: age range, gender mix, income level, education, location.
  - Psychographics: attitudes toward life and general psychology.
  - Behavior: what the person does in a typical day.
  - Goals: what they want more than anything.
  - Fears: what they are afraid of not achieving.
  - Desires: the outcome or feeling they are after.
  - Pain points: the problems blocking that outcome.
  - Motivation: the underlying drive behind their behavior.
  ```

### Voice and reader focus

- The word "you" matters more than any other word in a sales letter, so the copy should focus on what the reader wants rather than on the seller. People are interested in their own goals, so the letter should speak to them directly. [L1365, 2019+]
- Copy written as one person speaking directly to one reader, using I, you and me, feels personal and converts better than copy written as if addressing a crowd. A byline with a real person's name strengthens this feel. [L1366, undated]
- Personal first-person invitation (pattern): rewrite a crowd-directed corporate line as a note from one person who would like to meet the reader again and show the new product.
  - Impersonal form: *We invite all customers to visit our booth.*
  - Personal form: *You may remember me from the last show. I would love to see you at the next one and show you the new machine.*
  - The personal version creates warmth and a direct invitation. [L1366, undated]
- Honesty shows through the copy. Hidden flaws or evasive claims are noticed by readers, while a candid tone about the product builds trust and makes the buying environment feel real. [L1366, undated]
- Creative and personal writing shows through the copy. Readers sense the personality of the writer, so write in the voice that represents the company rather than mimicking someone else. [L1366, undated]
- Logic does not have to be strictly correct to work, as long as the phrase conveys the intended feeling clearly. A reassuring phrase about returns can sound odd when read literally and still be effective, because it signals a prompt and caring company. [L1366, undated]
- The goal of all copy is to get the reader to exchange their hard-earned money for the product or service, so every choice in the copy serves that exchange. [L1366, undated]

### Buying environment and layout

- The first paragraphs of an ad set a buying environment, and layout and copy tone must match the price and nature of the product. A premium product needs a refined, confident look and tone, while a bargain needs a busy, deal-focused look and tone. [L1366, undated]
- In print, the copywriter controls the buying environment completely through layout and word choice, unlike a store where setting it up costs thousands. Use this control on purpose instead of leaving the mood to chance. [L1366, undated]

### Word choice and emotion

- Single words carry emotional associations, so word choice can change the reader's feeling even when the facts stay the same. Choose the more evocative word when it is accurate, for example *cottage* instead of *motel office* for a small building, and do not change the facts to suit a mood. [L1366, undated]
- Word-level emotion choice (pattern): when the facts allow it, choose the more evocative word, such as *cottage* instead of *motel office* for a small building, without changing the facts. Test single-word swaps on high-traffic claims, because one swapped word can change response measurably. [L1366, undated]
- Test case: in a mail order offer, replacing *repair* with *fix* raised response by about 20 percent in the original test. Test single-word swaps on high-traffic claims, because small wording changes can move response measurably. [L1366, undated]
- Readers respond more to vivid, emotional description than to a dry specification list. People enjoy emotion at any time but must be pushed to study facts, so put life and occasionally a light touch into the copy to stir the reader out of indifference. [L1369, undated]
- Replace long lists of points with a few vivid images the reader can see. Long sequences of *firstly, secondly and thirdly* put readers to sleep and lose the attention you have built. [L1369, undated]
- Sensory description (pattern): describe the product through a few concrete sights, sounds or textures the reader already knows, rather than through a list of specifications. The reader pictures the product in his own life, which makes the benefit feel real and memorable. [L1369, undated]
  - Example one: *The lamp throws a warm, even pool of light across the desk, and the dial turns without a flicker.*
  - Example two: *The jacket feels heavy and soft in the hand, and it closes with a sound that tells you the zip will hold through a wet walk home.* [L1369, undated]
- Build a word picture in the reader's mind: [L1369, undated]
  1. Pick a familiar object or scene the reader already pictures. [L1369, undated]
  2. Add one point of interest at a time, in logical order, starting with the framework and then the parts that matter. [L1369, undated]
  3. Stop when the reader can build the complete picture himself. [L1369, undated]
- Use sensory language and big-impact phrases that let the reader see and feel the benefit, such as describing a cold, dark office late at night when selling a fast computer. Build a card file of vivid phrases from advertising, novels and television, and use reference works of descriptive words when stuck. [L1370, undated]
- Sell an emotional story first and back it up with logic. Buyers decide on emotion and then justify with logic. Cold, purely factual letters rarely work, so the letter needs an enthusiastic voice to compensate for being ink on paper. [L1370, undated]
- Boredom is the most serious sin in marketing copy, so find something to be excited about even for dull products. Excitement can come from the feeling of owning the product, the enjoyment of the money or time it saves, or from exaggerated and passionate language that the reader will still find moderate. [L1370, undated]
- Passion exercise: write a letter that tries to persuade someone to do something risky but exciting, using vivid sensory detail, bold promises and the removal of each objection as you go. Then carry that energy into the real offer. Example for a hardware retailer: *Picture the first morning your shop opens with the new displays up. Your customers walk in and see exactly what you promised them. Here is how to get that morning ready in ten days.* [L1370, undated]

### Readability and simplicity

- Write sales copy in very simple language, keeping sentences and words basic and introducing one new idea at a time. Simple wording makes it easier for a reader on a phone to follow the argument and reach the offer. [L1365, 2019+]
- Make copy readable with short, punchy sentences, sentence fragments, short paragraphs of three or four sentences, everyday language and no jargon. Writing that sounds like speech moves the reader along, and grammar rules matter less than momentum. [L1370, undated]
- Write at a simple reading level. The target audience is often less literate or less patient than the writer assumes, a trap called the sophistication trap. Assuming sophisticated readers costs money, and the simpler approach is safer because even TV commercials are pitched at about a sixth-grade reading level. [L1370, undated]
- Reduce the perceived effort of your offer by stating the time it takes in plain terms, such as 15 minutes a day. A small, specific time commitment makes the reader more willing to start. [L1369, undated]

### Opening and first page

- Answer the first two questions, what is new and what is in it for the reader, on the first page. Readers often browse on phones and look for a reason to leave. If the prospect does not see their benefit quickly, they will leave and return to what they were doing. [L1365, 2019+]
- Structure the first paragraph as an extended headline that sells the reader on reading the letter, and only then sell the offer. Cut any preamble, such as apologies for not writing a personal letter or long explanations of why the mail matters. [L1370, undated]
- Write the headline so that it makes the reader feel the letter is welcome. [L1370, undated]

### Core belief and planning

- To find the core of a promotion, ask: what is the single most important thing the reader must believe? Write the answer as one sentence before drafting anything, because it works as the mission that every later section must support. [L1365, 2019+]
- Planning sequence for a promotion idea: [L1365, 2019+]
  1. Write the one belief for the promotion idea. [L1365, 2019+]
  2. Answer the first seven questions with a couple of lines each. [L1365, 2019+]
  3. Map out the whole promotion, which takes about 5 to 10 minutes. [L1365, 2019+]
  4. Do the research the promotion needs. [L1365, 2019+]
  5. Write each section in order, starting with the sections the map has already defined. [L1365, 2019+]
- Full sales letter sequence, in order: [L1370, undated]
  1. Research the buyer thoroughly with the ten questions and connect with their priorities. [L1370, undated]
  2. Use the offer yourself or talk to its users, and list every feature with its benefit, sorted by importance. [L1370, undated]
  3. List every objection and identify the flaws in the offer, then decide how to admit them. [L1370, undated]
  4. Plan delivery: postage class, envelope design, and whether it is a letter, postcard or self-mailer. [L1370, undated]
  5. Write the headline that makes the reader feel the letter is welcome. [L1370, undated]
  6. Build the reading path with subheads, bold text and short paragraphs so skimmers get the gist. [L1370, undated]
  7. Minimise price impact with comparisons, value stacking and payment framing. [L1370, undated]
  8. Use the proven persuasion techniques: scarcity or intimidation, ROI, ego, a strong guarantee and a story. [L1370, undated]
  9. Write a first draft without editing or worrying about length. [L1370, undated]
  10. Rewrite for strategy, adding repetition, a yes sequence and page-ending teasers. [L1370, undated]
  11. Rewrite for style with short sentences, big-impact words and personality. [L1370, undated]
  12. Answer every question and objection. [L1370, undated]
  13. Create urgency with a deadline, premium or limited availability. [L1370, undated]
  14. Add a P.S. that restates the offer. [L1370, undated]
  15. Run the checklist. [L1370, undated]
  16. Add graphic devices and build the layout with the copywriter involved. [L1370, undated]
  17. Rewrite for passion and edit aggressively for clarity. [L1370, undated]
  18. Compare the draft with strong examples. [L1370, undated]
  19. Pretest it aloud and with real customers at no cost. [L1370, undated]
  20. Prepare the final version, mock it up, cool off for a day, get second opinions, do a final review, then print and mail. [L1370, undated]

### Flow and the slippery slide

- The first seven questions should be answered within the first third of the letter. By then the reader should be emotionally sold. Only after the emotional sale is complete should the copy turn to logic. [L1365, 2019+]
- Once the reader understands how the mechanism delivers the result, they are pre-sold and ready for the offer. Present the offer only then, because the reader has already accepted that the new opportunity is the key to what they want. [L1365, 2019+]
- Story formula (pattern): [Background fact] and [supporting context], BUT [conflict or problem], THEREFORE [result or conclusion]. Use *and* to set up background and momentum, *but* to create tension, and *therefore* to deliver the conclusion.
  - Why it works: stories are how people process information, and the *but* creates the tension that keeps the reader reading. Proof and quotes should therefore be wrapped in this structure instead of being listed. [L1365, 2019+]
  - Example 1: *Reorders had been climbing for two years, and the team celebrated, BUT the newest customers had stopped coming back after month two, THEREFORE we rebuilt the onboarding sequence around month-two results.* [L1365, 2019+]
  - Example 2: *Our waitlist grew every week and the signups looked healthy, BUT more than half of those people never bought, THEREFORE the first purchase offer now appears on the confirmation page.* [L1365, 2019+]
- Each sentence should only have to earn the next sentence, so the reader moves through the copy like a train gathering speed. Once the reader is moving, the next lines become easier to read, which is why short opening sentences work. [L1366, undated]
- Slippery slide rule: every element and sentence must pull the reader forward so strongly that they keep reading to the end. Once a reader gets past about 25 percent of an ad, they are likely to read the whole ad, so the goal is to get them past that point. [L1366, undated]
- Seed of curiosity (pattern): end a paragraph with a very short line that promises the next paragraph holds something worth reading, so readers keep going through slower sections. Use it sparingly, because overuse makes copy feel manipulative. [L1366, undated]
  - Examples: *The catch is hidden in paragraph four. Most buyers miss the one detail that changes everything, so keep reading.* [L1366, undated]
  - *Most buyers miss the detail that changes everything. Keep reading.* [L1366, undated]
  - *The cheapest fix takes less than a minute. Here is why it works.* [L1366, undated]
- Hidden payoff (pattern): state a real benefit near the top of the ad and say it will be revealed later, so the reader has to read the full copy to get it. The payoff should be real and should arrive at the end. [L1366, undated]
  - Examples: *Your refund question gets answered in the final section, after the warranty details.* [L1366, undated]
  - *The cheapest way to run this setup is explained at the bottom of the page.* [L1366, undated]
  - *The fastest way to cut your phone bill is in the last paragraph, and most readers skip it.* [L1366, undated]
  - *The one setting that doubles battery life is explained after the list of features.* [L1366, undated]
- Never end a page with a complete sentence, since that gives the reader permission to stop. End each page mid-sentence or at an exciting phrase, and add teaser copy at the bottom of the page that works like a headline for the next page. [L1370, undated]
- Copy flows like a one-way chain: each block must anticipate the question raised by the one before it. A reader who stops at any question will not reach the order request, so check each transition. [L1366, undated]
- Create a double readership path. Impulsive readers skim headlines, subheads, photos with captions and boxed short paragraphs, while analytical readers read every word and want facts, figures, charts and statistics. Write the skim path so it carries the full offer, then use it as signposts along the full text for analytical readers. [L1370, undated]
- Repetition in one letter (pattern): restate the core promise in six different forms: as a plain statement, as an example, as a short true story, as a testimonial, as a quote from an expert or customer, and as a numbered summary. Repetition is one of the two ways people accept new ideas, the other being shock, so vary the wording on each pass. [L1370, undated]

### Length

- Copy length rule: copy should be long enough to create the buying environment, build interest, relate to the buyer's needs and make the full case, and no longer. Judge length by whether the reader takes the requested action, not by word count. [L1366, undated]
- Higher-priced products need more copy to justify the price and create the need. A low price that looks like a clear value needs less copy, while a low price that seems too cheap to be credible needs more copy to build trust. [L1366, undated]
- Short copy works only when the photos, the price and the medium already do the environment-building work, as in a catalog with strong photography and low prices. Otherwise write more copy. [L1366, undated]
- Readers read long copy when they care intensely about the subject. Interest drops once the reader has mastered the topic, so long copy pays off mainly for a target reader who is still learning and deeply involved. [L1366, undated]
- The more unusual a product, the more copy it needs to relate it to the user, build the buying environment and explain its new features. Mail order suits unusual products that need that much copy, because most retail settings do not allow it. [L1366, undated]
- Long copy works when it targets qualified buyers, because most readers will glance and discard, and that is acceptable. Write for the buyer, not for the non-buyer, and add pages until the buyer has every fact needed to act. Some letters grew from one or two pages to many and got better results with each addition. [L1370, undated]
- Research cited by the author found that readership of industrial ads with extensive text drops sharply for copy under about 50 words, and drops much less between 50 and 500 words. Do not shorten copy just to fit a format or page limit; write to tell the whole story. [L1370, undated]

### Drafting process

- Practice produces the largest gains in copywriting. Write every day in small forms such as letters, postcards, articles for local papers and ads, because volume builds skill faster than study alone. [L1366, undated]
- A first draft is expected to be weak. Do not judge the draft by how it reads, because the first draft is an emotional release of everything you want to say and the real quality comes from editing. [L1366, undated]
- When drafting, get the copy out first and ignore spelling and grammar, then switch to logic when editing. Write with the intuitive, emotional side of the mind and edit with the analytical side. [L1366, undated]
- Write the first version as a print direct response ad, even when the final product will be sold on the internet, in a catalog or on TV. The print version reveals the core selling points that can then be used in every other medium. [L1366, undated]
- Use a checklist as a mid-course correction in the writing process, scanning the draft against every formula, technique and strategy in the playbook, because drafts drift from good practice as they are revised. [L1370, undated]

### Editing

- In editing, refine copy so that it expresses exactly what you intend with the fewest words. You can rearrange sentences, cut words that add little, substitute stronger words, or add words for clarity, but keep the same emotional feel. [L1366, undated]
- Edit aggressively to clarify, not only to shorten. Remove any word or phrase that does not advance, strengthen or reinforce the core sales story. Edit the draft in several passes, setting it aside for hours between passes, because the shorter version usually follows naturally from clearer wording. [L1370, undated]
- Compare your draft with several strong examples, not just one. Check whether your text flows as smoothly, speaks to the audience as well, is structurally sound, is as easy to understand, and moves the reader to act as effectively. [L1370, undated]

### Objections, admissions and proof

- List every objection, fear, doubt and excuse a prospect could have for not responding, writing each one on its own card. Then answer each objection in the copy, because a letter that leaves an objection unanswered lets the reader stop at that doubt. [L1370, undated]
- Damaging admission (pattern): openly state the real drawbacks of the offer and who it is not for, then show why the trade-off is still worth it for the right buyer. This raises credibility because readers trust a seller who admits weak points.
  - Example: *This is not the cheapest option, and it takes two weeks to ship. If you need it tomorrow, a local store is the better choice, but if you want a tested product that lasts five years, this one is built for you.*
  - Second example: *We do not offer same-day installation or phone support after 6 pm. If you can plan a week ahead and want a repair done right the first time, you will be glad you waited.* [L1370, undated]

### Closing

- Use a P.S. after every sales letter, because many readers look at the end first. A P.S. that restates the offer or promise acts as a second headline. Do not consider the letter finished until a P.S. is written. [L1370, undated]

### Envelopes: attention and first impression

- Assume recipients sort their mail over a wastebasket and decide in seconds. In business settings an assistant may screen the envelopes first. The envelope must therefore earn the open before the letter is read. [L1370, undated]
- Never be half-way in envelope design. If the envelope reveals that the mail is business or sales material, use the whole face as a billboard with teaser copy and a strong image. Choose the disguised look or the billboard look deliberately, not a mix of both. [L1370, undated]
- Whatever the envelope promises must be delivered inside, such as a *Personal* label followed by something personal in the mailing. Unfulfilled envelope promises destroy the credibility of everything enclosed, while fulfilled promises increase it. [L1370, undated]
- Sneak-up envelope (pattern): a plain white envelope with no business name, only an address, a person's name as return address, no teaser text, individual or ink-jet addressing and a live stamp, so it looks like personal correspondence. The author estimated about a 98 percent chance of delivery and opening with first-class postage. Use it when prospects are cold and you want the envelope read before it is judged. [L1370, undated]
- Avoid gummed white address labels, which look like junk mail. Individually addressed or ink-jet addressed envelopes produce better response. The author paid home workers to hand-address envelopes because testing showed the lift justified the cost. [L1370, undated]
- A mailing to doctors that used the individual doctor's name and address as return address, with no company name, pulled up to 300 percent better than the same letter in envelopes showing the company. The author reports this from his own tests with a professional audience. [L1370, undated]
- Hybrid envelope (tool): combine personalisation with teaser copy. For example, use a window envelope showing a personalised letter headline with the recipient's name and city, plus printed teaser text on the front and testimonials on the back. Personalised headlines, body copy and enclosures are now easy to produce with printing technology. [L1370, undated]
- Use an attention-getter on the envelope only with a clear purpose. On third-class mail a catch-phrase pulls readers into the envelope, but on first-class mail a plain corner card usually pulls better because it looks like a personal letter. Test both versions against each other. [L1369, undated]
- If you use a catch-phrase or attention-getter on the outside of an envelope, send the mailing third class to save about half the postage. Otherwise the postage cost of a first-class letter is wasted on an envelope that already looks like an advertisement. [L1369, undated]
- Envelope picture (pattern): place a picture linked to the recipient's work or organization on the envelope, with the recipient's name line replaced by a care-of line for his company. A picture of a person he recognizes from his trade flatters him and draws favorable attention before he reads the letter.
  - Example one: a photo of a shop owner from a trade magazine, addressed as care of the owner's store and street address.
  - Example two: a photo of a plant manager from an industry newsletter, addressed care of the plant's name and city. [L1369, undated]
- When a mailing carries a named person's name as the return address, enclose a small notepad-sized note signed by that person. The note says the information helped them and asks the reader to go through everything enclosed. This note is the promise the envelope makes, delivered inside, so the reader trusts the rest of the mailing. [L1370, undated]

### Story and emotional frameworks

**Hero's journey as an ad storyline:**

- The customer is the hero with a goal. The problem is the villain that must be made to look bad. Stakes show what happens if they fail. Doubt in their own ability is shown. A credible guide (the product with proof of use or authority) appears and gives a path and the tools to win. The customer is called to action. The story ends with the hero winning, surviving and thriving. [L0515, 2023]

**Storytelling structure, in order:**

1. Give the hero a goal.
2. Introduce an obstacle and make it the villain, for example the constant hair on the bed.
3. Show the stakes: what happens if they fail, such as illness, allergies or losing guests.
4. Show the hero's self-doubt about reaching the goal.
5. Introduce a guide with credibility and proof who shows the way.
6. Call the reader to action.
7. Give the reader the means to win. [L0516, 2023]

**Hook-story-offer:**

- Open with a hook that names the reader's exact frustration and shows empathy, tell a short personal story that makes the struggle concrete, then end with an offer. The reader should feel understood before the offer appears.
  - Example: "Tired of finding fur on your clean clothes? I had the same problem and was embarrassed to invite friends over. Now I use a brush that clears it in minutes, and you can get it at a 20% discount this week." [L0516, 2023]

**Product story from the founder:**

- A product story can be told word for word from the founder's own experience, such as the frustration that led to developing the product. It attracts people who share that frustration and makes them receptive when the product is presented. ** [L0580, 2021-22]

**Story formula for health and science claims:**

- Formula: [Discovery or event] that [surprising result], which means [consequence for the reader]. Narrate who studied what, what they found and what it led to, instead of listing study results. A story keeps the reader in an emotional frame, while a bare list of results pushes them into analysis and lowers response.
  - A warehouse engineer timed every pick route for a month, found that one aisle layout doubled walking distance, and that change is why the new layout cuts shipping delays.
  - A soil scientist tested two fertilizer schedules across three seasons, found that one schedule kept yields steady when rain was scarce, and that is why the new schedule is built around dry weeks. [L1365, 2019+]

**Teach, transform, transact:**

- Through email series, testimonials sharing results-of-results, or direct imagination triggers, draw the audience into an emotional state that closes the deal. People buy on emotion, not facts and figures. [L0196, 2020+]

**Formula with a blame shift:**

- Formula: If you've tried [common solution] and got [disappointing result], it's not your fault: [common solution] only ever addressed [surface cause], while the real blocker was [real cause]. [New opportunity] is built to remove that real blocker.
- It removes blame from the reader by shifting it to the old approaches, which makes the new mechanism look like the logical fix.
  - "If you've tried sleep trackers and still wake up tired, it's not your fault: they only count hours, while the real blocker is late-day light exposure, which our routine resets."
  - "If your ads keep getting clicks but no sales, it's not your fault: most tools only measure traffic, while the real gap is the checkout step where buyers drop off, which our tracker pinpoints." [L1365, 2019+]

**Formula with an outside enemy:**

- Formula: [Frustrating outside force the audience already distrusts] is standing between you and [desire]. It names an enemy the reader already feels, which creates an us-versus-them feeling and aligns the reader with the seller.
  - "Big-box retailers that bury upsells in checkout are standing between you and a fair price on home repairs."
  - "Supplement brands that hide their exact doses are standing between you and results you can actually measure." [L1365, 2019+]

### Status quo and contrast

- Pattern for contrasting the status quo: "Normally you would" followed by two or three painful steps, the cost or inconvenience of the current way, then the product as the shortcut. It makes the old way feel costly and frames the product as the easy alternative. **
  - "Normally you would drive across town, wait in line and pay a fee just to get one key cut. This compact key cutter lets you do it from your kitchen table in two minutes."
  - "Normally you would call three installers, wait a week for a quote and pay a call-out fee just to hang one shelf. This kit lets you mount it yourself in ten minutes with just a drill." [L0396, 2021-22]
- Before-and-after framework:
  1. Show progress over time, using a carousel ad format when possible.
  2. Instead of labelling it "before and after", use language like "with and without" to show the implicit influence of your product. [L0258, undated]
- Avoid negative ads and before-and-after imagery that dwell on problems, and tell readers what to do instead. Ads that show bright, desirable outcomes outperform those that show the gloomy side. *(Note on use: the negative-impact lines below show the cost of inaction in one clear sentence. Then move to the bright outcome, and do not dwell on the problem.)* [L1261, undated]

### Future pacing, presupposition and negative impact

**Future pacing:**

- Future pacing writes copy that places the buyer in the outcome as if it has already happened, for example a clean, hair-free bed, so the reader feels the result before buying. It works best once the problem, the solution and the audience are already clear. [L0353, 2023]
- Describe the customer already using the product and enjoying the result, so they experience the outcome in their mind before buying. Pattern: "Once your [product] arrives, you will instantly notice [result]."
  - "Once your standing desk arrives, you will instantly notice how much less your back aches by Friday."
  - "Once your new running shoes arrive, you will instantly feel how much lighter each morning run becomes." [L0515, 2023]
- Describe the moment after purchase as if the reader has already taken the action and is already enjoying the result. This makes the benefit feel real and nearer.
  - "Once your new kettle arrives, you will have coffee brewing in under a minute."
  - "Imagine walking into a home that smells fresh the moment you open the door." [L0516, 2023]
- Future pacing is also the final step of the thought-command pattern, where the scene follows directly from the primary promise so the copy stays consistent (see the thought-command pattern below). [L0580, 2021-22]

**Emotional build in advance (procedure, in order):**

1. Take the audience through an emotional build, using humor to lower their guard and then a vulnerable story to deepen the connection.
2. Paint an aspirational picture of life after buying, in concrete detail, beginning with the word "Imagine".
3. Make the offer.
4. Only if they have not bought, paint the pain of doing nothing: what their life looks like if nothing changes.
5. Close with a simple question that asks whether their life would be acceptable if nothing changed. [L0378, 2019+]

Template for the aspirational picture:

```
Imagine waking up [TIMEFRAME] from now. [SPECIFIC PROBLEM SOLVED, e.g. your bills are paid / your back no longer hurts]. [CONCRETE DAILY MOMENT, e.g. you pick your kids up from school / you walk into the gym without dreading it]. [SOCIAL OR EMOTIONAL REACTION, e.g. friends comment on how well you look / your partner notices your energy]. [A SENSORY OR EMOTIONAL CLOSING DETAIL, e.g. you feel calm and proud of what you've achieved]. Imagine what that feels like.
```

[L0378, 2019+]

Template for the pain of doing nothing:

```
I understand if you're still on the fence. It's natural to feel some fear about buying. Let's think for a moment about what happens if you don't.

Imagine waking up [TIMEFRAME] from now and [PAIN DETAIL, e.g. your credit card debt has grown from $10,000 to $15,000 / your health has gotten worse]. [DAILY CONSEQUENCE, e.g. you dread going to work / you miss moments that matter]. [RELATIONSHIP OR EMOTIONAL CONSEQUENCE].

I'm not saying this to be harsh. I'm saying it because there is a cost to doing nothing, and I don't want you to be in exactly the same place [TIMEFRAME] from now. If your life was exactly the same in [TIMEFRAME], would you be okay with that?
```

[L0378, 2019+]

**Presupposition:**

- Word a sentence so it assumes the purchase or a fact is already true, so the reader is not asked whether to accept it. Pattern: "The last [product] you will ever need to buy" or "Have you tried the most effective [product] on the market?", where the reader answers about experience, not about the claim.
  - "The last kitchen timer you will ever need to buy."
  - "Have you noticed how quickly your pantry stays organized with this shelf?" [L0515, 2023]
- Word the copy so the desired outcome is assumed, rather than argued. Phrases like "the last X you will ever need to buy" or "have you tried the most effective X" assume the reader has or will have the benefit. Avoid claiming a ranking that the product cannot defend.
  - "The last password manager you will ever need to set up."
  - "Have you tried the sleep routine most nurses swear by?" [L0516, 2023]

**Negative impact:**

- Show what happens if the customer does not act, so they fear the cost of inaction. Pattern: "Stop [unwanted result] before it [bigger consequence]."
  - "Stop your kitchen from smelling of old grease before your guests notice."
  - "Stop the water stains from spreading before they ruin your hardwood floor." [L0515, 2023]
- Spell out what happens if the customer does nothing, for example a home slowly becoming overrun or a problem getting worse over time. Customers act more readily to avoid a clear, specific downside than to chase a vague upside.
  - "Every week you wait, the mould under the sink spreads a little further." [L0516, 2023]

### Feelings-first and identity-led copy

- Lead with the feeling the buyer wants to have. For emotional products, this comes before the features. (See the product-type rule above.) [L1219, undated]

### Thought commands and picture-led copy

- Thought command pattern: open with a command phrase such as "Imagine", "What if" or "How would you feel if", then describe a concrete picture of life with the primary promise delivered. The phrase makes the reader start imagining the scene automatically. The scene should follow directly from the primary promise so the copy stays consistent.
  - "Imagine finishing your morning run and feeling your sore muscles loosen within minutes of stepping into your home sauna."
  - "What if your evenings felt lighter, with a short routine that leaves you calm enough to sleep through the night?" [L0580, 2021-22]
- Picture-of-daily-life pattern: describe a simple, realistic sequence of the customer's day with the product, from the first action to the end state. Each step should be easy to imagine, so the reader can visualise using it at home.
  - "You wake up, walk to the kitchen and start your coffee machine. By the time the kettle boils, your cold plunge is ready, and you step in feeling awake."
  - "You finish work, open the app on your phone and start the massage chair. Ten minutes later you walk away from the desk with your shoulders loose." [L0580, 2021-22]
- Example of the sequence for an infrared sauna product: the customer rolls out of bed, switches the sauna on, makes a coffee while it heats, then relaxes inside for about 15 minutes and comes out feeling great and ready for the day. The copy works by making the reader see this routine happening in their own home. [L0580, 2021-22]

### Sales ad sequences: hook, education, curiosity, product, proof, close

**Proven ad structure (hook to call to action):**

- Hook, education, curiosity, product introduction, benefit demonstration, social proof, agitate-solution, call to action. The hook names a surprising fact the audience has not heard. Education explains why it matters. Curiosity hints at a solution. The product is then shown solving the problem with a visible benefit, followed by social proof and a strong call to action. Each step moves the viewer one stage forward.
  - Example for a dog toothbrush brand: "Your puppy's breath could be a warning sign of hidden gum damage." Then explain that bacteria build up under the gumline, hint at a simple daily fix, show the brush in use, add "Join 10,000 owners cleaning their dog's teeth at home", and end with "Order your starter kit today." [L0640, 2023]
- Ad structure presented in order: a compelling opening (for example, "Dog owners never ignore these three things in your dog's teeth"), instructional content exploring the underlying concern (such as gingivitis), engagement content raising questions ("What if I told you there was a solution?"), the commodity or service, how it solves the problem (displaying functionality), customer testimonies ("Join thousands of dog owners seeing amazing results"), and a powerful call to action. [L0067, 2023]
- Hero-style AIDA: move the viewer through attention, interest, desire and action, in that order. Each stage prepares the next, so the viewer moves from noticing the ad to buying.
  - Example: a bold opening question about cracked heels grabs attention, a short demonstration builds interest, a before-and-after result creates desire, and a clear call to shop ends the video. [L0902, undated]

**Means-end chain (benefit of the benefit):**

1. Write the product's basic feature.
2. Ask what the benefit of that feature is.
3. Ask again what the benefit of that benefit is, repeating until the answer is a final outcome such as making money, saving time or enjoying family life.
4. Build the copy around that final outcome, and show the positive result rather than the mechanics. [L0340, undated]
- Most people do not buy a product for its physical form but for the outcome it produces, such as cooking faster or reaching a place sooner. Lead with the outcome and treat the product's features as proof that the outcome is achievable. [L0340, undated]
- Use a sales-transition sequence that moves the buyer from knowing you to converting:
  1. Know: the buyer gets to know you through story and personal level.
  2. Like: show humour and share personal stories for common ground.
  3. Trust: share successes and use video testimonials.
  4. Challenge: challenge their perspective on the problem and offer an alternative solution. Create a "eureka moment".
  5. Convert: sell insights, not solutions. Plant the idea of what results could mean, and sell the "benefit of the benefit". [L0196, 2020+]

**Video ad copy-to-screen sequence (ad storyline by ad layout):**

- Ad layout sequence for a direct response print ad, in order:
  1. Write the headline and check that it grabs the reader.
  2. Write the subheadline and check that it makes the reader want to read further.
  3. Write the photo caption, since it is read often.
  4. Write the first sentence, short enough to pull the reader into the second sentence.
  5. Write each next sentence so it answers the question the reader is likely to have at that point.
  6. Build the body in blocks: first hook and set the mood, then explain how the product differs and how it works, then justify the price with comparisons, then address lasting value, then address service and reliability concerns.
  7. Finish with the request for the order and the response device.
  8. Block-diagram the complete ad to check the flow and that each question is answered in the right order. [L1366, undated]
- Direct response print ad elements and their jobs. The stated purpose is the textbook role. The real purpose is the one the copy should serve.

| # | Element | Stated purpose | Real purpose in the ad |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Headline | Get attention and draw the reader to the subheadline | Pull the reader into the subheadline and then the first sentence |
| 2 | Subheadline | Add information and explain the headline | Make the reader want to read the first sentence |
| 3 | Photo or drawing | Get attention and show the product more fully | Draw the eye and help the reader start reading |
| 4 | Caption | Describe the photo or drawing | Be read often, so it must also lead to the first sentence |
| 5 | Copy | Convey the main selling message | Hold the reader through every sentence to the offer |
| 6 | Paragraph headings | Break copy into chunks so it looks less imposing | Keep the reader moving down the copy |
| 7 | Logo | Show the company selling the product | Add continuity and trust |
| 8 | Price | Tell the reader the cost, in large type or buried in copy | Support the buying decision once the reader is engaged |
| 9 | Response device | Give a way to respond (coupon, toll-free number, order info), usually near the end | Make ordering easy at the moment of decision |
| 10 | Overall layout | Provide the overall look through graphic design | Create the buying environment for the whole ad |

### Ad structure library
**Prompt for building the structure toolkit:**

```
From successful campaign scripts, identify the repeating beat sequences and build a reusable structure toolkit. Document whether the approach is to apply an exact brought structure, select from a skeleton menu, or make a case-by-case judgment call. Separate narrative attitude (which stays constant) from production format (which varies). List psychological moves that succeed with a worked example for each. Catalog the distinct structural patterns found in winners, explain how to combine them with voice and moves, and keep a log for patterns that don't fit the existing set.
```

[L0039, 2026]

- Expect to derive 3-5 distinct winning structures (called skeletons) from your winning scripts when building the Structure Library. Derive only from actual scripts; do not invent structures that don't appear in the proven winners. [L0039, 2026]
- The Structure Library defines three modes for building ad scripts:
  - MODE 1: when given a specific brought structure to work from, rebuild exactly that structure rather than forcing a stock skeleton.
  - MODE 2: when given only an idea with no template, pick the best-fit skeleton from your corpus of proven winners.
  - MODE 3: when given a template that could go either way, flag whether to convert it to a proven skeleton or keep its original shape. Present both options and let the decision-maker choose before writing.
  - Off-corpus structures are valid and should not be rejected automatically. [L0039, 2026]
- A "brought structure" means a structure template provided externally or brought in from outside your corpus of proven winning ads, as opposed to structures derived from reverse-engineering your own top performers. In MODE 1, rebuild the brought structure exactly. In MODE 3, decide whether to convert it to a proven skeleton or keep its original shape. [L0039, 2026]
- Pattern for winning ad copy: reframe the problem as not permanent, name the mechanism that works, add social proof with a number and a timeframe, then close with a risk-free guarantee. Keep the sequence and swap in your own problem, mechanism, proof and guarantee.
  - Example 1: "Stubborn hair thinning doesn't have to stay. Our scalp serum uses a peptide complex to support follicles while you sleep, and thousands of customers report fuller hair within weeks. Try it with our 60-day money-back guarantee, no questions asked."
  - Example 2: "Puffy eyes don't have to be permanent. Our cooling gel-patch works on the swelling overnight, and over 5,000 reviewers say they see a difference by the second week. Risk-free: return it within 30 days for a full refund." [L0374, 2026]

### Personal offer and founder story hooks

**Personal-version offer.** Offer a special, personally made version of the product. Present it as something the seller would keep for himself, and invite the reader to accept it as a mark of respect [L1369, undated]. This works because pride responds to being singled out, and a personal gift feels more valuable than a standard offer [L1369, undated].

- Example one: I have had made for my own use a version with reinforced seams that most shops never offer; I would like you to try the first one before it reaches the market [L1369, undated].
- Example two: Our founder built this model for his own workshop, and we are letting a few long-standing customers buy it before it is listed [L1369, undated].

**Founder story.** Tell the human story behind the product, naming who made it and the specific event that led to it, so the product becomes memorable [L1369, undated]. A story gives the reader something to care about beyond the specifications [L1369, undated].

- Example one: A fisherman who lost his boat in a storm designed a lightweight anchor that one person can carry and that stores under a seat [L1369, undated].
- Example two: A nurse who spent her nights repacking supplies for a rural clinic built a kit that fits in a backpack and can be restocked in minutes [L1369, undated].

### Sales letter structure

**Six essentials, in this order** [L1369, undated]:

1. **Opening:** get attention by fitting into the reader's current thoughts, establish a point of contact with his interests, and make him want to keep reading [L1369, undated].
2. **Description:** picture the offer by first outlining its key features, then filling in the necessary details [L1369, undated].
3. **Motive:** show what the offer will do for the reader, such as comfort, pleasure or profit, rather than describing the offer itself [L1369, undated].
4. **Proof or guarantee:** give evidence that your claims are true, or build confidence with a money-back guarantee [L1369, undated].
5. **Snapper or penalty:** hold over the reader's head the loss of money, prestige or opportunity he faces if he does not act now [L1369, undated].
6. **Close:** tell the reader exactly what to do and how to do it, and make the action easy [L1369, undated].

**First sales letter.** Use a first sales letter with three jobs: select readers who already care about the idea, make the first commitment feel easy, and remove the risk [L1369, undated]. In one historical series, the first letter targeted adults who lacked a college education and wanted culture, then offered a short daily reading habit and a free trial [L1369, undated].

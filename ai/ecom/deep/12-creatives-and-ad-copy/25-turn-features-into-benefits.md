### Turn features into benefits

- Identify the product benefits and the desired outcome from the features before writing any copy, because features are the proof and benefits are what the customer buys. [L0917, undated]
- Write each feature on its own card, translate it into the benefit it gives the buyer, and sort the cards by importance. People buy what a product does for them, not what it is, so every feature must be converted into a benefit. [L1370, undated]
- Look beyond the obvious benefit for a hidden benefit that the buyer cares about deeply but rarely states, and build the message around it. Example: a product that saves work time can be pitched around the free time the buyer gets back for leisure, which the buyer values more than the savings alone. [L1370, undated]
- Do not write landing page copy that lists features without linking them to a customer benefit, because the customer cannot see why the feature matters. Follow each feature with a "why is it there" answer that names the result the customer gets. [L0917, undated]
- Benefit bullet pattern: translate each feature into the outcome the customer gets, so the feature explains the benefit. Pattern: *[Feature] keeps [surface or person] [protected outcome] without [unwanted side effect].* Examples: *Non-slip base keeps your rug in place without damaging your floor.* and *Quick-release buckle keeps your strap secure without the hassle of fiddly fastening.* [L0917, undated]
- Building ad or landing page copy around features (for example waterproof, quick-drying, smooth writing, no ink leakage) misses what buyers want. Write about the end benefit the buyer gets, such as fun time with kids, less screen time, or children's creativity. [L1183, undated]
- Manufacturers write specifications for technical buyers. A store owner should rewrite them into customer benefits, such as *easy to adjust, no tools needed* instead of a thread specification. [L1293, 2014]
- Write product descriptions as benefits, not features. Features are technical specs (for example CDMA and GSM, or 110 to 240 volt switching) that customers do not understand. Benefits state what the product does for them (for example *works in Canada, the UK and Asia* or *plug it in anywhere in the world*). [L1293, 2014]
- Where sources differ on how much feature content to keep, both apply in different places:
  - **Product descriptions:** lead with benefits and keep features as supporting detail. [L1293, 2014]
  - **Ads:** benefits carry more weight, but features must still appear, because a feature describes the product part and the benefit is the outcome that part produces. [L1425, undated]

### Lead with the outcome and the problem

- Sell the desired outcome first, then list the features that support it. Features act as proof that the outcome is reachable, not as the main pitch. [L1207, undated; L1209, undated]
- The buyer wants to know how the outcome is achieved, so present the outcome first and then the features, with each feature serving as proof that the outcome is possible. [L1209, undated]
- Describe the outcome with a vivid, dramatized metaphor to make the promise memorable, for example *melt inches off your waist* instead of *lose inches*. Keep the tone fun and positive so exercise feels less like a chore. [L1207, undated; L1209, undated]
- Make the big benefit claim either the unique selling point or the strongest benefit of the product, and state it boldly. A vivid metaphor can make it memorable, but the claim must still match the actual product benefit. [L0917, undated]
- Persuasive benefit copy describes the customer's desired outcome and, where useful, first surfaces the customer's problem and makes it feel worse before presenting the product as the solution. Frame the solution as fast, instant or convenient where that is true, and emphasize benefits more than features. [L0918, 2023]
- Benefit copy structure:
  1. Name the problem.
  2. State the solution.
  3. State the concrete benefit in a short form.

  Not every page needs to surface a problem, but the benefits must always be emphasized clearly. [L0918, 2023]
- Present benefits by showing the reader the life they could have, rather than only naming the benefit. Use the word *Imagine* followed by a vivid scene, or drop it and state the benefit directly, for example *You'll speak with confidence after attending this public speaking class.* [L1356, 2019+]

### Use occasions and lower the entry barrier

- Occasion pattern: state the moment the product is used, then the emotional result for the buyer's loved ones. Formula: occasion + product's role + result for others. [L0773, 2023]

| Occasion | Product's role | Result for others | Example line |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cold evenings | Soft fleece blanket | Whole family stays warm | Keep the whole family warm on cold evenings with this soft fleece blanket. [L0773, 2023] |
| Birthday dinner | Personalized cocktail glasses | Dinner feels special | Make every birthday dinner special with these personalized cocktail glasses. [L0773, 2023] |

- Emotional benefit pattern: replace a feature statement such as *high quality item, ships in two days* with a use case that connects the product to a shared moment and the customer's loved ones. Formula: person-centered situation + the product's role in it. Examples: *Make your weekend hikes easier with this lightweight backpack* and *Give your kids a calmer bedtime with this soft nightlight.* [L0773, 2023]
- A line such as *suitable for all ages* or *no experience required* lowers the entry barrier. It makes the product seem usable by anyone, which removes the feeling that the buyer has to be skilled first. [L1207, undated]

### Make benefits instant with analogies

- Formula: describe the product by comparing its effect to an experience or object the reader already knows, such as a common event, a sport or a household task. Why it works: the reader already has a clear picture to attach the new idea to, so they understand the benefit at once. [L1369, undated]
  - Example one: *Like finding a parking spot right at the front door on a busy Saturday, the new loading ramp saves the dock crew twenty minutes per shift.* [L1369, undated]
  - Example two: *Like having a second pair of hands on the bench, the clamp holds the frame steady while the glue sets.* [L1369, undated]

### Stack benefits and make claims specific

- Benefit stacking: list the ingredients and benefits one after another in a single run, each tied to a result the viewer wants, such as firmer, brighter, less volume loss or fewer lines. The ad in the lesson stacked about ten benefits in a row. Stacking makes each claim feel more convincing than the last, but each benefit must still match the product's real ingredients. [L0707, 2026]
- Name ingredients, such as a molecule that supports elasticity or a compound that works against overproduction of pigment, to add specificity that makes benefits believable. Link each ingredient to one concrete skin result. [L0707, 2026]
- A generic claim of *lowest prices* was replaced with a specific net profit figure of 3 percent, which proved more convincing because it implied prices were already near the minimum possible. Specific financial facts make a claim credible. [L1261, undated]
- A generic purity claim was replaced with specific production details, such as a count of experiments or a measured process, which created a far stronger impression. Turn general quality statements into exact, verifiable process facts. [L1261, undated]
- An itemized breakdown of hidden costs, such as 735 dollars of hidden costs on a 1,500 dollar car, supports a profit-margin claim better than the margin figure alone. Readers accept a concrete cost breakdown more readily than a bare percentage. [L1261, undated]

### Build the unique mechanism

- A unique mechanism explains what makes the product different from everything the reader has tried, without naming competitors. The reader infers that older solutions failed because they lacked that specific ingredient, method or technology. [L0772, 2026]
- Formula: name the one specific technology, ingredient or method that sets your product apart, then imply that older solutions lacked it, without naming any competitor. It works because the reader connects their past failures to the missing element.
  - Example 1: *Most serums stop at the surface. Ours uses a sealed-release carrier that reaches the layer where the damage sits.* [L0772, 2026]
  - Example 2: *Shampoo rinses away before the scalp absorbs much. This formula stays on the follicle for the full wash.* [L0772, 2026]
- The real problem is the reason the prospect's past solutions failed. Find it by reverse-engineering the answer to the uniqueness question. Once revealed, the reader sees that old approaches never addressed it and that your new mechanism does, which gives them hope. [L1365, 2019+]
- Explain the mechanism by linking it to a belief the audience already holds, which makes an unusual claim feel obvious. The explanation and its proof are also copy, so they must be engaging and wrapped in the story structure. [L1365, 2019+]
- Mechanism formula, in order:
  1. Existing belief the audience already holds.
  2. Your mechanism follows logically from that belief.
  3. Credible third-party proof confirms the result.

  Why it works: readers accept a mechanism faster when it extends something they already believe, and outside sources make the claim harder to dismiss. [L1365, 2019+]
  - Example 1: Most people already know consistent sleep drives recovery; our routine schedules the light and temperature cues that make that consistency happen, and an independent sleep lab measured the effect. [L1365, 2019+]
  - Example 2: Shoppers already believe fresh produce keeps its nutrients when frozen right after harvest; our delivery cold chain applies exactly that, and independent testing measured the vitamin retention. [L1365, 2019+]
- Give proof on the first page without revealing the mechanism yet. A reference such as a patent filing number or a named publication shows the opportunity is real, while the explanation of how it works comes later in the letter. [L1365, 2019+]

### Proof elements

- Each promise must be backed by proof at least as strong as the claim. Useful proof includes testimonials, short anecdotes, clear metaphors, case studies, charts and specific statistics. Promises that are not proven make skeptics doubt the whole message. [L0756, undated]
- Proof should come in threes. One or two pieces leave the claim feeling unresolved, while three is the standard target and more is better. Common proof types are case studies, demonstrations, testimonials, quotes from authorities, charts and graphs, official documents, and articles or headlines. [L1356, 2019+]
- When a claim is too high to prove with three examples, build proof from a sequence of smaller examples that add up to the claim.
  1. Show one example worth $5,000.
  2. Show a second example worth $7,500.
  3. Show a third example worth $10,000.
  4. State the total claim of $10,000 as proven. [L1356, 2019+]
- Show proof visually where possible (charts, screenshots) and reframe each proof point two or three times in different forms. A reframe shows the same information from a new angle, for example turning a one-time $10,000 gain into a yearly income of six figures, or comparing it to a familiar benchmark such as an S&P 500 return over a year. [L1356, 2019+]
- Proof answers the question "how do I know this is real" and must back up the one belief. Initial proof can be historical charts, testimonials or third-party quotes. In competitive markets, where skepticism is high, it should come right after the bold promise. [L1365, 2019+]
- Testimonials should mimic the target reader as closely as possible:
  - Show images of people who look like the ideal customer and reflect their economic and life status.
  - Use royalty-free or commercially licensed images, never random images from a search engine.
  - Make testimonials specific (amounts, timelines, steps followed, family reactions), since specificity conveys Big, Fast, Easy, Safe and Anybody. [L1356, 2019+]
- For mailings that sell a product for purchase now:
  - Use testimonials from happy users, which matter more than anything else for sales.
  - Use photographs rather than drawings.
  - Prove the product is easy to use through copy, photos or testimonials. [L1370, undated]
- For mailings that sell professional services, factual credibility such as years in business, clients served and sample client lists supports the offer. Real customer experiences, benefits achieved and doubts resolved are more persuasive. Make intangible benefits tangible with before and after photos, case histories and examples. Consider offering a free first consultation or free information to break down skepticism. [L1370, undated]
- Historical example (not current data): in ordinary homes of that period, about two-thirds of the heat from the furnace was wasted, with one-third lost through windows and doors and another third through uninsulated walls and roofs. A product that stops that waste could be argued with these figures. [L1369, undated]

### Close with a bottom line

- Close the benefits section with a bottom line of three short, parallel adjectives that sum up the product, for example *fun, simple, effective*. Three words are easy to remember and repeat, and the rhythm makes the claims feel complete and confident. [L1209, undated]
  - Example 1: *Fast, quiet, and built to last.* [L1209, undated]
  - Example 2: *Light, clean, and easy to store.* [L1209, undated]

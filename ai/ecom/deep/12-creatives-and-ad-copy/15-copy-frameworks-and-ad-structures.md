## Copy frameworks and ad structures

### Purpose of the copy and the Rule of One

- The primary purpose of ad copy is to generate clicks, not direct sales. Most ecommerce ads lead to a landing page where the full sales pitch happens, so the copy should persuade the reader to click the ad. ** [L0241, 2021-22]
- Starting with a problem statement separates relevant from irrelevant audiences before they engage. People who experience the problem read on or click, and those unaffected skip the ad, which reduces wasted spend and irrelevant clicks that cost money. ** [L0241, 2021-22]
- Each piece of copy follows the Rule of One: one central idea, one core emotion, one supporting story or fact, one main benefit, and one call to action. Competing ideas dilute emotional impact and split the reader's attention. [L0756, undated]
- Judge every element of an ad (headline, subheadline, photo, caption, logo, layout) by one question: does it get the reader to read the first sentence? Elements that do not lead there weaken the ad even if they look correct. [L1366, undated]
- Putting the main sales pitch at the very beginning usually performs worse than first building reader interest. Lead with hooks and story, then deliver the pitch once the reader is engaged. [L1366, undated]
- Direct response copy works by making the customer feel understood. The page shows the customer their problem, then the solution, in a way that connects emotionally, so they feel the product was made for them. [L0917, undated]
- Choose the copy approach by product type. Use emotional copy for products with personal meaning, such as a gift that expresses gratitude toward family. Use problem-solution copy for practical products that fix a specific issue. [L1219, undated]
- Not every product needs a problem-first ad. For hobby, identity or self-expression products, lead with the emotional need, such as comfort, control over a space or creative expression. [L0644, undated]

### Copy build order: from desire to headline

**Direct response build order (use in this sequence):**

1. Define the market desire: what the customer wants to reach or move away from, then ask "why" repeatedly to find the underlying need and emotion (for example shame about the home, fear of being judged, lost time). [L0515, 2023]
2. List the product's strongest features. For each one, write the reason it exists and the concrete benefit it gives the user (what it lets them do, how it saves time or effort, how it makes them feel). [L0515, 2023]
3. Map each benefit to a desire from step 1, so every feature is tied to a goal. [L0515, 2023]
4. Build a quick persona: demographics (fill what you can, not everything), psychographics, typical behavior, needs and motivations (connection, control, freedom from pain, conserving resources), feelings (frustrated, tired, ashamed), goals, problems and pains, and deepest fears. [L0515, 2023]
5. Draft headlines that combine desire, the persona's specific situation and a benefit, moving from generic to specific (for example, a specific breed or pet type in the headline). [L0515, 2023]
6. Set market awareness and market sophistication, then choose headline style and mechanism to match. [L0515, 2023]
7. Apply psychological principles and headline tactics to refine the copy, then write body copy and the call to action. [L0515, 2023]

- The order of work is: define the market's desire, then list features and convert each into a benefit, then build a quick persona, then choose the headline using market awareness and sophistication. Each step feeds the next, so the headline comes last. [L0515, 2023]
- Build copy in this fixed order:
  1. List the customer's main desires (what they want to get or get away from) and dig into the underlying tension behind each one.
  2. List the product's unique features, and for each one write the concrete benefit it gives the customer.
  3. Build a persona covering demographics (location, occupation), psychographics (values, personality type), behavior, the context in which they need the product, and their underlying needs, motivations and feelings.
  4. Determine the customer's market awareness stage.
  5. Determine the market sophistication stage.
  6. Choose the psychological principles to apply, then write the headline, body copy and ad script. [L0516, 2023]

- Value equation for checking whether a message delivers value: Value = (Dream Outcome multiplied by perceived Likelihood of achievement) divided by (Time plus Effort plus Sacrifice). Copy should raise the dream outcome, reduce the pain, raise the perceived likelihood that it will work, and cut time, effort and sacrifice. This is the Alex Hormozy value equation. [L0515, 2023]

### Sales copy order and body structure

**Top-to-bottom order for a product sales copy :**

1. Primary promise (the main headline-level benefit)
2. Painting a picture (vivid description of the customer's situation or the result)
3. Unique mechanism (why this product works differently)
4. Remaining features and benefits
5. Power benefits (strong, emotional outcomes)
6. Call to action at the end

The call to action comes last, after the emotion and logic have been delivered. [L0461, 2021-22]

**Body copy structure (general):**

- The headline opens the reader's attention. The main body leads into the problem and how the product solves it. A short segment builds the click and prepares the call to action. The call to action asks the reader to act by building on an existing belief the reader holds and moving them toward the belief you want. [L0515, 2023]
- Add the following elements where they fit: problem-agitate-solution in the body; curiosity questions (for example, "What if you could come home to a [clean result] every day?"); authority (for example, "approved by [expert group] across [country]"); similarity ("as [customer type] ourselves, we understand..."); features and their benefits; social proof ("Join thousands of happy customers"); future pacing; and limited-time offers or a free gift, when they are true. [L0515, 2023]

**Body copy opening:**

- Start by tapping into a belief or frustration the customer already holds, since readers accept claims that match their experience. Common examples are a chore they dislike or a problem they already expect. Example: "Keeping pet hair under control can be frustrating, especially when it returns as soon as you finish cleaning." [L0516, 2023]

**Body copy structure, step by step:**

1. Open with a belief or frustration the reader already holds.
2. Agitate the problem by describing how hard it is to fix and how it returns.
3. Pose a curiosity question that promises relief.
4. Name the unique feature or mechanism as the solution.
5. Quantify the claim, ideally as a comparison with competitors (for example, "twice as much as any other tool").
6. Add an ease claim such as "in just a couple of swipes".
7. Add a second feature and its benefit that removes time, effort or sacrifice.
8. Add social proof.
9. End with a call to action, optionally with an offer such as a free second unit. [L0516, 2023]

**Body copy template (fill in the brackets):**

```
[Opening belief or frustration the reader already has about the problem]. [Agitation: how hard the problem is to fix and how it returns]. What if you could [desired outcome] every day? This is now possible thanks to [product name]. Its [unique feature or mechanism] [quantified comparison claim with competitors] in [short ease or speed claim]. It also has [second feature] that [removes a specific time, effort or sacrifice], so you can [outcome]. Join [number of customers or other social proof] who already [result]. [Call to action]: [offer, e.g. a free extra unit or a discount] when you order today.
```

**Benefit stacking and feature-to-benefit conversion:**

- Ad components that strengthen direct response ads when a brand lacks equity:
  1. An attention hook that highlights a fact or the problem being solved.
  2. The big benefit or unique selling proposition that keeps viewers watching.
  3. A compelling offer.
  4. A clear call to action, such as "Shop now" or "Buy now".
  5. Benefit-focused copy stating what the product does for the person, backed by features.
  6. Social proof, since people copy what others do (cognitive shortcut).
  7. Scarcity or urgency, such as limited time or limited stock, because scarce items feel more valuable.
  8. Educational or entertaining content, since most Instagram and TikTok users want entertainment rather than a sales pitch.
  9. Curiosity sparking.
  These elements do not all need to appear in every ad. [L1348, 2023]
- Feature-to-benefit: for each feature, write the concrete benefit it gives the customer, such as a cleaner home, fresher clothes, more comfort, lower cost or saved time. [L0516, 2023]
- Use the same angle and vocabulary as the primary promise. For example, use efficiency words like "quickly" and "effectively" so the copy flows from the headline. Test different scenarios of the same product, such as morning use versus evening use, to see which converts better. [L0580, 2021-22]
- Short product ad pattern: open with a hook that names the problem, present the product as the answer, list two to four concrete benefits, each on its own text line with a tick or icon, then close with a time-limited offer and a clear call to action.
  - Example one: a hook about tired feet at the end of a shift, a stainless steel insole presented as the fix, four benefit lines, then "Pairs ship free this week, tap below."
  - Example two: a hook about dull kitchen counters, a cleaning spray presented as the solution, three benefit lines, then "Second bottle half price until Sunday, order now." [L0344, 2023]
- Takeaway statement: name the reader the product is not for, and the reason, before returning to the offer. This raises trust and desire among true prospects.
  - Formula: "This is not for [excluded reader] if [condition]. It is built for [target reader] who [need]."
  - Example: "This is not for weekend hobbyists who run once a month if you only need a basic pair. It is built for daily runners who log over 40 miles a week."
  - Example: "Skip this mixer if you bake twice a year. It is made for home bakers who knead dough every week." [L0340, undated]

### Problem-first frameworks: PAS and its variants

**Core PAS:**

- Problem-Agitate-Solution is one of the best frameworks for moving people to action: outline the problem, make the emotional impact worse, offer the solution, provide comfort, and finish with a strong call to action. [L0067, 2023]
- First name the problem, then make it feel worse by spelling out its consequences and how it feels, then present the product as the solution, and finally intensify the benefits by asking what each one means for the customer. Use it in headlines, body copy and ad scripts. [L0515, 2023]
- Problem-Agitate-Solution steps in order:
  1. State the problem the customer is already experiencing.
  2. Agitate it by making it worse and describing how it feels, for example frustrating, embarrassing or anxious.
  3. Present the product as the solution.
  4. Name the features.
  5. Translate each feature into its benefit for the customer, such as a cleaner home, fresher clothes, more comfort, lower cost or saved time.
  6. Close with the outcome, such as sleeping easily or being comfortable hosting guests. [L0516, 2023]
- Pain-Agitate-Solve framework:
  1. Describe the problem in the hook, optionally quoting customers.
  2. Explain the problem in detail and intensify it or villainize it.
  3. Present the product as the solution to the problem.
  4. Describe how the product works.
  5. State the product benefits.
  6. Show the result and how it improves the customer's life. [L0258, undated]
- Problem, agitate, solution pattern: name a problem, make it feel worse by hitting the audience's pride or identity, then present the product as the fix. The agitation step is what makes the solution feel necessary.
  - Example one: "Your running shoes look fresh, but your insoles smell after two miles, and everyone at the gym notices before you do. Switch to a sole that dries in an hour."
  - Example two: "Your garden looks tired all summer because the soil is starving, and your neighbour's roses prove the difference. Feed the soil with this and watch the change in one month." [L0524, 2023]
- Problem, agitate, solution framework: name the problem, make it feel worse, then show the relief, followed by a call to action. This creates urgency before the offer appears.
  - Example: "Still waking up with a stiff neck? Every night of bad sleep adds to the strain. Our adjustable pillow supports your neck in the position you actually sleep. Try it free for 30 nights." [L0640, 2023]
- Problem-agitate-solution steps for video, in order:
  1. Name the problem the viewer already feels.
  2. Make the problem feel worse by showing its consequences.
  3. Present the product as the solution.
  4. List the benefits and explain the mechanism that delivers them.
  5. Show the desired outcome the viewer gets after using the product. [L0644, undated]
- Example for snowboard shorts: open on the rider's problem of falling often in the park and losing progress, then make that pain feel worse by showing the time lost and being laughed at. Present the thick absorbent padding as the mechanism that moves the impact away from the body, and end on the desired outcome of riding jumps and progressing faster. [L0644, undated]
- PAS script formula: open by showing the customer's problem, agitate it by showing how serious it is and why action is needed now, then present the product as the solution with its concrete benefits. The viewer feels the pain before seeing the fix. Example: a worker wakes up tired from a poor mattress and sees how poor sleep disrupts daily tasks, then sees the mattress giving restful sleep and better daily productivity. [L0902, undated]
- PAS copy pattern: state the customer's problem and desired goal, intensify the discomfort around it, then present the product as the solution with its key benefits.
  - Pattern: "Cold feet are a real problem: [pain point with a credible statistic]. [Desired goal]. Our [product] is the [superlative] way to [outcome], designed to [benefit]."
  - Example: "Hot water bills are a real problem: your heater runs non-stop in winter. You want a warm home without the cost. Our smart thermostat is the most efficient way to keep rooms comfortable, designed to cut heating waste."
  - Example: "Tangled cables are a real problem: your desk looks chaotic and you lose time searching. You want a tidy workspace. Our cable organizer is the simplest way to clear clutter, designed to keep every cord in place." [L0917, undated]
- Problem-Benefit-Solution variant of PAS: instead of agitating the problem, state the problem next to the desired emotional goal, then present the product as the solution that delivers that goal. The copy talks to the customer's need directly rather than listing product specs. [L0917, undated]
- Problem-then-solution copy formula: name the problem the buyer already has, make its cost or discomfort feel more serious, then present the product as a fast or convenient fix with a concrete outcome. The reader feels the problem first and sees the product as the relief.
  - Example for a sleep aid: "Waking up at 3 a.m. again? Stop relying on sleeping tablets that leave you groggy. Our drop-free formula helps you fall back asleep within minutes."
  - Example for a back-support cushion: "Sitting all day wrecks your lower back. Switch to a cushion built for long desk shifts and feel the ache ease from the first session." [L0918, 2023]
- Pain-point copy pattern: pick a pain point that many customers share, expand it to show how large the problem is and which further problems it causes, then present the product as the solution and describe the improvement in daily life. The reader connects emotionally before seeing the product.
  - Example: "Back pain that follows you from the desk to the couch? Every week it steals more energy from work and evenings with family. This cushion relieves pressure so you can sit comfortably through the whole day."
  - Example: "Hair that frizzes the moment humidity hits? Every morning starts with a fight, and you end up hiding your hair under a hat. This serum keeps curls smooth from sunrise to dinner." [L0916, 2022+]
- Agitation ladder: write three lines that move from a physical problem, to a home or daily-life problem, to a social or emotional consequence. Each line describes a concrete situation the customer lives through. Each step raises the cost of doing nothing, so the solution feels necessary.
  - "My skin is itching all day. My bedsheets look dirty even after washing. I stopped inviting my partner's family over."
  - "The kids keep tracking mud through the hallway. The rug looks grey every morning. I feel embarrassed when the neighbours visit." [L0392, undated]
- Avoid-negative-outcome row: state what the customer loses by not buying, such as health risks, a dirty home and continued embarrassment. Use it to create urgency after the outcome section. Keep each consequence tied to the same problem used in the hook, so the message stays consistent from start to finish. [L0392, undated]
- Problem-aware ad formula: name the problem the reader already feels, then the fixes they have already tried or the costly or painful options, and only then introduce the product as the way out. The reader's frustration with old options makes the product feel like the logical next step.
  - "Tired of paying for laser sessions that fade after a month? Here is what can be done at home."
  - "Knee pain is not a reason to stop running, and painkillers only mask it. Here is the daily routine that works on the joint itself." [L0772, 2026]
- Naming a specific problem in the ad, such as smoker lines or dark hollow under-eye areas, lets the viewer recognise their own issue immediately. State the solution right after the problem so the link between them is obvious. [L0707, 2026]
- Universal pain point steps:
  1. Name a universal pain point that the target buyer shares, such as a boring routine (for example, "cardio is boring").
  2. Introduce the product directly as the fix for that pain.
  3. Promise that the product works for every level or type of user, for example "designed for everyone no matter your fitness level". [L1209, undated]
- Pattern: [common frustration stated as a universal fact]. We fix that with [product]. It names a pain the reader already feels and positions the product as the answer.
  - "Cold coffee every morning is annoying. We fix that with a self-heating mug."
  - "Slow checkout on phones loses sales. We fix that with one-tap payment." [L1207, undated]
- Segmented ad message formula: name a specific audience, describe their specific context or problem, then give one sharp promise that solves it. A narrow audience feels the message is written for them, which increases response.
  - Home cooks who keep nicking their fingers on the grater: a cut-resistant glove that keeps grip while slicing.
  - Commuters tired of arriving with soaked shoes: a foldable waterproof cover that fits in a work bag. [L0998, undated]
- Statistic pattern: open with a statistic about a share of the audience that has a shared deficiency, list the symptoms it causes, explain the mechanism behind them, then present the product as the fix. The reader recognises themselves, understands why the problem persists, and sees a solution that matches the cause. Use only figures you can back with a source.
  - "[X]% of remote workers report stiff shoulders by mid-afternoon; long hours hunched over a laptop weaken the muscles that hold posture. [Product] supports those muscles with a short daily routine."
  - "[X]% of new runners quit within six months, often because worn-out shoes cause shin pain. [Product] adds cushioning where the shoe loses support first." [L1164, 2026]
- Underdog principle: ads that cast the customer as the underdog, someone who wants a change, tend to outperform stories built on an all-powerful villain or on a brand that already has everything. Readers tend to root for the person who is trying to improve. [L0772, 2026]

### Opening patterns (hooks and first lines)

- Opening pattern for product copy: name a nightly frustration the buyer already feels, then the morning result, then the product's mechanism. The buyer recognises their own problem before reading any features.
  - "Lying awake at 3 a.m. again? Wake up with energy that lasts through the day."
  - "Stiff shoulders after a night on the sofa? A snug wrap that helps your body settle before bed." [L1194, undated]
- Angle-based ad copy: contrast how a buyer feels when well rested with how they feel after a bad night, then present the product as the bridge between them. A gap between two states is easier to feel than a list of features.
  - "Rested mornings or groggy meetings? See what one better night can change."
  - "Tight neck, cold feet, no sleep? Notice the difference by morning." [L1194, undated]
- Copy opening with the story or thought command: start the product copy directly after the headline with the story or the thought-command section, as the first element of the copy. Choose the story if one exists; otherwise use the thought commands. ** [L0580, 2021-22]

### Objection handling

- Handle the most common objection before the shopper raises it. For example, state that cheap knockoffs are never the same as the original product. [L1207, undated]
- Pattern: [dismissal of cheap alternatives] + [short reason they fall short]. The objection is answered before the buyer voices it.
  - "Do not be fooled by cheap imitations, they are never built the same."
  - "Cheap lookalike bags skip the lining, so they are never the same." [L1207, undated]
- Three-step pattern: [short label]: [step one], [step two], [step three], done. A three-step count makes the process look simple and quick.
  - "Easy start: pick your size, clip it on, go."
  - "Easy checkout: choose, pay, track." [L1207, undated]

### The Four U's

- A headline must carry a lot of information early, because most readers never read beyond it. Its purpose is to grab attention and convey the Four U's: Urgent, Unique, Useful, and Ultra-Specific. The four do not all need to be present, but if two or more are missing, the headline will likely fail to hold attention. [L1356, 2019+]
- **Urgent:** Usually conveyed by a date or time-limited prompt, such as *Take action by December 21* or *Sale ends this Friday*, or indirectly through FOMO when the big emotion is strong. Urgency is the simplest U and can be dropped if the other three are present. [L1356, 2019+]
- **Unique:** Non-negotiable. The headline must offer something the reader has never seen before. To find a unique angle, pick one specific element of the product or offer and emphasize it, such as a feature competitors do not highlight or a fresh framing. The revolution is not required; the unique element can be small, as long as the reader has not seen it. [L1356, 2019+]
- **Useful:** The headline must promise something the audience values. Research the ideal prospect, what topics they are currently curious about, and which benefits would make them keep reading. A headline built only on intrigue is not useful and fails the "What's in it for me?" test. [L1356, 2019+]
- **Useful, tied to a hot topic:** Tie the useful promise to a topic top of mind for the audience, such as a major news event or government policy, then add a concrete, useful benefit such as a money figure. The topic creates interest and the benefit creates usefulness. [L1356, 2019+]
- **Ultra-Specific:** Use uncommon, oddly precise numbers rather than round figures, for example $10,251 instead of $10,000, 12.5 years younger instead of 10, or a 251% strength gain instead of 100%. Precise numbers imply the claim was tested and make it seem real. Be as specific as legally possible and do not lie. Ideally, the specific figure is backed by an example demonstrated in the lead or body. [L1356, 2019+]

### Specificity and Numbers

- Specific measured claims beat general superlatives, because readers assume a precise figure reflects a real test. Formula: state the measured result, the test size or method behind it, and the concrete benefit. [L1261, undated]
  - *Stops pain in 30 seconds, measured on 400 users.* [L1261, undated]
  - *Cuts drying time by 42 percent versus the leading brand.* [L1261, undated]
- Pattern: a specific number from a real test or process turns a common claim into a fact that stands apart from rivals. Formula: name the common benefit, then add the exact figure that proves it. [L1261, undated]
  - *Lasts 9 hours on a single charge, tested across 60 days of use.* [L1261, undated]
  - *Sets in 12 seconds, measured on 200 test batches.* [L1261, undated]
- Solution-first property headline: *How to Do Wonders With a Little Land!* beat a conventional problem-solution headline by 40%. It also beat a flat promise headline, *Two Acres and Security*, by 75%. Start with the solution when the reader's problem is familiar and the product is clearly relevant. [L0756, undated]
- Problem-solution campaigns can also work at scale. In the Kimberly-Clark case below, they helped build a brand worth $1.6 billion, so the choice between problem-first and solution-first depends on whether the reader already knows the problem and sees the product as relevant. [L0756, undated]

### Finding the Angle

- Concept headline from a production detail: a technical detail that explains why a product is special can become the headline concept. Ask the manufacturer how a feature is made and why competitors cannot copy it, since that answer often holds the strongest angle. Example: *Laser Beam Digital Watch* came from learning that the laser was the only way to seal the capsule. [L1366, undated]
- Curiosity from an unusual but true fact about how the product works invites the reader to learn more. Formula: state a surprising fact, then reveal the mechanism or benefit that explains it. [L1261, undated]
  - *Your morning coffee gives its kick two hours after you drink it.* [L1261, undated]
  - *Each batch is filtered through a material that catches particles smaller than a human hair.* [L1261, undated]
- Question about a surprising, specific unknown tied to the reader's trade or daily life. The reader must read the first line to get the answer. An unanswered question creates curiosity, and relevance to the reader's work keeps it from being mere entertainment. [L1369, undated]
  - *What is the one invoice error that quietly drains a small shop's cash every month?* [L1369, undated]
  - *Why do some restaurants keep their regulars for ten years while others lose them after a single visit?* [L1369, undated]

### Benefit Headline Formulas

- Pattern: [desired outcome] + [novelty word] + [low-effort or fun word]. It promises the goal while removing the perceived effort and sacrifice. [L1207, undated]
  - *The simple new way to sleep through the night.* [L1207, undated]
  - *The fun way to keep your dog active every day.* [L1207, undated]
- Headline formula: combine the big desired outcome with a novelty word that signals something different from what the buyer already tried and failed, and an effort-reducing word that signals it is quick or easy. [L1209, undated]
  - *The fresh and easy way to build strength at home.* [L1209, undated]
  - *The smarter way to learn a language in 10 minutes a day.* [L1209, undated]
- Principle: novelty words such as "new" make an offer feel different from failed past attempts, which raises interest. Effort-reducing words such as "fun" or "easy" counter the common belief that reaching a goal is resource-intensive, slow, or unpleasant. Benefit headlines work best when they signal low effort. [L1207, undated; L1209, undated]
- Pattern: [product] suitable for [broad group], [skill or experience] not required. It removes the fear of not being good enough. [L1207, undated]
  - *Built for beginners and pros alike, no training required.* [L1207, undated]
  - *Works for every shoe size, no tools needed.* [L1207, undated]
- Formula: [Specific measurable outcome] in [timeframe] [without the usual cost, effort, or risk]. A concrete outcome with a timeframe makes the big promise believable, and naming what is avoided removes the main objection. [L1365, 2019+]
  - *Cut your monthly grocery bill by $300 within six weeks without clipping coupons or changing what you eat.* [L1365, 2019+]
  - *Earn a second income of $2,500 a month from a phone and a laptop, without building a website or holding inventory.* [L1365, 2019+]
- Formula: Introduce a new [type of solution] that [unique claim] before [the point the market usually waits for]. The "new" element triggers attention, and the unique claim signals a breakthrough the reader has not seen before. [L1365, 2019+]
  - *A new bookkeeping method that flags tax deadlines a full month before your accountant does.* [L1365, 2019+]
  - *A new breathing routine that lowers resting heart rate within ten days without setting foot in a gym.* [L1365, 2019+]
- Product-page pattern: product name, then a USP or bold claim, then the emotional result the buyer gets. The page continues the feeling the ad created, so visitors do not lose the thread of their buying journey. [L0916, 2022+]
  - *Lightweight Standing Desk Mat: Stand for hours without the ache in your lower back.* [L0916, 2022+]
  - *Compact Travel Blender: Fresh smoothies on the road, zero mess in your bag.* [L0916, 2022+]
- Add a short semi-headline or benefit line directly after the product name that uses the same emotion as the ad creative. This keeps the visitor's experience consistent from ad to landing page. [L0916, 2022+]

### Product Image and Customer Language

- Strong product image headlines borrow the words customers use in their reviews, such as the transformation they describe or the frustration they are relieved to be rid of. Formula: real customer phrase plus the outcome it promises, short enough to read at a glance. [L0887, 2026]
  - *Finally sleep through the night without waking up sore.* (transformation) [L0887, 2026]
  - *No more scrubbing the stains off by hand.* (removed frustration) [L0887, 2026]
- Transformation headline: Product that takes you from [current pain] to [desired state], often followed by a short pair of outcomes. The reader sees their own pain first, then the before-and-after shift the product promises. [L1164, 2026]
  - *Running shoes that take you from sore heels to pain-free miles.* [L1164, 2026]
  - *Coffee that takes you from groggy mornings to focused, steady starts.* [L1164, 2026]

### Audience and Identification

- Pattern: a headline that names the specific group a product is for signals relevance and filters out everyone else. Formula: state the group, the core benefit for that group, and a concrete result. [L1261, undated]
  - *For busy parents: dinner ready in 15 minutes.* [L1261, undated]
  - *For runners with knee pain: a sleeve that adds support on every mile.* [L1261, undated]
- Identification headlines address the reader as the person who has the problem or is responsible for it. In one comparison, a headline naming the older-looking woman beat a solution-only headline by a large margin, because it targeted a feeling she wanted removed. Formula: *For the [person] Who [problem they feel or cause].* [L0756, undated]
  - *For the Freelancer Who Chases Invoices Every Month.* [L0756, undated]
  - *For the Homeowner Who Keeps Paying for Repairs That Should Have Been Caught Early.* [L0756, undated]
- Flagged how-to headlines: a straightforward promise of a desirable result, often preceded by a flag that names the specific reader. This makes the headline feel written for the reader. [L1370, undated]
  - *How To Get Your First Ten Reviews In Two Weeks.* [L1370, undated]
  - *For Freelancers Who Hate Invoicing: How To Get Paid On Time.* [L1370, undated]
- Question and conditional headlines: a question that challenges or provokes the reader, or an "If you are X, you can Y" statement that targets the reader's identity. [L1370, undated]
  - *Are You Losing Sales Because Your Photos Look Cheap?* [L1370, undated]
  - *If You Are Over 50, You Can Still Start a Profitable Online Store.* [L1370, undated]

### Lead Types

- Offer and promise elements can be combined with other lead types, because every sales piece needs them anyway. Most other combinations break the one-idea focus. A secret lead can be paired with a promise, for example a technique kept hidden while the headline promises a result, as long as one idea and one emotion drive the copy. [L0756, undated]
- **Secret lead:** In a typical secret lead, around 300 to 400 words go to illustrating the promise and building credibility before the secret is named. The secret itself stays withheld until the reader is emotionally ready to buy. [L0756, undated]
- **Story lead:** Testimonials, founder or expert bios, historical proof, and track records can all serve as the basis of a story lead. Story leads engage readers who do not know the seller or who distrust bold claims. Tell the story quickly and tie it back to the core promise of the promotion. A story lead can work directly or indirectly, depending on the audience. [L0756, undated]
- **Indirect story opener:** Describe two people who start from the same background and end up in different situations, with no product or offer mentioned until later. The reader keeps going to learn why the paths split, and the sale follows once interest and emotion are set. Pattern: *Two [people with same starting point] began the same way; one [outcome A], one [outcome B].* [L0756, undated]
  - *Two freelance designers signed their first client on the same week; one now sets her own rates, the other still waits for invoices to clear.* [L0756, undated]
  - *Two neighbors bought the same model of rain barrel; one has a full garden tank every summer, the other has a rusted lid and an empty yard.* [L0756, undated]
  - This works because the reader wants to know the cause of the difference, and the product becomes the answer. [L0756, undated]
- Underdog story headline: says someone was doubted for attempting something and then succeeded. It appeals to sympathy for the underdog and to the reader's own private doubts. [L1370, undated]
  - *They Said My Small Shop Would Never Last Past Winter, and It Just Hit Its Best Year.* [L1370, undated]
  - *Everyone Laughed When I Asked for a Raise. Here Is What Happened Next.* [L1370, undated]
- Who-else headline: a question or statement that implies many other people already know something the reader does not, creating curiosity and fear of missing out. [L1370, undated]
  - *Who Else Wants Their Kitchen Clean Without Scrubbing?* [L1370, undated]
  - *Who Else Is Quietly Cutting Their Shipping Bill in Half?* [L1370, undated]
- First-person story headline: starts with how something changed for the writer, followed by a dramatic result. It works best when the before and after are very different and the story has real stakes. [L1370, undated]
  - *How a Cheap Tripod Turned My Sales Around in One Month.* [L1370, undated]
  - *How I Doubled My Repeat Customers Without Spending a Cent on Ads.* [L1370, undated]
- Secrets, numbers, and warnings: "Secrets of" headlines promise insider knowledge, "N Ways to" headlines add a specific number to a how-to promise, and "Warning:" headlines grab attention for problem-solution copy. [L1370, undated]
  - *Secrets of Shops That Sell Out Every Monday.* [L1370, undated]
  - *17 Ways to Cut Your Packaging Costs.* [L1370, undated]
  - *Warning: Your Current Supplier May Be Adding Hidden Fees.* [L1370, undated]
- Promise and crowd headlines: "Give me X and I will Y" telegraphs a clear, short offer, and "Thousands now do X even though they Y" uses a crowd plus a surprising contrast to build belief. [L1370, undated]
  - *Give Me Ten Minutes a Day and I Will Show You How to Automate Your Orders.* [L1370, undated]
  - *Thousands Now Sell Online Even Though They Never Studied Business.* [L1370, undated]
- Promise-only check: remove the offer, price, and guarantee from the headline or opening promise, then read the bare promise. [L0756, undated]
  1. Take the headline or opening promise and remove the offer, price, and guarantee.
  2. Read the bare promise and decide whether it still creates anticipation.
  3. If it does, use it as the opening for prospects who are on the fence or not ready for an offer yet.
  4. If it does not, make the promise more specific or more relevant to the core desire. [L0756, undated]
- Indirect leads carry specific risks: using an indirect angle when a direct offer would have worked, choosing an angle too subtle to connect back to the product, taking so long to reach the product that the reader gets bored, and writing something interesting that has nothing to do with the sale. Clever or coy copy that never ties to the product is not indirect selling and often leaves the prospect unclear about what is for sale. [L0756, undated]

### Offer and Sale Headlines

- Sale headline formula that converts for a specific customer: use a short line that speaks to the customer's situation or the moment they are in, then put the offer in a badge or subheading. It works better than generic sale slogans because it tells the reader the offer is meant for them. [L0992, 2025-26]
  - *Chilly commutes need a better layer. Get 40% off until Sunday.* [L0992, 2025-26]
  - *Old sneakers holding you back? Your new pair is 25% off this week.* [L0992, 2025-26]
- Service-first offer: tell a trial or sample story instead of asking for a purchase, because it lowers the reader's risk. Formula: state the free or low-risk benefit, the way to get it, and what the buyer keeps. [L1261, undated]
  - *Try a full week of the new filter at home before you pay a cent.* [L1261, undated]
  - *Get a free sample pack of our blend at your nearest store, no purchase needed.* [L1261, undated]
- Returning-visitor offer headlines: address the visitor as returning, state a concrete discount or benefit, and put it in the headline to raise click-through rate. Original forms were *Special offer for returning visitors* and *Returning visitors get 10% off*. [L1203, 2016]
  - *Welcome back: free shipping on your cart today.* [L1203, 2016]
  - *Returning visitors save 15% this week.* [L1203, 2016]

### Advertorial and Pre-sales Headlines

- Advertorial headlines work when they promise practical, specific knowledge about a problem the reader already has, framed as education rather than an ad. Formula: a credible insider angle + a specific symptom or problem + a concrete benefit or secret to learn. [L1131, 2018+]
  - *Why senior dogs lose their walking energy, and the three daily habits that help them feel younger again.* [L1131, 2018+]
  - *Five overlooked reasons a cat's coat turns dull, and what owners can check this week.* [L1131, 2018+]
- Pre-sales content titles answer a question the reader already has about a product, such as "Do X really work?", "What to look for in a Y", "The definitive guide to Z", or "The origin of a product". Such titles are engaging enough to get a click from cold traffic. [L1203, 2016]
  - *Does a magnesium spray really help you sleep?* [L1203, 2016]
  - *What to look for in a weighted blanket.* [L1203, 2016]

### Search Listicle Headlines

- Listicle formula: [Number] + [best/top] + [item] + [location or topic] + [audience or experience qualifier]. The base phrase captures the broad search, and the trailing qualifier targets a specific segment such as beginners, advanced users, or first-time buyers without losing general appeal. ** [L1421, 2021-22]
  - *11 Best Hiking Trails in Colorado for Every Fitness Level.* (travel niche) [L1421, 2021-22]
  - *19 Best Budget Apps for Freelancers Who Are Just Starting Out.* (finance niche) [L1421, 2021-22]

### Long-form Sales Headline Blocks

- A long-form sales headline block usually has three parts: a short label line above the headline that signals a limit or warning, the main headline, and a sub-line beneath it that expands the claim with a figure or time frame. A testimonial box is often placed under this block as an extra trust signal. Test the whole block as one unit, since the parts work together. [L1356, 2019+]

### Testing and Adapting Headlines

- Take a printed article, headline list, or product description. [L1343, undated]
  1. Use a permanent marker to black out every word you do not want.
  2. Read the remaining words in order; they form a new headline, hook, or short piece of copy.
  3. Adapt the result to your product and audience. [L1343, undated]
- Test several headlines against the same body copy and compare cost per customer, not clicks, because clever headlines can draw many non-buyers. [L1261, undated]

### Case Studies

- **Kimberly-Clark and Kleenex:** Kimberly-Clark had warehouses of cellucotton, a filter material originally made for gas masks, at the end of World War I. The company first sold Kleenex as a disposable replacement for towels used to remove cold cream. A 1925 magazine campaign promised no more dingy cold-cream towels, using celebrities to say it was how movie stars kept a pretty skin. Other ads tested uses such as handkerchiefs, coffee filters, and cleanup towels. A head researcher with chronic hay fever pushed the top copywriter to rewrite the ads with the handkerchief use up front, under headlines such as *Don't Put a Cold In Your Pocket* and *No more sore noses*. These problem-solution campaigns helped build a brand worth $1.6 billion. [L0756, undated]
- **Victor Schwab and the self-help book:** A self-help book was first printed with only 5,000 copies, and its author had a long, unrelated career in sales before teaching the course that became the book. A promise-led space ad by copywriter Victor Schwab used the book's own title promise, followed by a quote and a list of promise-laden chapter titles. This ad and others that followed sold over 15 million copies. [L0756, undated]

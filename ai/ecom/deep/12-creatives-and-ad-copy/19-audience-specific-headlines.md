### Audience-specific headlines

- Two-audience headline: name each audience segment in capital letters, then ask a question that promises a specific, visible result for that segment.
  - Formula: 'SEGMENT A! Want [specific result]? SEGMENT B! Want [different specific result]?'
  - Examples: 'TRAIL HIKERS! Want boots that stay dry through a full day of rain? COMMUTERS! Want a bag that fits a 15-inch laptop and a lunch?' [L0340, undated]
- Audience-specific question: name the audience's exact pain point as a question, then give the specific benefit with a measurable detail.
  - Example 1: "Are you sick of dog hair on your bed? This microfiber brush lifts hair in half the time."
  - Example 2: "Tired of sweat in your running shoes? This mesh insole dries 2 times faster after every run." [L0552, 2023]
- lead the headline with the main problem your business solves that the customer is likely experiencing, such as sizing, installation or delivery questions for products that often cause hesitation. Use other common pain points if they fit better. [L0488, 2021-22]
- question headline naming one specific purchase hesitation, such as size, installation or delivery time, so the viewer sees their own doubt in the ad. Formula: a question about the hesitation, followed by body copy offering direct contact for an answer. An open doubt draws attention in a crowded feed and makes the call to action feel useful.
  - "Not Sure Which Size Fits Your Garden?"
  - "Wondering How Long Delivery Takes Before You Buy?" [L0488, 2021-22]

### Schwartz-style headline tactics

**Breakthrough Advertising tactics (template)**

```
Breakthrough Advertising headline tactics (Eugene Schwartz), used to strengthen headlines. Write one headline per tactic that follows the tactic with your own product and benefit, keeping a number, comparison, or demonstration where the tactic calls for it.
1. Measure the size of the claim: put a number on the claim (e.g. '[Result] in just one [action]').
2. Measure the speed of the claim: state how fast the result comes (e.g. 'Get [result] in under [time]').
3. Compare the claim to an unnamed rival: state a comparison to other leading options (e.g. 'Collect twice as much [thing] as other leading brands').
4. Metamorphose the claim: turn the product into a vivid image (e.g. 'like a magnet').
5. Sensitize the claim: appeal to the senses (e.g. imagining running a hand over a clean surface).
6. Demonstrate the claim: show the result visually (e.g. 'See the pile of [thing] collected from a single cushion').
7. Dramatize the claim: contrast the bad outcome with the good one (e.g. 'Instead of a couch covered in [thing], find a clean space').
8. Remove limitations from the claim: say it works on all variants (e.g. all types of the item or all surfaces).
9. Associate the claim with values or people: connect it to what the audience values or who they are.
10. Show how much work the claim does in detail: explain the mechanism that delivers the result.
11. State the claim as a question: pose the problem as a question the reader answers yes to.
12. Offer information on how to accomplish the claim: give a clear how-to in the copy.
13. Tie authority to the claim: link to a respected publication, expert, or award.
14. Show before and after: display the transformation.
15. Stress how new the claim is: present the product as the latest technology.
16. Stress the exclusivity of the claim: be among the first to use it, so the reader feels status.
```

**Tactic examples**

- Measure the size: state a specific, measurable result so the reader can judge it. The number makes the claim concrete and easier to believe.
  - Examples: 'Our brush removes 99% of pet hair in one sweep.' 'Cut your monthly grocery spend by 15% with one shared list.' [L0516, 2023]
- Measure the speed: name a short time frame in which the result happens, so the reader sees how quickly the problem goes away.
  - Examples: 'Say goodbye to pet hair on your furniture in under five minutes.' 'Clear a clogged drain in under two minutes with no chemicals.' [L0516, 2023]
- Metamorphose the claim: describe the product as a different, vivid object that performs the function in an exaggerated way, which makes the mechanism memorable.
  - Examples: 'This brush is a magnet for every hair on your bed.' 'This mop is a sponge that soaks up spills in a single pass.' [L0516, 2023]
- Sensitize the claim: attach the reader's senses to the outcome, such as touch, sight or smell, so the benefit feels physical.
  - Examples: 'Imagine running your hand over your couch and feeling nothing but smooth fabric.' 'Picture a kitchen that smells fresh the moment you walk in.' [L0516, 2023]
- Demonstrate the claim: show a concrete visual scene of the result in a small, specific amount of time, so the reader can picture the proof.
  - Examples: 'See a month of dust lifted from a single sofa cushion in one pass.' 'Watch one swipe collect all the crumbs from a car seat.' [L0516, 2023]
- New mechanism question: name the unique mechanism, add curiosity, and tie it to the problem outcome in the form 'Could [new mechanism] really [outcome]?' Keep the mechanism in the headline so the reader sees what makes the product different.
  - Examples: 'Could this heated insole really end cold feet on early morning runs?' 'Could a single tap really lift every stain from your sofa?' [L0516, 2023]

**Objection and benefit headlines**

- Turn the biggest objection into the headline: name it openly, then explain why it is a benefit.
  - Example one: Not the lightest bag on the market, because it's built to carry a laptop and a week of groceries without tearing.
  - Example two: Not cheap, and we're fine with that, because it lasts through years of daily use. [L0378, 2019+]
- Benefit-led headline: name the outcome the buyer wants in their own words, then the product quietly solves the underlying problem.
  - Example one: Get your old routine back, and stop wasting evenings on the wrong fix.
  - Example two: Get the weekend you were promised, and leave the chores to a system that already runs itself. [L0378, 2019+]

**The 38 ways to strengthen a headline**

Thirty-eight ways to strengthen a headline by how the claim is worded, in the original order:

1. Measure the size of the claim with an exact number.
2. Measure the speed of the claim with a time figure.
3. Compare the claim against a rival, a higher price or an older method.
4. Express the claim as a metaphor.
5. Make the reader feel, smell, taste, touch, see or hear the claim.
6. Demonstrate the claim with one prime example.
7. Dramatize the claim or its result as a short scene.
8. State the claim as a paradox.
9. Remove a usual limitation from the claim.
10. Attach the claim to a value or a person the prospect wants to be like.
11. Show in detail how much work the claim does.
12. State the claim as a question.
13. Offer information on how to get the result.
14. Attach an authority to the claim.
15. Use a before-and-after contrast.
16. Stress that the claim is new.
17. Stress that the claim is exclusive to you.
18. Turn the claim into a challenge the reader can test.
19. Present the claim as a short first-person case statement.
20. Condense the claim by naming your product as the replacement for the old one.
21. Symbolize the claim with a parallel image instead of a direct measure.
22. Connect the mechanism to the claim inside the headline.
23. Startle the reader by contradicting how he thinks the mechanism works.
24. Connect the need and the claim in one line.
25. Offer information inside the ad as the hook.
26. Turn the claim or the need into a short case story.
27. Name the problem or need precisely.
28. Warn the reader about the risks of not using the product.
29. Emphasize the claim through phrasing, by splitting it into two sentences or repeating part of it.
30. Show the claim is easy by pointing to a limit everyone already accepts as overcome.
31. State the difference that sets the product apart.
32. Surprise the reader into seeing that a former limit no longer applies.
33. Address the people who cannot buy the product.
34. Address the prospect directly by the role or rank he holds.
35. Dramatize how hard it was to make the product.
36. Say the claim sounds too good, to meet the reader's doubt head-on.
37. Challenge the prospect's limiting belief about himself.
38. Turn the claim into a question and answer it.

### Crowded markets and unique features

- Crowded-market headline: name the unique feature in the headline when many similar products already exist. The feature is the hook.
  - Example: 'Unique microfiber static head removes more hair than any other brush' [L0353, 2023]
- Name the unique mechanism or feature so the buyer can tell the product apart. The pattern names a unique mechanism, a concrete result and a short time frame.
  - Examples: 'Patented magnetic head pulls hair out of every fabric in one pass.' 'Dual-layer cleaning pad lifts pet fur from sofas in seconds.' [L0353, 2023]

### Lead types

**Choosing the lead: direct or indirect**

- The more aware and ready the prospect, the more direct the lead should be. The less aware or more skeptical the prospect, the more indirect the lead should be. Direct leads fail when the audience is skeptical or does not yet believe a solution exists. [L0756, undated]
- Direct approaches work well when the product is easy to understand, the promise is large but easily accepted, the deal or guarantee is exceptional, the customer already trusts the seller, or a product improvement was already awaited by the market. [L0756, undated]
- Indirect approaches work better when the customer knows little about the seller, the product needs explanation, the customer is jaded and skeptical, a timely news connection is strong, or the product is being reinvented or elevated. [L0756, undated]
- Being clever or coy is not the same as being indirect. A lead that entertains without ever connecting to the product often leaves the prospect unclear about what is being sold. [L0756, undated]
- A direct lead with a skeptical or highly aware-but-jaded prospect, such as a car buyer or someone who has heard too many similar offers, usually fails. Shift toward the indirect side to rebuild trust first. [L0756, undated]

**Promise lead**

- Use the Promise Lead for prospects who are mostly aware and almost ready to buy, and for prospects sitting on the fence. It opens with the product's biggest and most relevant claim and usually ties the claim to a core desire. [L0756, undated]
- A Promise Lead must target the prospect's core desire. The promise should be original, relevant, bold but believable, and supported by proof at least as strong as the claim. Oversized promises that cannot be proven make skeptics disbelieve the whole message. [L0756, undated]
- To find a strong promise: first check what competitors have already promised, then identify what the prospect expects, and finally identify what the product can truly deliver without overselling. Under-delivery from an over-sold product makes the product fail faster. [L0756, undated]
- When a promise seems too large and readers become skeptical, reduce the claim, turn it into a question that addresses the doubt, or shrink it to a believable size. Scaling a claim back can make it more credible, as a Sears battery demonstration with three or four cars was more believable than one with ten. [L0756, undated]
- Promise lead pattern: state the product's biggest benefit as the headline, add a specific time frame or result, then prove it.
  - Formula: '[Desired result] in [short time frame], without [common objection].'
  - New example: 'Clear, even skin in 14 days, without the redness that harsher serums cause.'
  - New example: 'Sleep through the night in one week, with no sleep aids or alarms needed.'
  - The time frame makes the promise concrete, and the second clause pre-empts the common objection. [L0756, undated]
- Promise leads usually fail with skeptics and highly unaware prospects, because a bare promise sounds like one more sales claim. They work best with people on the fence who just need a nudge. [L0756, undated]

**Problem-solution lead**

- Show empathy first, linger on the problem only long enough to earn a commitment to read on, then offer hope of a relevant solution. Lingering too long without offering hope makes the copy feel like it exploits fear. [L0756, undated]
- Name the deep emotional worry in one phrase (for example guilt, shame, fear of running out of money), then offer relief.
  - Formula: '[Painful situation]? Here is what ends it.'
  - New example: 'Worried your savings won't last past 75? Here is how to build a withdrawal plan that keeps paying out.'
  - New example: 'Tired of feeling like the only one who can't keep up at work? Here is a 10-minute routine that closes the gap.'
  - It works because it names the feeling and immediately gives a path to relief. [L0756, undated]

**Question and inversion leads**

- Questions that state a problem with an almost inevitable yes answer from the right audience are a strong way to start a conversation. The word 'these' in a question like 'Do You Make These Mistakes?' can make the problem feel like it applies to the reader personally. [L0756, undated]
- Inversion (solution-problem): promise the benefit of escaping or avoiding a situation before naming the problem.
  - Formula: 'Enjoy [benefit] without [problem].'
  - New example: 'Keep your savings growing without the stress of market swings.'
  - New example: 'Get the energy you need for your day without the afternoon crash.'
  - The reader senses the solution first, and the problem is framed as something they can avoid. [L0756, undated]

**Invented-term problem**

- Give the problem a new name that makes sense of the negative situation, then explain it so the reader learns what it means and what to do about it.
  - Formula: '[Coined term]: Is it costing you [specific loss]?'
  - New example: 'Pantry drift: Is it quietly adding $40 a month to your grocery bill?'
  - New example: 'Comparison fatigue: Is it making your savings feel smaller than they are?'
  - A new label feels like insider knowledge and creates curiosity about the cure. [L0756, undated]

**Invitation lead**

- Frame the offer as an exclusive membership or invitation so the prospect feels included, and reveal the deal details less openly. It works for products with a long tradition of quality, clubs, societies and luxury items, but only when the offer really has exclusive value.
  - Pattern: 'You are invited to join [exclusive group] and receive [valuable access] before anyone else.'
  - New example: 'You are invited to join the Alpine Pantry Circle, where members receive first access to limited-batch olive oil each season.'
  - Exclusion creates desire to be let in. [L0756, undated]

**Secret lead**

- Use a Secret Lead when a product has an element the customer does not know, or when its known qualities can be renamed or reframed to feel new. Start the secret in the headline and keep the teaser going through the copy. [L0756, undated]
- The secret must be both intriguing and beneficial, and tied to a major benefit of the product quickly enough that the reader understands why the secret matters. [L0756, undated]
- Do not disclose the secret during the lead. Revealing it too soon removes the curiosity that keeps the prospect reading. For many Secret Leads, the secret is held back until the prospect buys the product. [L0756, undated]
- Specificity overcomes the skepticism that secrets trigger. Exact figures, dates, named sources and worked examples make a teaser feel credible even when the secret is not yet explained. [L0756, undated]
- Provide clues as the copy progresses: restate the headline in different words, add dates, name credible figures or celebrities, or tell the reader what the secret is not. The prospect feels closer to the answer while the actual answer stays hidden. [L0756, undated]
- Secret lead pattern: a headline combining a credible claim with an unexplained term, a number, or a closed-then-reopened opportunity, with the secret deferred to the body copy.
  - Formula: '[Unfamiliar term] That [specific result] for [specific investment]'
  - New example: 'The Harvest Ledger Method That Turned a $200 Pantry Budget Into $1,800 in Savings.'
  - New example: 'The Quiet Lever Behind Consistent 9-Minute Morning Routines That Actually Stick.'
  - The reader must read on to learn what the term means and how the result was achieved. [L0756, undated]
- Transubstantiation means taking something ordinary and reframing it as something special, carrying over the emotional appeal of a more exciting example. Neologizing means giving a familiar concept a new name to create intrigue. Use one of these when the product's qualities are already known. [L0756, undated]

**Proclamation lead**

- A Proclamation Lead opens with a bold, emotionally compelling statement that is relevant and nearly unbelievable, usually in the headline. The copy then provides evidence that makes the implied promise believable, and the product is revealed only after the proof is shown. [L0756, undated]
- Use it for consumer products when the prospect is unaware of the product, and for business-to-business audiences already aware of the product who could be alarmed by a fact that threatens their current tools or practices. [L0756, undated]
- Proclamation pattern: an imperative or declarative headline implying a serious consequence, followed by a claim the reader wants to check.
  - Formula: '[Imperative] or [consequence]' or '[Outdated tool] Is Obsolete.'
  - New example: 'Stop Using Your Old Budget Sheet. It Is Costing You Up to 30% More Than You Realize.'
  - New example: 'Your 2019 Shipping Calculator Is Obsolete: Here Is What Changed in Carrier Rates.'
  - It startles, creates a worry about the status quo, and promises a clear way forward. [L0756, undated]

### Editorial and advertorial headlines

- Editorial headlines should be benefit-driven, combining a news-style or attention-grabbing claim with a benefit or curiosity element. A plain product name or price in the headline weakens the editorial. [L0578, 2023+]
- Headline pattern: pair a surprising problem or bold claim with a benefit or curiosity hook, such as a benefit-driven news headline or an expert reveal. The headline decides whether the reader continues, so it must be interesting enough to keep them on the page.
  - Example 1: 'Why Millions of Commuters Wake Up Stiff and the Surprising Pillow Fix Experts Recommend'
  - Example 2: 'Vet Reveals the Simple Daily Habit That Keeps Dogs Moving Well Into Their Senior Years' [L0578, 2023+]
- Numbered advertorial headline: [number] reasons [target audience] [keeps failing at, or is missing out on, a specific problem], [promised fix or time frame]. The number signals a quick, scannable read, and naming a specific problem lets the reader see themselves in it before the product appears.
  - Example: 'Four reasons your sourdough keeps collapsing, and the fix takes one weekend.'
  - Example 2: 'Three mistakes most home office workers make with desk lighting, and how to fix each in minutes.' [L0552, 2023]

### Meta titles and descriptions

- The meta description and headline are the ad for an article in Google search results, so they must sell the click. Use persuasive sales copy in these fields, which is a different skill from writing article body text. [L0382, 2017]
- Headline and meta description formula: name the exact topic people search for, add a concrete benefit or a question the searcher wants answered, then add a credibility cue such as the brand or a specific detail. Search results are ads that must win a click against competitors, so the searcher should see that the page answers their exact need.
  - Example 1 (new): Shopify vs. WooCommerce: Which Platform Fits a $5,000 Monthly Budget?
  - Example 2 (new): Should an Online Store Show a Phone Number? What Shoppers Expect in 2026 [L0382, 2017]

### Other title patterns

- Personalised gift-book titles: replace a generic title with a short phrase about the relationship between the giver and the recipient, such as what they share or how they feel about each other. Keep it short enough for the cover.
  - New examples: 'Two Hearts, One Shared Story' and 'Counting Every Reason, Twice' [L0333, 2026]

### Testing headlines

- Signs and headlines do not need to be artistic, but they must say the right thing. Test variations until a clear winner appears. [L0378, 2019+]
- A fruit stand split-tested signs and found that a short, direct line such as 'Picked Today' outperformed more generic phrases. Daily income rose from hundreds of dollars to more than $1,000 with the winning sign. [L0378, 2019+]
- Check each headline device against three jobs. A device should do at least one of these: strengthen the claim with more detail or measurement, make a familiar claim fresh by changing its angle, or pull the reader into the body copy by promising information or asking a question. A device that does none of these adds nothing. [L0330, undated]

### Headline Principles

- A headline selects the people who could be interested and speaks to them directly, so the reader can tell at a glance whether the ad concerns them. Write each headline for one specific audience, not for everyone. [L1261, undated]
- Clever, blind, or amusing headlines may attract many more people, but most are not buyers, so the ad's true response falls. Headlines that hide the offer or product do not reach the right people. [L1261, undated]
- The headline has the largest effect on return. The same ad body run with different headlines can differ by five to ten times in response. Test several headlines against the same body copy and keep the one with the best cost per customer. [L1261, undated]
- One advertiser's keyed record for a single product included nearly two thousand headlines, and results varied enormously even when the body copy was almost identical. Headline testing is worth the effort. [L1261, undated]
- A headline counts as copy because its only job is to persuade the reader to continue with the rest of the content. The same headline principles apply to blog posts and similar content, although general educational content writing is a separate discipline. ** [L1460, 2021-22]
- In a letter, headlines do most of the selling, so they must be as strong as a print ad headline. Place the headline above the salutation or between the salutation and body, in bold or a boxed panel, because it gets the reader past the first glance. [L1370, undated]
- The main headline of a page should be bold and clearly visible, at most one to two lines. It can be a USP, a bold claim about the product, or a benefit-driven statement placed in the first section of the page. [L0916, 2022+]
- Open a landing page with a large, bold headline that states the main benefit or desired outcome. Visitors move toward pleasant outcomes and away from painful ones, so the headline should show the gain first. [L1207, undated]

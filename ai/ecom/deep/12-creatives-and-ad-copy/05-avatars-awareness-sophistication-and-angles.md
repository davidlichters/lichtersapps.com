## Avatars, awareness, sophistication and angles

### Awareness stages: the five levels

Schwartz's awareness scale has five stages, counted from unaware upward:

- **Stage 1, Unaware:** does not know they have a problem.
- **Stage 2, Problem-Aware:** knows the problem, does not know that solutions exist.
- **Stage 3, Solution-Aware:** knows solutions exist, but not all options.
- **Stage 4, Product-Aware:** knows your product, unsure whether it is right.
- **Stage 5, Most Aware:** knows your product, may be skeptical or comparing.

Default to Stage 2 for cold traffic with new products unless research clearly indicates otherwise. [L0039, 2026]

one source counts the same five levels from most aware downward:

- Knows the product and wants it (most aware). [L0330, undated]
- Knows the product but does not want it yet (product-aware). [L0330, undated]
- Knows the desire but not the product (solution-aware). [L0330, undated]
- Knows the need but not the solution (problem-aware). [L0330, undated]
- Completely unaware of the desire (unaware). [L0330, undated]
- Each level is separated from the next by a psychological wall between indifference and strong interest. [L0330, undated]

The same levels are described by what the prospect knows and what lead type suits them:

- **Most aware:** knows the product and only needs the deal. [L0756, undated]
- **Product-aware:** knows the product but doubts it fits. [L0756, undated]
- **Solution-aware:** wants the result but does not know the product delivers it. [L0756, undated]
- **Problem-aware:** feels the problem but not that a solution exists. [L0756, undated]
- **Completely unaware:** no knowledge of the problem, product or category. [L0756, undated]
- The awareness level decides the lead type and how direct the copy should be. [L0756, undated]

one source describes the stages by what each reader knows:

- Unaware readers do not see the problem. [L0772, 2026]
- Problem-aware readers know the problem but not that a fix exists. [L0772, 2026]
- Solution-aware readers know fixes exist but not your product. [L0772, 2026]
- Product-aware readers know your product but are not yet convinced. [L0772, 2026]
- Most-aware readers only need a reason to buy now. [L0772, 2026]

Each ad should be matched to one of the five stages. Each stage needs a different opening, because a reader who does not yet know they have a problem needs a different start from someone already looking for your product. [L0772, 2026]

| Stage | Who is in it | What the ad must do |
|---|---|---|
| Unaware | Does not know they have the problem | Make them notice the problem |
| Problem aware | Knows the problem, does not know the solutions | Present the problem and a way out |
| Solution aware | Knows several solutions, not your brand | Show why your solution beats the alternatives |
| Product aware | Knows your product and its ingredients but has not bought | Remove the last doubts and give a reason to buy |
| Most aware | Knows the product and is ready to buy | Give the offer and a reason to act now |

#### Message actions per stage

| Stage | Who is in it | Message action |
|---|---|---|
| Unaware | Does not know they have a problem | Educate on the problem first |
| Problem Aware | Knows the problem but not that solutions exist | Amplify the problem and hint that solutions exist |
| Solution Aware | Knows solutions exist but not your product | Present your product's unique selling points and how it solves the problem |
| Product Aware | Knows your product but is not convinced | Address objections about price, value, effectiveness and ease of use |
| Most Aware | Knows your product and its value, needs a final push | Use discounts, limited-time offers and urgency |

Match the message to the stage the audience is in. [L0515, 2023]

### Judging the stage and the copy implication

- Classify each customer on Schwartz's five stages. Failed-method and competitor-name language signals later stages, while pure "struggling" language signals Problem-Aware. [L0039, 2026]
- Immediately after classifying, extract the copy implication: one strategic directive that reshapes how the voice and approach must work for that stage. For example, for a Problem-Aware audience the voice must sound like a person describing how the world operates, not a polished advertiser trying to heal broken belief. [L0039, 2026]
- To judge how unaware a prospect is, ask how new the product is in the market and whether similar products already exist. If similar products exist, the prospect has a frame of reference and is not fully unaware, so a more direct lead can be used. [L0756, undated]
- Use the stage to choose the ad angle. Buyers who do not yet recognize the problem respond better to ads that surface a need or desire. Buyers already comparing options respond better to comparison ads such as us-versus-them. Do not use one formula for every audience. [L1348, 2023]
- Unaware customers do not know they have a problem, so the copy must first educate them about its consequences, for example by asking whether they have noticed a specific risk or a specific symptom. Once they recognize the problem, position the product as the solution. [L0516, 2023]
- Problem-aware customers know the problem but not that a solution exists, so use problem-agitate-solution: state the problem, make it feel worse, then introduce the product. Use vivid, specific wording of the frustration to make it land. [L0516, 2023]
- Solution-aware customers already know alternatives such as lint rollers or scrapers exist, so surface the problem and then show a unique feature that solves it in a newer or better way than those alternatives. Do not just repeat that a solution exists. [L0516, 2023]
- Product-aware customers know your product but have not bought it, so highlight features that make it better than other products and address their common objections to ease the purchase decision. [L0516, 2023]
- Match the copy to market awareness: an audience unaware of the problem needs education, an audience aware of the problem needs the solution, and an audience aware of solutions needs a clear reason to choose your product. A crowded market needs a unique mechanism or angle. [L0524, 2023]
- Buyers who have tried many methods and now feel skeptical are usually problem-aware and leaning toward solution-aware. Lead with empathy for the methods that failed, not with a polished product pitch. [L1140, 2026]
- Before writing, identify where the prospect sits on Schwartz's five levels. The level decides the lead type and how direct the copy should be. [L0756, undated]
- For problem-aware prospects, find the point of maximum anxiety: the frustration, desperation, fear or anger the problem causes. Opening with that emotion earns a connection before any product is named. [L0756, undated]
- The most aware buyers usually sit on the house list and among multi-buyers, the customers who have bought before and come back. These groups are the easiest to sell to, so the offer can be direct and short for them. [L0756, undated]
- Problem-aware prospects need the problem explained. Solution-aware prospects know several solutions exist and compare approaches. Product-aware prospects compare specific products or providers and need differentiators. [L0643, 2015]
- Buyers sit at different points of awareness, from knowing they have a problem but not that solutions exist, to knowing a solution exists but not which one to choose. Copy works best when it speaks to the stage the reader is actually in, because that makes the seller seem to understand them. [L1157, 2021-22]
- When a buyer feels that the seller understands their problem better than they do, the buyer assumes the seller also has the solution. Copy that speaks at the buyer's current awareness level builds this trust and makes selling easier. [L1157, 2021-22]

### Writing for each stage: the three-text pattern

Use three primary texts, one for each of three awareness stages. The problem-aware text opens on the pain and hints that a solution exists. The solution-aware text admits that other options fell short and introduces the mechanism with proof. The transformation text shows life after the product, lists what is included and makes the offer clear. It works because each reader meets the message at the stage they are actually in. [L0348, 2026]

- Problem-aware example, in italics: *Tired of cold draughts creeping under the door every winter? There is a simpler way to seal them this weekend.* [L0348, 2026]
- Transformation example, in italics: *Picture the first winter evening your living room stays warm without touching the thermostat. The full sealing kit arrives in one box.* [L0348, 2026]

### The product-journey ladder (a different model)

This is a second five-stage ladder about how familiar the buyer is with the product and the purchase. It is not Schwartz's awareness scale and should not be mixed with it.

- Five stages run from not knowing the product exists, to considering it, to planning a purchase and needing details, to buying, and finally to habitual repeat use. Ads can cover all five stages in a single piece of copy, or a series of ads can move the prospect from one stage to the next over time. [L0340, undated]
- Start copy at the stage where the target audience is, not where the product is. Early-stage prospects need an explanation of what the product is, while late-stage buyers need reasons to choose it. [L0340, undated]

### Market sophistication: what the market has already heard

- Market sophistication describes how exposed a market is to a claim, and it sets the type of headline. [L0515, 2023]
- Market sophistication describes how many competing products and claims the audience has already seen. If you are first to market, state the basic claim. If you are second but the product is new to the audience, enlarge the claim the first seller made, for example from "cleans hair" to "cleans hair quicker than ever before." [L0516, 2023]
- When the audience has heard many similar claims, lead with a new mechanism, meaning a unique feature that solves the problem in a better, more sophisticated way. When competitors copy that mechanism, exaggerate and amplify it further rather than inventing something unrelated. [L0516, 2023]

one source's stage-by-stage headline types:

| Sophistication stage | Market situation | Headline approach |
|---|---|---|
| Stage 1 | First to market | State the need or claim simply and directly. [L0515, 2023] |
| Stage 2 | Claim already seen, product new | Enlarge the claim and outbid competitors on the big benefit. [L0515, 2023] |
| Stage 3 | Competitors have claimed everything | Add a new mechanism or feature to the headline that solves the problem in a new, unique way. [L0515, 2023] |
| Stage 4 | Competitors copy the mechanism | Embellish the mechanism and solve more of the problem, or find a new feature that is more believable. [L0515, 2023] |
| Stage 5 | Market no longer believes claims | Shift from desire to identification, speaking to who the customer is and who they aspire to be, as large commodity brands do. [L0515, 2023] |

#### Assessing sophistication for a product

1. Pick the product and define the problem it solves. [L0515, 2023]
2. Count the competitors selling the same product. [L0515, 2023]
3. List the competitors solving the same problem with a different product (for example lint rollers or hard brushes). [L0515, 2023]
4. Decide the sophistication stage from these counts and the claims already made, then choose the headline approach for that stage. [L0515, 2023]

### Headlines at sophistication stage 2

Formula: state the core claim, then add a measured enlargement of its size, speed or count, with each new competitor trying to promise more than the last. Why it works: when the market has not yet seen the bigger number, a larger claim wins attention. The limit is reached when the claim becomes unbelievable. [L0330, undated]

- *Lose up to 30 pounds in 6 weeks or get your money back.* [L0330, undated]
- *Get 60 hours of battery life, then 90, then 120 hours on the same device.* [L0330, undated]

### Headlines at sophistication stage 3

Procedure for a market that has heard every claim and every extreme:

1. Accept that repeating or exaggerating the old claim will no longer work. [L0330, undated]
2. Introduce a new mechanism that explains how the old promise is achieved now. [L0330, undated]
3. Move the emphasis from what the product does to how it works. [L0330, undated]
4. Feature the mechanism in the headline, reducing the old claim to a single short word or phrase that points to it. [L0330, undated]
5. Once the reader accepts that a new chance exists, restate the full claim in the lead paragraph to show everything they get. [L0330, undated]

[L0330, undated]

- Formula: mechanism first, claim second. The headline names the new way the product works, in a short, striking phrase. The lead paragraph restates the full promise and the benefits it brings. Why it works: a familiar claim becomes fresh and believable again when it is explained through a new process. [L0330, undated]
  - *Melts the stubborn sweat patches out of your shirts before they set, the first laundry booster with a cooling enzyme.* Lead: *fresh shirts after a full day of heat, in one wash, with no scrubbing.* [L0330, undated]
  - *The first electric kettle that heats from the bottom plate.* Lead: *the full promise of boiling a litre in under two minutes.* [L0330, undated]
- Solution-aware markets: the headline should name the unique feature or mechanism instead of only the problem. [L0353, 2023]
  - Market awareness (Schwartz's stages) decides what the ad leads with. [L0353, 2023]
  - Schwartz's scale places a solution-aware market at stage three: buyers know the problem and know that competing solutions exist. The ad should not only restate the problem but show a unique feature or mechanism that sets the product apart from the alternatives. [L0353, 2023]

### Headlines at sophistication stage 4

- In the fourth stage, a competitor has just introduced a new mechanism that is selling. Respond by elaborating on that mechanism: make it easier, quicker or surer, let it solve more of the problem, overcome old limitations or promise extra benefits. This copy eventually loses believability, so the next move is a new mechanism that the market accepts. [L0330, undated]
- When a rival launches a new mechanism for the same claim and it sells, counter by making the approach easier, quicker and surer, letting it solve more of the problem, overcoming its old limits and promising extra benefits. This keeps you competitive in markets where promises keep escalating. [L1365, 2019+]

### Headlines for unaware and resistant markets

- Introducing a new product to a prospect who wants what it does but does not yet know that the product exists:
  1. Name the desire or its solution in the headline, pinpointing the vague desire that is spreading. [L0330, undated]
  2. Prove that the solution can actually be achieved. [L0330, undated]
  3. Show that the mechanism that achieves it is contained in the product. [L0330, undated]
  - Execution matters more than mechanics here. Success needs analysis of the right market, intuition to sense a trend early and to see when it becomes profitable, and verbal creativity to give a name to what people feel but have not yet expressed. [L0330, undated]
- When the market is completely unaware or resistant, first eliminate three headline options: do not mention the price or any price reduction; do not put the product name in the headline or prominent display type; do not state directly what the product does, what desire it satisfies or what problem it solves. Each of these is either meaningless or will fail at that stage. [L0330, undated]
- At the most unaware stage, the headline must be an identification headline. It calls the market together around an emotion, attitude or situation it already feels, sells nothing directly, and exists only to get the reader into the next paragraph. Each paragraph then pulls the reader into the next, moving from identification to awareness of the problem or desire, to the realisation that a solution exists, and finally to the product. [L0330, undated]
- An identification headline must be the prospect's own headline, describing their problem and their state of mind right now. It should attract only the logical prospects and repel everyone else. It must not be a cute, funny or shocking line with no real headline behind it. [L0330, undated]
- Before writing the headline, answer three questions: what mass desire drives the market, how much the market already knows about how the product satisfies it (state of awareness), and how many similar products the market has already seen (state of sophistication). The first answer gives the force. The second and third give the position of the market relative to the product and therefore the content of the headline. [L0330, undated]
- A headline that works at one awareness level usually fails at another, and a headline that worked for a market will stop working once that market moves to a new level. Re-check the awareness level before reusing any headline. [L0330, undated]

### Avatar document

The avatar document has six required analysis sections. Ground every claim in verbatim research data. [L0039, 2026]

| Section | What to write |
|---|---|
| (1) Awareness stage | Classify on Schwartz's 5 stages using the rule that failed-method and competitor-name language signals later stages while pure "struggling" language signals Problem-Aware, then state the copy implication. [L0039, 2026] |
| (2) Core fear / fear direction | The single dominant fear underneath the surface complaint, which direction it points (future dread vs. present shame), quoted from data. [L0039, 2026] |
| (3) Failed methods | What customers tried that didn't work, phrased as "this will never work" reasoning that copy can lean on. [L0039, 2026] |
| (4) Emotional drivers and trigger moments | The specific moment or circumstance that makes them buy. [L0039, 2026] |
| (5) Their own words | Verbatim swipe list of exact phrases customers use for the problem and desired outcome, to be echoed in copy without replacing them with marketing language. [L0039, 2026] |
| (6) Angle map | Distinct angles (each = fear + mechanism), ranked by data support. [L0039, 2026] |

- In the avatar, ground every claim about the customer in verbatim data from your research. If the research is thin on a point, state that explicitly instead of inferring or inventing customer psychology. [L0039, 2026]
- Give the avatar a name, an age and a photo, then write ad copy and emails as if speaking directly to that one person. A concrete, named person makes copy more specific than a broad demographic label. [L1243, 2017]

#### Research documentation template

```
Audience Research Documentation template: (1) List all pains and desires your audience experiences. (2) List all relevant features. (3) Define your product advantages. (4) List all problems your product solves. (5) Describe the transformation-the desired outcome and how customer life improves. Keep asking 'what does that mean?' repeatedly until you reach underlying motivations such as belonging, connection, or significance. (6) Add original angle ideas from your research.
```

- Before producing any ad creative, research the target audience's deepest needs and desires, and map how the product's features and benefits deliver that outcome. Ads that list generic benefits unrelated to the audience's needs consistently underperform. [L1348, 2023]

### Avatar research and buyer groups

Units in this group come from the 2021-22 course and are marked older.

- Build a separate avatar for each distinct buyer group, because people with different problems, needs and buying behavior respond to different copy. A single category can often be split by experience level, for example beginner, casual and advanced buyers. [L0907, 2021-22]
- Write each piece of copy for the avatar that the specific product is built for, because copy aimed at a different group will not appeal. For example, a basic entry-level running shoe should be written for first-time runners, not for marathon trainees. [L0907, 2021-22]
- Record the experts, creators and personalities each avatar follows. This supports interest targeting on ad networks, since people who follow a niche expert usually share that niche's interest. [L0907, 2021-22]
- Study the social profiles and content of the experts an avatar follows, and look at what their audience likes, shares and comments on. This shows the language and interests of your customer. [L0907, 2021-22]
- Record the online spaces where the avatar spends time, such as Facebook groups, forums, fan pages, Reddit and Quora. Team members can read or join these spaces to learn the pains and desires of the market and to find product angles. [L0907, 2021-22]
- Record the emotions the customer feels right now, such as frustration, sadness or excitement. Put yourself in their position to choose the tone for your copy. [L0907, 2021-22]
- The customer avatar template comes with a filled-in example sheet. Use it to judge how much detail each field needs, but do not copy its content, because your profile must reflect your own market. [L0907, 2021-22]
- A buyer of a backyard gazebo may want to host friends and be seen as a good host, so the action is entertaining guests in an outdoor space. Recording this identity and action next to the product lets copy speak to the host's goal instead of only the structure. [L0907, 2021-22]
- Ads should be built around the audience. The first question is who the product is being sold to, and the message should follow from that audience's view. Custom ads should be written from this audience view. [L1187, 2026]

### Desires, emotions and the reason behind them

- Each desire should be paired with the emotional reason behind it, such as fear of being embarrassed, feeling unhygienic or ashamed of the home. The reason ("why") is what gives the copy its tension, so a desire without its why produces weaker messages. [L0516, 2023]
- Sell what the buyer wants, then deliver what they need. Buyers rarely identify their needs, but they describe their wants clearly. [L0378, 2019+]

#### Wants and needs sort

1. Draw two columns on a sheet of paper. Label one "Wants" and the other "Needs". [L0378, 2019+]
2. List everything the buyer says they want in the first column, and everything they actually need in the second. [L0378, 2019+]
3. Write your sales message around the wants, then deliver the needs as the actual solution. [L0378, 2019+]

[L0378, 2019+]

- A strong ad angle combines three parts: a feeling the buyer has right now (for example present self-blame rather than fear of a future problem), a mechanism that explains why earlier attempts failed without blaming the buyer, and a stated reason the angle stands apart from competitors. Before using the feeling, confirm it appears across many survey responses in the buyers' own words, otherwise it is only the seller's assumption. [L1140, 2026]
- Competitors often speak to a symptom, such as a child's behavior. An angle about how the buyer privately judges themselves, for example feeling like they failed, can stand apart if buyers repeat that feeling in the survey. [L1140, 2026]

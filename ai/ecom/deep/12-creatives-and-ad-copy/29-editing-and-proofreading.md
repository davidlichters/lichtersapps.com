### Editing and proofreading

- Have every article edited, even if you wrote it yourself. Use native English editors, who cost about $20 to $25 per hour. Let the editor help pick and vet writers, using their judgement of good writing. Then write the headline and meta description, create a custom image, and upload the article with its image and alt tag. [L0382, 2017]
- Proofread scripts and angle sheets before publishing. An example template contained spelling errors such as "Emarrassed", "prperly", "malting" and "healty". Errors in ad copy make the brand look careless and reduce trust before the viewer reaches the offer. [L0392, undated]
- Check the balance of reinforcement by rereading the finished copy a few days after writing it, when the wording looks fresh. The passage where a new angle turns into repetition is easier to spot at that distance, and any passage that brings no new angle should be cut. [L0330, undated]
- Read every sentence during editing and ask whether it makes sense and is needed. [L0925, 2021-22]
- Hire a copywriter if your own copywriting skills are weak, and practise regularly to improve conversion rates for ads and landing pages. Copywriting is the most important part of Facebook ads, because the image stops people from scrolling. [L0643, 2015]
- Print your drafts (ad variations, email sections, product angles) at a small size. Spread them on a table or floor and group them into piles by theme. Lay the pieces out in the order you want and check the flow. Type the final order into your digital file, and return to the printed pieces whenever the sequence stops working. [L1343, undated]

### Drafting process

- When learning copywriting, aim to be better than you were before, not to become the best copywriter in the world. Each additional piece of copy should be a little stronger than the previous one. [L1143, 2021-22]
- Writing copy feels difficult at first, and that is normal for beginners. The difficulty decreases with repeated practice, so keep writing even when early results are weak. [L1143, 2021-22]
- Practice procedure:
  1. Write copy for your products on a regular, ongoing basis.
  2. Test different approaches (different angles, styles or messages) across different times and different areas you write copy for.
  3. Keep applying what you learn consistently in your business, and keep improving over time.
  [L1143, 2021-22]
- Build copywriting skill through a daily routine: read one piece of sales copy actively with a pen, write one page of copy (it may be discarded, or a hand-copied page from a strong writer), and generate one idea per day by asking "what if". Repetition and pattern recognition are the main drivers of skill. [L1356, 2019+]
- Active reading means reading with a purpose: looking for what the writer is trying to communicate beneath the text, marking patterns, and noting new lines that can inspire original copy. Passive reading only absorbs the words. [L1356, 2019+]
- Direct-response copy is a demanding school for copywriters, because a single ad must make the whole sale with no help from displays or salespeople. Techniques that work under those conditions carry over well to general advertising. [L0330, undated]
- Each sentence of the ad, from the second onward, has the same single job: force the reader to read the next sentence. The ad is a chain of sentences that moves the reader from headline to product to purchase. [L0330, undated]
- Direct-response copy must provoke action by first moving the prospect emotionally and only then persuading them intellectually. The emotional work is done in the opening, because the reader gives an ad only seconds before deciding whether to keep reading. [L0756, undated]
- A strong idea is big enough to stir interest, easy to understand, immediately convincing and clearly useful to the reader. Believability matters as much as size. [L0756, undated]
- Build the copy as a sequence of checks on the reader's questions (see the Ten-Question Sequence under Sales letters). [L1365, 2019+]
- Principle of consistency across channels: the same structural approach and best practices apply to ads, emails and websites. [L0241, 2021-22]

### Slippery-slide flow

- Each sentence has one job: make the reader read the next. The first line exists only to make the reader go on to the second line. Test it by asking whether it makes leaving the page almost impossible. [L0330, undated; L0378, 2019+]
- Do not open with your name, credentials or company history unless the piece is a parody or the audience already knows you. Start with the reader's situation or with a question that opens a loop. [L0378, 2019+]
- Strong first-line pattern: name a specific person or situation, state a concrete change or loss, and leave the outcome open so the reader must continue. Examples: "Maya found out her dog had been eating the wrong food for two years on the day the vet called." and "Nobody on the boat knew the forecast had changed until the wind hit the sails." [L0378, 2019+]
- Opening with "my name is" or "I've been an expert for years" wastes the most valuable seconds of attention. Replace it with a line about what matters to the reader. [L0378, 2019+]
- Case: a long-running sales letter opened with a credentials paragraph about the author being a certified specialist with 15 years of experience. The seller removed that opening line, and conversion rose by 22%. The team then tested a new opener built around a question about a hidden problem, which lifted conversion by another 20%. The lesson: the opening should focus on the reader's world, not on the author. [L0378, 2019+]
- Desire-line pattern: use "I'm going to" plus the feeling or outcome for the reader, never "I want you to" about the seller. Examples: "I'm going to make your Saturday mornings feel unhurried again." and "I'm going to have your kitchen smelling like fresh bread by the time your guests arrive." [L0378, 2019+]
- Every consecutive line must add new information or raise stakes. Never echo the structure of the previous line. [L0039, 2026]
- In longer copy, the lead-in creates curiosity and prepares the reader for the body. The body sells by surfacing benefits and building desire until the reader wants to click through to the landing page. The call to action comes last and can be an offer or a "learn more" click, depending on how aware the audience already is. [L0516, 2023]
- For the interest stage, open with a problem the ideal customer can relate to, then agitate it and explain why it happens. The stronger the agitation, the more the reader feels the need for a solution. [L0578, 2023+]
- Create a character who resembles the ideal customer and tell their problem story, with photos to make it engaging. Readers who see themselves in the story become warm without realising they are being sold to. [L0578, 2023+]
- In a long-form editorial, end every paragraph or section with a hook that pulls the reader into the next one. Readers who stop mid-story do not reach the product pitch, so the hook matters as much as the headline. [L0578, 2023+]
- Short-paragraph trick: ask a question or make a quick statement, then answer it in a short follow-up line. Example form: a short question, a one-line answer such as "I thought so.", then a short lead-in such as "Now let me explain…". [L0340, undated]
- Open-loop (bribe): a pledge to disclose information later. Examples: telling the reader that within a moment you will demonstrate precisely how the statements just made hold up completely, or that if they stay with you, you will show how to obtain the advantages you already promised. Launch a fresh loop before the earlier one is resolved so the reader keeps going. [L1356, 2019+]
- Pattern interrupt: open by showing something the reader has not seen in other ads, such as an unusual image, document or opening line, to grab attention. Check what competitors use as pattern interrupts and avoid repeating them. [L1356, 2019+]
- Payoff lines follow a headline and give the reader slightly more detail on the headline's topic without revealing the key information. A payoff holds attention while the copy moves toward the big promise, and the big promise should be reached as soon as possible. [L1356, 2019+]
- The lead should contain one big idea that can be summarised in a single sentence (the copy's "soul" or "golden thread"). Everything in the headline, lead and body must tie back to that idea, and content that deviates from it should be removed. The soul can be vague in the first draft and sharpened during writing. [L1356, 2019+]
- Express the reader's situation in plain steps and keep one thought per sentence so each sentence moves the reader forward. [L0340, undated]

### Opening lines and lead length

- Lead length, sources differ (use the one that fits the format):
  - The opening few hundred words (roughly 100 to 600 words in a long-form sales letter) decide whether the rest of the copy is read. A weak lead loses the prospect no matter how strong the later sections are. [L0756, undated]
  - The lead is the densest part of the copy and typically covers roughly the first 500 words, ranging from 2 to 7 pages in long-form letters. If the proof for the lead's claims has not started by page 7, the lead is likely too long and should be shortened. [L1356, 2019+]
- In long-form sales copy, roughly 80% of the emotional impact comes from the first 20% of the text, so the opening deserves the most drafting and testing effort. Revising the rest rarely fixes a weak opening. [L0756, undated]
- Opening test procedure:
  1. Read only the opening of the sales copy, without the rest.
  2. Ask whether it excites the reader and creates anticipation to keep reading.
  3. If it does not, rewrite the opening before editing anything else, because a weak opening usually means the whole promotion will underperform.
  [L0756, undated]
- Two otherwise identical sales letters that differ only in their opening lead can produce response rates that differ by a factor of two or three, for example 0.5% against double or triple that. Test whole leads rather than small details to find the largest gains. [L0756, undated]
- Dominant-emotion leads: once a control winner is found for a rollout, write up to six cover tests, each with a different lead type, including benefit-led and dominant-emotion leads. Run heads-up A/B splits between the strongest results. In recent heads-up splits of this kind, dominant-emotion (indirect) leads won more often than benefit leads. [L0756, undated]
- Listing every feature and benefit in one piece of copy and hoping one lands dilutes the message and splits the reader's attention. Keep one main benefit and one core emotion per lead, and cut the rest. [L0756, undated]

### Sales letters

#### Structure

- Structure a long-form direct-response sales letter in four sections, in this order: (1) headline, (2) lead, (3) body copy, (4) offer. Keep the order, because rearranging these sections makes the copy harder to follow and loses the attention of busy, skeptical readers. [L1356, 2019+]
- Write the lead in this order:
  1. Capture attention, via a pattern interrupt or the headline.
  2. Expand on the headline with a payoff that gives more detail without giving away the offer.
  3. Establish credibility with a single line, such as a quote from an authority or a track record.
  4. State the one big promise as early as possible.
  5. Open a loop that promises information revealed later in the copy.
  [L1356, 2019+]
- Credibility in the lead can be a single line (for example, a quote from an authority figure or a credential at the start of the letter), since full credibility is saved for the body. The quote or credential should make the claim seem inevitable or predictable. [L1356, 2019+]
- The lead does not need to reveal the product or offer. In many long-form letters the product is introduced only in the final pages of the lead, after the guru or seller is introduced. The lead's job is to build hype around an opportunity or to illustrate a severe problem. The body then proves the points. [L1356, 2019+]
- Body copy: repeat this sequence for each claim: (1) address an objection, (2) make a claim that answers that objection, (3) give proof, (4) describe the benefit. Keep to this template for body copy, unlike the emotion-based approach used elsewhere. [L1356, 2019+]
- Find objections by generating them from the four core emotions and their alter egos. For each of New/Only, Easy/Anybody, Safe/Predictable and Big/Fast, ask what a skeptical reader would question, e.g. "Why should I buy from you?", "What if I don't have money?", "How do I know this will work?", "How fast will I see money?". Common objections include "Why should I act now?", "Who are you?", "How does it work?", "Is this risky?", "How much does it cost?" and "What if it doesn't work?". [L1356, 2019+]

#### Body sections (patterns)

- Guru or credibility section: follows the lead and marks the transition from lead to body. It answers "Who are you?" and "Why should I listen to you?" by showing photos with credible people, appearances on TV or news networks, or a track record of success or accurate predictions. Case studies and testimonials are usually saved for the offer. [L1356, 2019+]
- Discovery story: explains how the seller found the opportunity, answering "How do you know about this?" and conveying Only. Use a secret source, an elite network or a chance event to show the information is not available to the general public, supported with proof that the relationship exists. [L1356, 2019+]
- Key insight: the single piece of information the reader must believe for the opportunity to seem real. Back it with "signs" that the event is coming (e.g. stocks of related companies quietly rising because insiders are buying), which conveys Safe and Predictable. [L1356, 2019+]
- Layman's explanation: describes how the opportunity works in simple words anyone could repeat, like two people talking at a bar. It answers "How does it work?" and should convey the New or Easy emotion, e.g. three simple steps plus a patented or new technology. [L1356, 2019+]
- Technical explanation: uses dense jargon and scientific detail to reassure the reader that the seller understands the mechanism, even though the reader does not follow it. This conveys Safe and Predictable through expertise rather than understanding. [L1356, 2019+]
- "Better Than" section: compares the offer to the most popular item currently in the market (e.g. FAANG stocks or Bitcoin) to show it outperforms it. The claim should be backed by a quote or figure from a news source, a chart or comparison, and a second proof point. The section should convey Big, optionally paired with Safe. [L1356, 2019+]
- "Last Time Something Like This Happened" section: uses a historical case with a subhead such as "The last time something like this happened was over 25 years ago!", tells the story, and says this time the opportunity is much bigger. The historical pattern conveys Predictability. A case study with a famous name, such as Gary Halbert's "Water Ad" example, can make the pattern strong. [L1356, 2019+]

#### Ten-question sequence

Structure the letter around the reader's questions, in this order:

1. **How is this different from everything else I've seen?** State the new opportunity and make sure it is truly unique, since without uniqueness the idea is not worth testing.
2. **What's in it for me?** Open with a big promise tied to the reader's key desires, because the reader must see the benefit within seconds.
3. **How do I know this is real?** Add early proof such as historical charts, testimonials or third-party quotes, wrapped in a story.
4. **What's holding me back?** Reveal the real reason past solutions failed, found by reverse-engineering the answer to step 1, so the reader is not blamed.
5. **Who or what is to blame?** Name a common enemy that the audience already distrusts or resents, drawn from their existing beliefs.
6. **Why now?** Create urgency with an either-or choice and a real deadline or event that raises the stakes.
7. **Why should I trust you?** Establish credibility through an "I've been in your shoes" storyline, a Robin Hood storyline or an expert storyline.
8. **How does it work?** Explain the new mechanism logically, anchored in a belief the reader already holds, and wrap it in a story with credible sources.
9. **How can I get started?** Present a no-brainer offer with a large gap between stacked value and final price, plus bonuses and a guarantee.
10. **What do I have to lose?** Close with a push-pull: let the prospect choose freely among options, then remind them what they lose by doing nothing.

[L1365, 2019+]

#### Offer and close

- Use the P.S. in sales letters to restate the offer, repeat the contact details and push for action. Readers often read the P.S. before the body copy, so it should contain the strongest closing line. [L0340, undated]
- If a P.S. is used after the sign-off, summarise the offer briefly, like an abbreviated lead. [L1356, 2019+]
- Close with one strong, one-time-only offer. [L0756, undated]
- Sales-letter build procedure (lift note and opening):
  1. Write a lift note that asks a question which inverts the promise, then support it with a single-sentence story that validates it.
  2. Open the sales letter with one statement that implies the core promise.
  3. Support the statement with a short bulleted list of facts.
  4. Validate the statement with your own result.
  5. Close with one strong, one-time-only offer.
  [L0756, undated]

### Advertorials and native-style long-form

- An advertorial is an article-style pre-sell page that looks like editorial content and leads to a product page, often as a story, listicle or "how I did this" narrative. It works as a psychological bridge: visitors get drawn into the story before they see the price, so they are less likely to bounce straight from a product page. It is time-consuming to produce, so it is most worth testing for problem-solving products. [L0691, 2024]
- Test with an advertorial right away if the product suits the format, rather than only a product page, when the goal is to avoid wasting test budget on a weak funnel. The trade-off is more production time. Teams that prefer fast testing may skip this step. [L0691, 2024]
- Advertorial research and writing method:
  1. Find advertorials that already sell in your niche or an adjacent niche, using search on YouTube and similar platforms.
  2. Copy the full advertorial text and paste it into a Google Sheet.
  3. Go through it paragraph by paragraph and note what each paragraph says and what job it does in the sequence.
  4. Rewrite the text for your own product, adapting the same paragraph roles, and then test it.
  [L0691, 2024]
- Editorial page steps, with a 70/30 split of about 70% information and story and the final 30% for the product pitch:
  1. Plan the page around the 70/30 rule.
  2. Write the headline first, making it benefit-driven and attention-grabbing with a bold claim, curiosity or news angle.
  3. Interest: open with a problem the ideal customer can relate to, then agitate it and explain why it happens.
  4. Desire: explain why other solutions fail, present your product as a new approach, and add social proof with customer reviews, photos, results over time and data.
  5. Action: introduce the product and place a clear call to action that leads to the product page.
  6. Add photos throughout the text to keep the reader engaged.
  [L0578, 2023+]
- Native-style long-form ad pattern: a large primary text that reads like an article. Structure: a bold headline, a first-person story of a small symptom that grew slowly, an ordinary check-up that found nothing alarming, a turning point where the person tried the product, then the product as the answer, and a disclaimer at the end. It works because the story earns attention at the top of the funnel, and the disclaimer keeps it compliant. Two new examples: "I Kept Losing Track of My Running Times for Two Years. A Coach Found the Cause in One Session." and "My Desk Routine Had Stopped Working by Spring. A Physiotherapist Showed Me What I Was Missing." [L1164, 2026]

### Direct mail and envelope techniques

- Mail-order ads must make the full sale on a single run, so they pack the greatest number of intensification devices into one long ad. They are often frozen and repeated after three to six months. National advertising keeps the product in front of the public constantly, so it runs far more often, and its product lifespans are much longer. [L0330, undated]
- Catalog-style direct mail: open the first paragraph of each product ad with several very short sentences of two to four words, which pulls the eye down the page and keeps readers moving toward the offer. Use the same opening rhythm across product ads to keep the reader moving through the page. [L0340, undated]
- Operator note on the rhythm rule: the repeated opening rhythm applies across separate product ads. Inside one ad, every consecutive line must still escalate and must not echo the line before it. [L0340, undated; L0039, 2026]
- Envelope technique: no knowledge units were supplied for this topic.

### Core principle: one reader, one action

- Direct response copy is a writing style built to make a reader take one specific action right away. Its formula is: name the reader's problem, show the product as the answer, and end with one clear instruction. It works because every sentence pushes toward that one action rather than informing for its own sake. Example in the same pattern: *Tired of cracked phone screens after one drop? This case absorbs the impact and ships in 48 hours. Tap to see the protection.* [L1460, 2021-22]
- Each piece of copy must aim at exactly one action, chosen by its place in the funnel. On a product page the action is adding to cart and buying. In an ad it is clicking through to the website. In an email it is visiting the website. [L1460, 2021-22]
- Adding a second competing action to the same piece weakens the first. [L1460, 2021-22]
- Ads that try to make the viewer buy directly fail when the ad does not allow purchase on the spot, because the viewer must first visit the site. The ad should only earn the click, and the sale happens later on the site. [L1460, 2021-22]
- Ads do not sell the product; they sell the click. A few lines of text cannot sell a product effectively, so the ad only needs to arouse enough curiosity that the person wants to visit the page. [L1507, 2021-22]
- Words in emails, search text ads and social ads all count as copy and must be written with one defined action in mind. This includes Google search text ads and Facebook ads. [L1460, 2021-22]
- The purpose of ecommerce copy is to move the reader to the action the business wants, which in most cases is buying the product. Every headline and text block should be judged by whether it pushes toward that purchase. [L1493, 2021-22]
- Today's online copywriting methods largely come from direct-response text marketing developed from the early 1900s through the 1950s and 1960s. Before TV and the internet, mail and newspaper advertising sold almost everything through text alone, which forced writers to become very skilled. [L1523, 2021-22]

## Hooks and openings

### What the opening has to do

- A hook is the opening line in the first 1-3 seconds of a video. It stops the scroll and starts a full watch. It works by triggering one of seven psychological patterns, and it decides whether the algorithm sees a watch completion or an exit. [L0160, undated]
- The seven triggers are: direct problem relevance, comparison against current solutions, personal authority from experience, curiosity about outcomes, fear of missing out, daily necessity, and removal of cost barriers. [L0160, undated]
- The hook is arguably the most important ad element. It must stop the scroll and keep the viewer watching, and it should highlight a fact, a problem, the key benefit or the unique selling point. [L1425, undated]
- The first 3 to 5 seconds decide whether a viewer keeps watching. If the hook fails, nobody sees the rest of the ad, however good the offer. [L0707, 2026; L0777, undated]
- The first seconds must stop the scroll and show what the product does right away. Shocking, unexpected or user-generated content (UGC) can achieve this. [L0777, undated]
- Attention is only the first step. The opening must also win interest, or attention fades and is much harder to win back. [L1369, undated]
- A strong opening drives the click, not only views beyond the hook. [L0950, 2023]
- A strong ad calls out a problem or a big benefit in the first seconds, so viewers are already qualified buyers. Attention-grabbing content that has no relation to the product attracts viewers who will not buy. [L0640, 2023]
- A hook should relate to the product, the video and the viewer's own situation, and appeal to emotion. Statements viewers can instantly relate to hold attention longer and improve conversion. [L0764, 2022]
- Name the audience directly in hooks and copy, for example the profession or life situation. People outside the group keep scrolling, people inside it qualify themselves, and clicks are filtered toward likely buyers. [L1426, undated]
- The first three seconds should target one specific audience and show either the pain point or the relief. The right people stay and others skip the ad. [L0871, 2021+]
- Simple copy with a clear hook in the first seconds matters most, because the opening seconds decide whether viewers keep watching. [L1176, 2023]

### Timing windows

Sources give different time values. Each value applies to a different element of the opening.

| Element | Value | When it applies | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hook line, length | 1-3 seconds, ideally 1.5-2 seconds | The hook sentence or on-screen hook itself | [L0764, 2022; L0160, undated] |
| Hook placement | Within the first 2-3 seconds, early and fast, never in the middle or after 3 seconds | Where the hook must appear | [L0764, 2022] |
| Opening window | First 3-5 seconds | The full hook period, tested as one unit: problem, face or first proof | [L0902, undated; L0280, undated; L0638, 2023; L0648, undated] |
| Problem intro | Keep it short | Before the solution appears | [L0640, 2023] |
| Solution or benefit | Within about 3-5 seconds | Reach the benefit or solution inside this window | [L0640, 2023; L0067, 2023] |
| Product on screen | Within the first five seconds | Unless the video is a strong unboxing | [L1377, 2022+] |
| Offer on screen (TikTok) | Within the first three seconds | So the audience recognises that a product is being sold | [L1177, 2023] |
| Demonstration (video ads) | First three seconds show how the product works, then installation or use | Video ads that lead with the product | [L0562, undated] |

- The hook must appear within the first 2 to 3 seconds of the video. [L0764, 2022]
- A hook lasts 1 to 3 seconds, ideally 1.5 to 2 seconds. [L0764, 2022]
- The first 3 to 5 seconds are the hook. Test that window separately from the rest of the video. [L0902, undated]
- The first 3 to 5 seconds should show the pain point, the face or the problem, and then introduce the product. [L0638, 2023]
- Reach the solution within about 3 to 5 seconds. A product introduction that takes around 10 seconds loses viewers before the benefit appears. [L0640, 2023]
- If an ad takes 10 seconds or more to introduce the product, jump straight to demonstrating the benefit (the action) within 3 to 5 seconds, then introduce the product. [L0067, 2023]
- Show the product in the first five seconds. Videos that delay the product tend to underperform unless they are strong unboxings. This applies to custom and sourced content. [L1377, 2022+]
- On TikTok, show the offer within the first three seconds, because the audience needs to recognise a product is being sold, otherwise attention drops. [L1177, 2023]
- For video ads, the first three seconds must show how the product works as the attention grabber. After those three seconds, show how it is installed or used. [L0562, undated]

### Problem first or product first

Sources disagree on what the first frame should show.

- **Problem first:** Show the problem the product solves, not its features or specifications. Do not lead with features such as "battery lasts 3 hours" or "rechargeable". Show the dirty lawn first, then the clean lawn, so customers understand how the product helps. [L0280, undated]
- **Problem first, for problem-solving products:** Bold claims and pain points are the most useful for product-led ads. The first seconds should show the audience's problem, the face, or the problem before the product appears. [L0398, 2023+; L0638, 2023]
- **Result or demonstration first:** Show the working product in the first three seconds (video ads), or show what the product does right away. [L0562, undated; L0777, undated]
- **Product in first five seconds:** Delayed products tend to underperform unless the video is a strong unboxing. [L1377, 2022+]
- **Use the problem or the demonstration:** Both are valid. Apply the problem-first rule to features-heavy or problem-led products, and the demonstration rule when the video is a product demo. The sources give no further conditions beyond the 10-second rule in [L0067, 2023] and the unboxing exception in [L1377, 2022+]. [L0280, undated; L0562, undated; L1377, 2022+; L0067, 2023]

### Hook categories and choosing one

- Three common hook categories work for Facebook video: [L0398, 2023+]

| Category | What it does | Best use |
|---|---|---|
| Bold claim | A direct, specific statement about the product (long proven) | Product-led ads |
| Pain point | Shows the problem the audience has | Problem-solving products |
| Suspense | Builds curiosity and entertains the right audience at once (TikTok-style) | Short-form feeds |

- Bold claims and pain points are the most useful for product-led ads. Suspense is strong for short-form feeds. [L0398, 2023+]
- Classify each hook by the 13-pattern set. [L0613, 2026]
  1. Claim size (for example an exact monthly income figure)
  2. Claim speed (a fast result)
  3. Authority figure
  4. Before-and-after
  5. Comparison to rivals
  6. Removal of limitations (for example "even if you have no experience")
  7. Question
  8. Information offer
  9. Newness
  10. Exclusivity
  11. Belief challenge
  12. Direct call-out to a specific person
  13. Identity-based hook
  [L0613, 2026]

### Problem and pain hooks

- **Problem identification and solution:** State the specific pain point your audience experiences, then introduce the product as the fix. Formula: "[If you / Stop scrolling if] you [specific pain]," then offer what fixes it. It works because viewers who have that exact problem recognise themselves and stop to see the solution. [L0160, undated]
  - "Stop scrolling if you're tired of planners you never actually use."
  - "If you waste 30 minutes every morning choosing an outfit, this changes everything."
  [L0160, undated]
- **Problem-first hook formula:** Name the viewer's exact problem in the first sentence, using the words the target customer uses. Add "this is for you" or an equivalent so the right person keeps watching. The viewer identifies with the problem within a second and stops scrolling. [L0392, undated]
  - "If your kitchen floor is covered in crumbs every single morning, then this is for you."
  - "If your car smells like wet dog every time you drive, then this is for you."
  [L0392, undated]
- **Problem-first, product as direct fix:** Name the customer's everyday problem in the first line, then present the product as the direct fix. The viewer recognises their situation and understands the benefit at once. [L0562, undated]
  - "Tired of cold feet at your desk? Our heated foot warmer keeps them toasty all day."
  - "Sick of your car windows freezing at 7 am? This quick-defrost cover clears the glass in seconds."
  [L0562, undated]
- **Problem plus benefit with a visible symptom:** Start with a question or statement that names a specific problem and promises a benefit for a named body area or use case, then show the product doing that job immediately. [L0657, undated]
  - "Still waking up with puffy eyes? Watch this gel reduce swelling in 10 seconds."
  - "Worried about slipping on wet tile? See how these shower slippers grip the floor."
  [L0657, undated]
- **Symptom-first:** Name the visible problem the buyer already notices, state what it costs them in time, money or appearance, then close with a question that invites them to recognise it. Buyers recognise symptoms faster than root causes. [L0701, 2015]
  - "Running shoes soaked before the first mile? Stop buying new pairs every month."
  - "Jackets stained after every commute? Your clothes deserve a fix that lasts."
  [L0701, 2015]
- **Problem-solution opening:** Lead with a direct question about the customer's pain ("Sick of the [specific problem]?"), then position the brand as the answer with an adjective that appeals to customer psychology, such as "wholistic", "calming" or "natural". It works because it names the problem state, triggers recognition and offers an escape path at once. [L0313, 2026]
  - "Tired of restless nights? This is your natural sleep solution."
  - "Frustrated with back tension? Meet your pain-free workday."
  [L0313, 2026]
- **Contrast question:** Open with a question or contrast that names the viewer's exact problem, then point to a different path than the one they are already on. The hook is judged by how fast it makes the viewer feel seen. [L0374, 2026]
  - "Are you still getting breakouts on your back after every workout? Here is the two-minute routine that finally stopped mine."
  - "If your running shoes still hurt after 5 km, don't buy another pair yet. Check this first."
  [L0374, 2026]
- **Frustration and "that is me" statement:** Name a frustration the target viewer already has, connect it to the product as the fix, and phrase it as a short statement that makes the viewer think "that is me". [L0764, 2022]
  - "Still scrubbing grease off the stovetop every night? Watch what happens in two seconds."
  - "Tired of your car smelling like wet gym bags after every rainy trip?"
  [L0764, 2022]
- **Specific everyday problem, number and action:** Open with one specific everyday problem the buyer already notices, state a fix with a number, then close with one action. Naming the exact situation makes the product feel relevant instead of generic. [L0794, 2026]
  - "Chargers dying by week three? Our braided cable is rated for 10,000 bends, so order a pack of two today."
  - "Boots soaking through on every wet walk? Our membrane liner keeps feet dry for 8 hours, so get the pair before the weekend."
  [L0794, 2026]
- **Relatable-problem hook:** Open with a question or statement about a problem the viewer experiences. Within seconds, show the product solving that problem on screen. Viewers who recognise themselves stay to watch. [L0795, undated]
  - "Tired of wiping muddy paw prints off the floor after every walk?"
  - "Hate scrubbing burnt cheese off your pan every night?"
  [L0795, undated]
- **Problem-specific creative in the customer's words:** Name the exact problem the audience has in their own words, as found in reviews, then offer the product as the fix. [L0999, 2023]
  - "Dog hair on your bed every morning? This brush lifts it out of wiry fur in one pass."
  - "Still stuck with cat hair on the sofa? This brush pulls it out before it spreads."
  [L0999, 2023]
- **Owner-group formula:** Name a specific owner group, describe the everyday annoyance they recognise, point to the visible symptom, and add a direct call to act. The viewer sees their own situation in the first line. [L0993, undated]
  - "Cat owners with a sofa full of fur, this roller lifts it off in one pass."
  - "Border Collie owners whose car seats are covered in hair, this attachment cleans them in minutes."
  [L0993, undated]
- **Pain-point hook:** Open with a relatable daily struggle, name the feeling it causes, then reveal the product as the fix in one sentence. [L1427, 2023+]
  - "Dreading the wait for your kettle while you still need a second cup? Here is a quick fix that works in any mug."
  - "Tired of brewing bitter coffee that tastes flat every morning? This ready-to-use coffee gives you a smooth cup in seconds."
  [L1427, 2023+]
- **Reassure, explain, act:** Open with a relatable pain point framed as a question, reassure the viewer that the problem is not their fault, name one mechanism of the product in plain words, and end with a simple call to action. The viewer recognises the problem first, so the product reads as a solution, not a pitch. [L1479, 2026]
  - "Stuck in an afternoon slump again? You're not lazy, your energy may just need support. One daily capsule helps keep focus steady."
  - "Dog still pulling on every walk? It's not you. This harness shifts pulling tension to the chest so walks feel calm."
  [L1479, 2026]
- **Precise customer words:** Open the hook with the exact words your target customers use to describe the problem in forums, reviews or comments. Only someone with that problem feels addressed. The reader sees their own inner voice in the first line. [L0772, 2026]
  - "My heel cracks every winter even after three creams, and I am sick of it."
  - "Every shirt is soaked by lunch, and nothing I have bought has fixed it."
  [L0772, 2026]
- **Pain-point hook over free stock footage:** Find a free stock clip that shows the frustration, convert it to grayscale, and overlay a caption that names the pain point, plus a cross mark over the clip. Viewers recognise their own problem within seconds. [L0398, 2023+]
  - Grayscale clip of a dripping tap, caption: "Still mopping up the same puddle every morning?"
  - Grayscale clip of a cluttered desk, caption: "Losing 20 minutes a day hunting for chargers?"
  [L0398, 2023+]
- **Warning hook:** Warn the viewer off a product, or ask them to check one detail before they act. It makes the viewer feel protected from a mistake, which holds attention longer than a plain product claim. [L1164, 2026]
  - "Hold off on buying [product] until you check the ingredient list on the back."
  - "Stop reordering [product] before you read the renewal terms in the checkout."
  [L1164, 2026]
- **Desire-result hook:** Name a result the buyer wants, then name the product or ingredient as the way to get it, in the form "if you want [result] you need [product]". The viewer sees the benefit in the first seconds. [L0901, 2026]
  - "If you want deeper sleep without feeling groggy, you need magnesium glycinate at night."
  - "If your knees feel stiff every morning, you need this collagen drink."
  [L0901, 2026]
- **Problem and relief in three seconds:** Start with the viewer's specific problem and show the relief within the first three seconds. Viewers with the same problem recognise it and keep watching, and others leave, so the ad reaches a qualified audience. [L0871, 2021+]
  - "Tired of waking up with a stiff neck? Watch what happens after 60 seconds on this."
  - "Working from a laptop all day and feeling that back ache again? Here is the stretch that gives relief."
  [L0871, 2021+]
- **Relief sound or reaction:** A relief sound or reaction while using the product works as a recurring hook pattern. Keep the same voice and actions across ads and change only the camera angle. [L0871, 2021+]
- **Testimonials in the problem hook (Pain/Agitate/Solve):** In the problem description hook, use customer testimonies or direct quotes. This gives social proof at the opening, before the product enters as the solution. [L0258, undated]

### Claim, benefit and transformation hooks

- **Challenge the standard, offer a better alternative:** State what people currently believe or do, then immediately introduce a different or better way. It creates doubt about the current approach and positions the product as the smarter choice. [L0160, undated]
  - Formula: "Everyone says [common belief], but [your approach is better]" or "Don't [current solution], [do this instead]."
  - "Everyone says drink more water for energy, but here's what actually works."
  - "Don't buy those expensive noise-canceling headphones-try this instead for a quarter of the price."
  [L0160, undated]
- **Outcome or hidden insight:** Promise a specific result or reveal a non-obvious reason, but do not explain it in the hook. Formula: "Here's [the secret / the real reason / what I discovered] about [topic]" or "How I [achieve specific result]," then pause. Curiosity and the wish to know HOW keep the viewer watching. [L0160, undated]
  - "Here's the real reason your productivity hack isn't working."
  - "How I sleep 2 hours less and wake up more rested (and it's not what you think)."
  [L0160, undated]
- **Transformation hook:** State that the product changed the speaker's space or health, then name the result. The viewer sees a before and after before the product is explained. A visible result creates curiosity and gives proof before any claim is made. [L0392, undated]
  - "This is how my car went from covered in dust to spotless in one week."
  - "This is how a single bottle changed my morning routine and gave me back my energy."
  [L0392, undated]
- **Bold claim:** Open with one specific, strong statement about the product result, so the right viewer relates at once. A direct claim filters the audience in the first second. [L0398, 2023+]
  - "This pan fries eggs without a drop of oil."
  - "This charger refills a phone in the time it takes to make coffee."
  [L0398, 2023+]
- **Bold claim with a payoff later:** Open with a bold claim or surprising statement that makes viewers expect a payoff, such as a shocking reveal or a question they need answered. The payoff must come later in the video, which keeps viewers to the end. [L1384, 2022]
  - Example A: a tiny change in the kitchen that saved a family a surprising amount each month.
  - Example B: nobody expected this result until the final second of the test.
  [L1384, 2022]
- **Surprising cost or loss:** Name a surprising cost or loss tied to an everyday situation, then present the product as the fix. It grabs attention with a concrete problem the viewer recognises. [L0638, 2023]
  - "Commuters lose hours each month to tangled cables. This organiser keeps every charger in one place."
  - "Gym goers waste money on bottles that leak. This sealed bottle stays dry in any bag."
  [L0638, 2023]
- **Benefit-led hook:** The hook states the target audience and problem, then creates curiosity, and the product benefit follows.
  - Ergonomic mouse: "Office workers, never ignore these three signs of wrist strain. Wrist pain often starts small, and most people only notice it when it's too late. Our curved mouse keeps your hand relaxed through an entire workday."
  - Sleep mask: "Light sleepers, never ignore these three signs of a bad night's rest. Most sleep problems start with a stray light you barely notice. Our blackout mask blocks 100% of light so you wake up rested."
  [L1425, undated]
- **Budget-conscious solution:** Acknowledge the cost barrier directly, then position the product as solving both the need and the price concern. [L0160, undated]
  - "Want luxury skincare without the luxury price tag? Here's how."
  - "Designer jeans energy on a thrift store budget."
  [L0160, undated]
- **Daily use or essential status:** Highlight frequency or category status to show the product is proven and essential, not a trend. Daily use suggests reliability and builds confidence in real value. [L0160, undated]
  - Formula: "[Number] products I [use daily / cannot live without / use every morning]" or "My must-have [product category] I use without fail."
  - "Three tools I use before 9 AM that actually change my day."
  - "The one item in my skincare routine I've used for five years straight."
  [L0160, undated]
- **Authority through extensive testing:** Share your personal journey or quantify the scope of your testing (every, all, tested X number, daily, without fail). Testing builds trust, and viewers believe you have done due diligence. [L0160, undated]
  - Formula: "I tried [number or scope], and here's what I kept" or "One [product] I use [frequency] is."
  - "I tested 23 coffee makers this year and kept only one."
  - "The one desk lamp I use every single workday and will never replace."
  [L0160, undated]
- **Trusted source and numbered list:** A hook that credits a trusted source, such as a viral trend or a group of people, with a purchase decision adds social proof in the first seconds. A hook that promises a numbered list of reasons sets a clear, quick-to-scan promise. [L1335, 2023+]
  - "Busy parents keep switching to this, here is why."
  - "Five reasons your kitchen needs this before the weekend."
  [L1335, 2023+]

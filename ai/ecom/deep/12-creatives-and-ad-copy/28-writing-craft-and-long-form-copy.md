## Writing craft and long-form copy

### Voice and stance

- Write ad copy as if you are telling a friend about an amazing product you just bought. Never seem pushy. Let the customer make their own decision rather than trying to convince them. [L0206, undated]
- Blog copy should sound like a friend giving a recommendation, with short words, short sentences, short paragraphs and plenty of white space. Images also help readers stay engaged. [L0382, 2017]
- Use plain, direct language in the register a good salesperson would use face to face. Avoid literary style, slogans and attention-seeking devices, because overly polished writing draws attention away from the offer and triggers resistance. [L1261, undated]
- Before keeping a line, ask whether the same wording would help a salesperson sell the product face to face. If it would not, cut it, because clever wordplay, slogans and performance-style copy pull attention away from the offer. [L1261, undated]
- Inform and persuade; do not entertain. Ads target prospects making a serious spending decision, and readers come for information, not amusement. [L1261, undated]
- Avoid a transactional tone. Lead with what the customer gains and how the brand adds value, because buyers reject overly transactional brands. [L1348, 2023]
- Humour and blunt tone suit products with a casual, direct brand voice. They can backfire on premium or high-end products, so match tone to price point and audience. [L0524, 2023]
- For a serious product, an informational and scientific tone works better than entertainment. Celebrity or humorous articles brought traffic, but far fewer relevant buyers than direct informational articles did. [L0382, 2017]
- Write for new prospects only. Existing customers have already decided and seldom read ads, so spend space on them only if it can be done in the headline. [L1261, undated]
- Include every important claim in each ad. A reader sees an ad once and may need a different argument than the one in the headline, so do not spread claims across a series of ads expecting readers to connect them. [L1261, undated]
- Long, complete copy sells more than short copy for products that require consideration, because the reader decides in one reading and needs every important claim. A complete story is more persuasive than a single slogan or short claim. [L1261, undated]
- Clever, cute or award-seeking ads that are not built around persuading the buyer waste budget. Before approving an ad, ask whether it moves a specific reader to a specific action. [L0340, undated]
- Message clarity comes first. If the reader does not understand the message, no level of evidence or style will persuade. Disorganised or misread ads can also harm the business over the long term. [L0340, undated]
- Build connection through a personal story related to the reader's situation. Use it when you start in an industry to give context, and show relatable attributes of yourself or the brand. This is foundational for the Know, Like, Trust phase. [L0196, 2020+]
- Make stories about the listener, not the seller. Every audience relates a story back to their own life, so the story should give them a situation they recognise. [L0378, 2019+]
- Tell the stories you least want to share, especially emotional, vulnerable or embarrassing ones. Readers identify strongly with shared experiences, and these stories tend to produce the highest response. [L0378, 2019+]
- Procedure for finding the personal stories that sell your product:
  1. Write a list of ten personal moments that were embarrassing, painful or hard to talk about.
  2. Mark the ones you would least want to share publicly.
  3. For each marked story, note the feeling a buyer would have in a similar situation.
  4. Choose the story whose feeling matches the feeling your product addresses.
  5. Write it in the first person, focusing on what happened to you and what you learned.
  6. End by linking the lesson from the story to one step the reader can take.
  [L0378, 2019+]
- Show the rough edges of your product. Perfection can make a message feel unbelievable, while honestly named weaknesses build trust when framed as acceptable trade-offs. [L0378, 2019+]
- Educate before you sell when the audience is unaware. Explain the problem and its consequences before offering the product. Trust signals such as before-and-after photos, money-back claims and real customer proof make the product claim believable. [L0515, 2023]
- Brand guideline document for writers, in five sections:
  1. Resource: what the business sells, why it sells it, who the company is, and why the reader should care.
  2. Guidelines: style rules such as short words, short sentences and short paragraphs, written casually as if speaking face to face.
  3. Key factors: required items per article, such as a minimum word count, three links to external sites, and using the keyword a set number of times, plus SEO basics that editors check.
  4. Ground rules: topics writers must avoid, for example health claims that a product cures a disease, which readers and regulators will not accept.
  5. Examples of articles that follow the guidelines.
  [L0382, 2017]

### Voice guide
Prompt for writing the voice guide, to be used unchanged:

```text
Given high-performing ads and a diagnosed awareness stage, extract the consistent tone and voice across them so every future ad maintains the same character. Write a guide covering the single core reason this voice works for that specific audience stage, the sentence mechanics that create the voice (length, rhythm, contractions, repetition, subordination), recurring word choices and registers that work or break the spell, whether the stance is peer-level or authoritative, how the winning ads open (and what opening patterns dominate), and a one-line test any sentence can pass or fail.
```
[L0039, 2026]

- Pull every rule from patterns actually present in your winning ads, not from generic copywriting advice. Quote short fragments from the ads as proof for every rule stated. [L0039, 2026]
- The Voice Guide has six required sections, in this order:

| # | Section | Required content |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | PRIORITY RULE | The single core insight about why this voice works for the identified awareness stage, in 3-4 sentences. Everything else defers to it. [L0039, 2026] |
| 1 | SENTENCE-LEVEL RULES | Mechanics of sentence length, subordination, declarative tone, contractions, repetition and rhythm, with banned-to-rewritten example pairs pulled from real ads. [L0039, 2026] |
| 2 | VOCABULARY | Recurring word registers, and words that would break the spell. [L0039, 2026] |
| 3 | NARRATIVE STANCE | Whether the voice is authoritative and outward, or peer-level and confessional. State it as a fixed rule with its failure mode (inverting it). [L0039, 2026] |
| 4 | HOOK RULES | Recurring opening moves and entry-point variety. Ban any opener formula that repeats across ads. [L0039, 2026] |
| 5 | THE TEST | A one-line gut check applied to every sentence, e.g. "Would a slick copywriter write this? If yes, rewrite it." [L0039, 2026] |

### Specificity and proof

- Lead with the reader's outcome, not the company's factory, staff, size or equipment. Most readers filter every message by what it does for them, and describing the business rarely moves anyone to buy. [L0340, undated]
- Use mental-movie copy: replace vague verbs and nouns with specific visual ones so the reader can see the product in use. Build each sentence in steps: a vague action, then a clearer action, then a specific place and object, then a sensory or sequential detail. Example of the ladder: "Set the water heater to 60 degrees, step into the shower, and feel the first warm rush on your neck." Write new lines in this same way for your own product. [L0340, undated]
- Use positive visual adjectives to describe the product's result, not just its features. Every word that does not create an image lowers the chance that the reader forms a picture of the benefit. [L0340, undated]
- Use colourful language, metaphors, similes and poignant stories to break out of generic marketing. Balance gusto with substance, and avoid flowery language for its own sake so the marketing does not feel like marketing. [L0196, 2020+]
- Concrete examples persuade more than statistics, because they trigger self-demonstration, where the reader imagines using the product. Lead with stories and examples, and add statistics as support, in a proportion set by the product. [L0340, undated]
- Recommended balance by product type:

| Product type | Recommended balance |
|---|---|
| Beer | Mostly emotional imagery of people and good times, minimal statistics [L0340, undated] |
| Cars | Lead with examples, add statistics on performance, safety or efficiency for the buyers who care [L0340, undated] |
| Laser printers | Mostly statistics: paper capacity, resolution, ink life, monthly duty cycle [L0340, undated] |
| Landscaping services | Examples about beauty, neighbour perception and convenience [L0340, undated] |
| Gym memberships | Photos of attractive members, before-and-after results and testimonials; statistics only as a secondary detail [L0340, undated] |

- A rhetorical question is a statement phrased as a question, and it can make the reader think about the claim. Research on persuasion is mixed, but rhetorical questions reliably help retention when used to emphasise a point rather than to argue. [L0340, undated]
- Use "imagine", "what if" and "think about" to stop the reader's scanning and move them into picturing the outcome. These triggers take the reader out of their current state and into thinking about what the copy describes. Examples: "Imagine finishing your run and still feeling like you could go another mile." and "What if your kitchen clean-up took five minutes instead of forty?" [L0396, 2021-22]
- Use definite, specific words such as proper names, concrete nouns and precise verbs instead of vague terms like "someone" or "something". Specific words reduce the work the reader must do to understand the message. [L0340, undated]
- Limit adjectives and describing words, especially when describing a product. [L0925, 2021-22]
- Write the mechanism section in a conversational tone with short sentences and short paragraphs, and use "you" and "your" frequently. Write as if talking to one person, because this works better than formal product text. [L0465, 2021-22]
- Copywriting principles stay the same across formats. Ads, emails and web pages follow the same structural approach and best practices. [L0241, 2021-22]
- Copy strategy target. A strong copywriting target combines clarity, implementability, grounding in reality and a defined timeframe. Model statement:

```text
Using this [email/campaign/sales page/webinar/ad], I will persuade my audience that [named feature] works better than their existing solution for [named goal]. My proof: tangible data, documented examples, and resonance with xyz emotional drivers.
```
[L0196, 2020+]

### Readability

*Note: the one source units below are from 2021-2022 and count as older advice. Where the 2021-2022 advice and the one source or one source material differ, both values are shown with the context in which each applies.*

- Readability is measured with the Flesch Reading Ease formula, which runs from 1 to 100, where higher scores are easier. Microsoft Word shows this score in its readability statistics. Check every ad, sales letter, email and landing page before publishing. [L0340, undated]
- Reading Ease score to school grade level:

| Flesch score | School grade level |
|---|---|
| 0-30 | College graduate |
| 30-50 | College |
| 50-60 | 10th to 12th grade |
| 60-70 | 8th and 9th grade |
| 70-80 | 7th grade |
| 80-90 | 6th grade |
| 90-100 | 5th grade |

  [L0340, undated]

- Readability targets for ad copy (Flesch Reading Ease framework): average sentence length of about 11 words, mention of people such as he, she or a name about 14 times per 100 words, and 70 to 80 percent of words that are one-syllable words. [L0340, undated]
- Flesch-Kincaid target: a score of 6 or 7, which corresponds to a reading level of roughly sixth or seventh grade. Read against the table above, a grade of 6-7 sits at a Flesch Reading Ease of about 70-90. [L0925, 2021-22; L0340, undated]
- Sample scores: "John loves Mary" scores 92; "John has a profound affection for Mary" scores 67; a long, wordy version with many hard words scores 32. Each added complexity measurably lowers the score, so simplify words and sentences when a score is too low. [L0340, undated]
- Readability checkers: the Flesch-Kincaid Readability Score, the Hemingway App, Grammarly and Readability. The Flesch-Kincaid score is built into Microsoft Word. Hemingway or Grammarly serve the same purpose on a Mac. Grammarly also finds spelling and grammar errors and sentences that could be shortened, and its premium version gives more features. [L0925, 2021-22]
- Other readability formulas exist: the Fog Index estimates the years of education a reader needs, the SMOG formula from 1969 estimates grade level from long words, and the Forcast formula was built for technical forms and manuals. A single score such as Flesch is enough for most ad copy, so use the others only when a specific audience or document type calls for them. [L0340, undated]
- Ads that are reread or studied only rarely need to be simpler, because most readers skim and do not work hard to decode the message. Write for quick scanning. [L0340, undated]
- Write for an average reader with average or lower reading comprehension, not for the most educated person, because most visitors do not have a high command of the language. [L0925, 2021-22]

### Simplicity and plain language

- Explain it so a six-year-old can understand. If you cannot explain it simply, you do not understand it well enough. Revise first drafts to simplify and clarify the message. Simple is good; simplistic, which means talking down, is bad. The rule may be broken to prove a point, but use this with caution. [L0196, 2020+]
- Oversimplifying copy until it sounds condescending is a mistake. Explain in everyday language without losing respect for the reader's intelligence. [L0196, 2020+]
- Lead by translation: distil complexities into everyday language. Clarity matters most. [L0196, 2020+]
- Grammar matters, but not that much. Marketing copy is about getting the point across, and core function often overrides form. Casual is fine if the audience is casual. Simplicity matters more than perfect grammar, and formalities distance readers. [L0196, 2020+]
- Use short, simple words instead of formal substitutes. Plain words like "tired" or "hungry" outperform inflated synonyms, and pompous phrasing reads as complicated and slows persuasion. [L0340, undated]
- Use short sentences. Long sentences are harder to make sense of, and short sentences are easier to read and are read more often. [L0925, 2021-22]
- Express only one thought in each sentence, and let the next sentence carry the next thought. Aim for about 11 words per sentence, varying length so the copy sounds natural. [L0340, undated]
- Write the way you would speak to a single person sitting next to you. Use plain, direct language and avoid stiff, overly formal phrasing. [L0925, 2021-22]
- Use the vocabulary of your target customer. Technical or formal language is appropriate only when the audience is itself a trade or expert group, such as scientists or engineers. For general consumers, avoid supplier and manufacturer jargon and explain terms in simple words. [L0925, 2021-22]
- Use plain, to-the-point wording with no flashy or flowery language. Short sentences that get straight to the point keep the text readable. [L0580, 2021-22]
- Remove flowery language and fluff, meaning any words or sentences that add no value. Check each sentence during editing by asking whether it makes sense and is needed. Brain-dump drafts are fine, but they must be edited. [L0925, 2021-22]
- Format and simplify a product description in this order:
  1. Increase the font size to at least 18pt, keeping the same font.
  2. Break long paragraphs into shorter ones of about two lines each.
  3. Shorten overlong sentences and rework the sentence structure where needed, so that sentences are short.
  4. Remove fluff, flowery language and unneeded adjectives.
  5. Replace jargon with plain words.
  6. Test the result with a readability tool such as Flesch-Kincaid, Hemingway or Grammarly, and aim for a score of 6 or 7.
  [L0925, 2021-22]
- Example: a dense product description with a font size of about 12pt, very long paragraphs and four-line sentences was improved only by raising the font to 18pt and splitting paragraphs into roughly two-line blocks, without changing any words. The text became noticeably easier to read, which shows that formatting alone changes readability. Sentences of four lines still need rewriting. [L0925, 2021-22]
- After setting font size and paragraph length, break long product copy further with bullet points and short headings so the page is easier to scan. Keep every fact and claim the same while doing this. Sentences that still run across several lines need a separate rewrite into shorter sentences. [L0925, 2021-22]

### Length and layout

- Use a minimum font size of 18pt for web copy such as product pages, long sales pages and content pages. Never go below 18pt, and 20pt can be used on text-only sales pages. Larger text is also needed because most traffic now comes from mobile devices. [L0925, 2021-22]
- Use one font family throughout a piece of copy. Mixing several fonts in a small section makes the text hard to read. [L0925, 2021-22]
- Keep paragraphs to two lines or fewer most of the time. Occasional longer paragraphs are acceptable. [L0925, 2021-22]
- Short paragraphs create white space, which helps the eye separate elements and scan the page with less strain. This makes the reader more likely to keep reading. Dense blocks of text, as found in printed books, are harder to read on screens. [L0925, 2021-22]
- Limit the length of regular paragraphs in direct-response text to about four or five short sentences, so the page reads quickly. [L0340, undated]
- Copy length should match the amount needed to communicate the message, not a fixed word count. Long copy works when it has quality, and short copy works for simple products that need only a few strong points. Length is a matter of quality, not quantity, and this applies to conversion copy, not SEO copy. [L0925, 2021-22]
- Length rules by context. The sources differ, so apply each value where it fits:

| Context | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Paragraphs, general | Two lines or fewer most of the time | [L0925, 2021-22] |
| Paragraphs, ad copy | One to two lines maximum | [L0241, 2021-22] |
| Paragraphs, direct response | Four or five short sentences maximum | [L0340, undated] |
| Thought-command section | One or two paragraphs, with short sentences and short lines | [L0580, 2021-22] |
| Story section | Five or six paragraphs if engaging | [L0580, 2021-22] |
| Considered purchase | Long, complete copy | [L1261, undated] |
| Simple product | Short copy with a few strong points | [L0925, 2021-22] |

- Ad length depends on three factors: how much copy is needed to build desire to its full strength, how much is needed to make the buyer feel comfortable and flattered and see the product fitting into their life, and how much is needed to overcome existing beliefs and make the claims believable. [L0330, undated]
- Format ad copy in short, simple sentences in paragraphs of one to two lines maximum. Make the call to action stand out and visible. Use only one call to action per ad. [L0241, 2021-22]

### Banned patterns and checklist

Checklist prompt, to be applied before finishing copy:

```text
Compile a checklist of forbidden patterns and language that a writer applies before finishing copy. This includes structural problems (stacking false contrasts, using generic opening formulas, starting with rhetorical devices), the parallel-repetition trap where consecutive lines repeat the same structure to fake emphasis instead of escalating, dead transition phrases, hedging language that weakens conviction, and formatting crutches like excessive emoji or punctuation.
```
[L0039, 2026]

The Banned List has five required sections, in this order:

1. **Banned structural patterns.** False-contrast stacks such as "It's not X. It's not Y. It's Z." Rhetorical section-openers. Generic "Imagine if" openers. Each comes with a bad example and a fix. [L0039, 2026]
2. **The parallel repetition stack (highest priority).** Repeating the same opener or skeleton across consecutive lines to fake emphasis. Define it with 5-6 prohibited examples and explain why it fails: it is mechanical and signals AI-written copy. Replace it with the Escalation Principle, where each line adds new information or raises stakes. [L0039, 2026]
   - The Escalation Principle: in ad copy, every consecutive line must add new information or raise stakes. It must never echo or repeat the structure of the previous line. Repeating the same opener or skeleton across consecutive lines creates mechanical, AI-like copy and signals fake emphasis rather than genuine momentum. [L0039, 2026]
3. **Banned transition and filler phrases.** Dead connectives such as "Let's dive in," "Here's the thing," "At the end of the day," and "But wait, there's more." [L0039, 2026]
4. **Banned qualifiers and hedging.** Weak words such as "might," "could potentially," "often" and "usually" are replaced with absolutes plus proof. [L0039, 2026]
5. **Banned formatting crutches.** Emoji overload, exclamation overuse and dense paragraph blocks, each with its own rule. [L0039, 2026]

- Repetition for emphasis, one source view: anaphora (repetition at the start of sequential lines) and epistrophe (repetition at the end) drive home points. Examples include "I have a dream…I have a dream…" (MLK) and "We shall fight…we shall fight…" (Churchill). Use them to break copy into digestible bits, make related information rhythmic, and repeat core arguments. [L0196, 2020+]
- Repetition, reconciled: the one source rule and the one source anaphora advice conflict on the surface. Operator reading: apply the Escalation test to every repeated line. If the repetition adds new content or raises the stakes, it is anaphora. If it only copies the skeleton of the line before it, it is the banned parallel stack. [L0039, 2026; L0196, 2020+]
- Rhetorical questions, reconciled: a rhetorical question used to emphasise a point helps retention [L0340, undated]. Do not open a section or a piece of copy with a rhetorical device, and do not use a rhetorical section-opener [L0039, 2026].
- Avoid FAQ sections in sales letters. They break the story flow, repeat material already covered and remove the seller's control of the conversation. [L1356, 2019+]
- Avoid "Imagine if" openers and generic opening formulas. [L0039, 2026]

### Build the promise from the four core emotions

| Core emotion | Alter ego | Signal words | Ids |
|---|---|---|---|
| New | Only | New: Breakthrough, Revolutionary, Discovery, Game Changing, Disruptive, NEW, Announcing, Revealed, Breaking. Only: Exclusive, Patented, Only here, Proprietary | [L1356, 2019+] |
| Easy | Anybody | Easy, Anybody, Simple, 1-2-3, Push-button, Automatic, Fool-proof, Step-by-step, System, All you have to do is, Simply follow the steps, You don't need | [L1356, 2019+] |
| Safe | Predictable | Certainty, risk removal, track record, testimonials, case studies, and repeating patterns from history | [L1356, 2019+] |
| Big | Fast | Big, Monster, Huge, Once in a lifetime; Fast, Quickly, Overnight, Weekly | [L1356, 2019+] |

- Every line of persuasive copy should carry at least one of four core emotions: New, Easy, Safe, and Big, each paired with an alter ego (New/Only, Easy/Anybody, Safe/Predictable, Big/Fast). Not every line needs all four, but the copy as a whole should weave in as many as possible, leaning on the two or three that best fit the offer. [L1356, 2019+]
- New is the most important emotion because prospects are skeptical and have short attention spans. If a reader categorizes the message as something they have already seen, or as similar to something they have seen, the copy loses them. New also gives hope that a previously failed solution might finally work, and it should feel genuinely new, not merely a better version of something existing. [L1356, 2019+]
- Words that convey New include Breakthrough, Revolutionary, Discovery, Game Changing, Disruptive, NEW, Announcing, Revealed, and Breaking. Use them only if the message is truly new. The power comes from the subtext of newness, not the word itself. [L1356, 2019+]
- Only signals exclusivity: the offer is available only here, through this seller, or was never sold before. Words that convey Only include Exclusive, Patented, Only here, and Proprietary. Phrases such as *the only document in the world that contains...* do the same job. [L1356, 2019+]
- Easy (and its alter ego Anybody) tells the reader the solution requires little work, is step-by-step or push-button, and is simple enough for anyone, including people who feel insecure about their abilities. Words that signal it include Easy, Anybody, Simple, 1-2-3, Push-button, Automatic, Fool-proof, Step-by-step, System, All you have to do is, Simply follow the steps, and You don't need. [L1356, 2019+]
- Readers want Easy solutions for two reasons: laziness (they want less workload) and insecurity (they fear past failures reflect their own inadequacy). Making a solution feel possible for anybody, regardless of intelligence, money, or experience, addresses both. [L1356, 2019+]
- Not-statements serve two purposes. They build intrigue by naming what the thing is not, e.g. *It's not muscle confusion, it's not power lifting, and it's not cardio*. They also convey Easy/Anybody by stating what the reader does not need, e.g. *You don't have to be a financial genius, you don't need coding experience, you don't need much money*. Pair them with a concrete, low-effort starting point. [L1356, 2019+]
- The Safe emotion reduces skepticism and simultaneously raises perceived value. People who see something as less risky judge its benefits as more valuable, even when a higher-risk option offers a higher expected return. Safe is therefore a double-acting emotion in persuasion. [L1356, 2019+]
- People are risk averse and place high value on certainty. In a classic study, most participants chose a guaranteed $80 over a gamble with an 80% chance at $100 and a 20% chance at $10, even though the gamble's expected value was $82. Copy should therefore emphasize certainty and eliminating risk. [L1356, 2019+]
- Predictable (Safe's alter ego) can be conveyed by showing a repeating pattern from history, track record, testimonials, or case studies, so the reader infers that the outcome will follow a known sequence. Let the reader draw the conclusion themselves rather than stating it outright. [L1356, 2019+]
- A track record can imply Safe and Predictable without explicitly guaranteeing results. Present past performance as a pattern the reader can see, not as a promise. [L1356, 2019+]
- Big refers to the size of the opportunity and conveys that it is extremely important, groundbreaking, or rewarding. It demands attention and implies change, which is why readers read on. Big pairs well with fear of missing out (FOMO). Its alter ego Fast means results come quickly rather than over years. [L1356, 2019+]
- Words that convey Big include Big, Monster, Huge, and Once in a lifetime. Fast-related words convey speed: Fast, Quickly, Overnight, and Weekly. Combining Big and Fast (a large result delivered in seconds, days, or weeks rather than years) multiplies the effect. [L1356, 2019+]
- Emotions can work together. A New discovery is usually Big and can be tied to Predictability by referencing a past event, e.g. *The last time something like this happened, it was extremely disruptive*. Easy can be woven into Big by stating that a huge result is simple to achieve. [L1356, 2019+]
- Build the big promise in the lead from the emotions: New or Only, Easy or Anybody, Safe or Predictable, and Big or Fast, chosen to suit the product type. A system or step-by-step product leans on Easy/Anybody and Fast. A prediction or trend leans on New and Big to create fear of missing out. [L1356, 2019+]
- Copywriting works through subtext, meaning the emotions a reader infers rather than the literal words. Judge copy by what it communicates, not by how clever or conversational its wording is. Plain conversational text without emotional subtext still reads as ordinary English, not persuasive copy. [L1356, 2019+]
- Copy persuades by emotion, not logic, so a reader can be drawn to an opportunity even while knowing it is made up. Using the four core emotions and the four sections is the framework that lets copy bypass a reader's rational checks. [L1356, 2019+]

### Lead with the outcome and the feeling

- Buyers move toward pleasurable outcomes and away from painful ones, so the main headline should state the big benefit or desired outcome at the top of the page. Surface that benefit before anything else. [L1209, undated]
- Sell emotional value and the psychological outcome, not only the product. Use the buyer's emotions as the core of the message. [L1195, 2026]
- Novelty switches on attention, because people's brains react strongly to things that are completely new and ignore what is familiar. The uniqueness of the new opportunity should appear in the headline and at the start of the letter, framed as a breakthrough. [L1365, 2019+]
- Novelty can take many forms: a new cure, a new way to lose weight, a new way to make money, or a new business model. In financial copy it can be a new law, loophole, threat, prediction, discovery, scandal, or trading system, as long as the reader has not seen it before. [L1365, 2019+]
- Sell the feeling and the desire first, then give the technical facts and reasons that let the buyer explain the decision to themselves and others. People buy on emotion and then justify the purchase with logic. [L1366, undated]
- Do not mention the product too early in the lead. Resistance to persuasion comes in four forms, each handled by a different section of the copy:
  - Reactance: the feeling that your freedom is being limited, so pushy selling backfires.
  - Distrust: handled by proof and the credibility section.
  - Scrutiny: readers want logic behind their emotional decision, handled by the mechanism explanation.
  - Inertia: staying put is easier than acting, handled by the urgency section.
  - Even a hint of selling can make a reader leave. [L1365, 2019+]

### Persuade without triggering resistance

- Telling the prospect that their failure is their own fault, such as saying they are bad with money or lack discipline, makes people reject the copy. People do not want to take responsibility for their failures, so point the justification at the old solutions instead. [L1365, 2019+]
- Choose the common enemy from beliefs the audience already holds, instead of inventing one from nothing, such as an institution or group they already distrust or resent. Blaming that enemy releases a sense of belonging and makes the prospect feel part of a group aligned against a shared problem. [L1365, 2019+]
- Facts and statistics alone push readers into an analytical mindset, which lowers emotional response and reduces the chance they will act. Presenting the same proof inside a story produces a stronger reaction than either facts alone or a mix of facts and story. [L1365, 2019+]
- Ask every reader to nod in agreement early by making statements that are both honest and believable and that the reader already holds to be true. A single statement the reader disputes causes them to stop reading. Check each claim for truth and relevance. [L1366, undated]
- Open paragraphs or subheads with questions and statements the reader can easily agree with before the offer appears. This builds a habit of agreement that makes the offer easier to accept. [L1370, undated]
- Use fear, threats, or crime statistics with caution, because most buyers are repelled. Let the buyer recognize the need first, then show the points they care about most, such as reliability and ease of installation. [L1366, undated]

### Use formulas to turn features into outcomes

- Formula: state the behavioral benefit, then ask what the buyer ultimately gets from it, and lead with that deeper outcome. The deeper outcome, such as financial freedom, security, or being able to care for family, is more persuasive than the metric itself.
  - Example A: instead of *add 20 leads a month*, lead with *stop worrying about where next month's clients come from*.
  - Example B: instead of *get 30 minutes back each day*, lead with *finally have evenings for the people you care about*.
  - Both work because they connect the feature to the reason the buyer wants it. [L0997, undated]
- Saying a business will grow to 10k a month is a behavioral claim. Explaining that this means never worrying about money again, affording what you want, and looking after your family taps into the real reason the buyer wants it. The second version is the more potent message. [L0997, undated]
- Motive-driven ad formula: replace a measurable outcome with the freedom, security, or control it brings. The formula works because buyers pay for what the outcome means to their life, not the number itself. [L0998, undated]
  - Instead of *Hit 20k a month*, write *Stop checking your bank app with dread*.
  - Instead of *Lose 5 kg in six weeks*, write *Walk into your reunion feeling like yourself again*.
- Status-driven ad formula: show that the product lets the buyer look successful, stay current with trends, or avoid being left behind by peers. The formula works because people want to appear in control and up to date among the people they compare themselves to. [L0998, undated]
  - *Be the person whose watch everyone in the meeting notices, without a word.*
  - *Stay ahead of the look your friends are already wearing this season.*
- Translate an unfamiliar effort into a reference the buyer already understands, so they grasp the value without learning new facts. A time-based comparison such as 30 minutes of exercise equals a two mile walk works this way. [L1207, undated]
- Pattern: [amount of effort in familiar time] equals [familiar everyday activity]. It anchors an unknown effort to a reference the shopper already understands.
  - *Ten minutes of this routine equals a 30 minute gym session, if your results back the claim.*
  - *Five minutes with this tool equals an hour of hand sanding.* [L1207, undated]

### Use social proof, authority, and urgency

- Stating a credible role, such as a nurse, gives the speaker authority, and viewers tend to follow advice from people in that role. Mention the role naturally in the testimonial, not as a sales claim. [L0707, 2026]
- Social proof works through five-star reviews, a product that sold out, and a long waiting time for delivery. Each of these signals demand, so the viewer assumes others already trust the product. [L0707, 2026]
- Social proof builds trust and can take the form of testimonials, case studies, or customer reviews. It is useful in an ad even though not every creative needs it. [L1425, undated]
- Scarcity and urgency work through fear of missing out, because people fear losing an opportunity and value what is scarce. Emphasize limited availability or a time-limited offer to push action. [L1425, undated]
- Jay Abraham's principle: if you can define a customer's problem better than the customer can, they will assume you have the solution. Content that explains the problem clearly builds authority and trust. [L1461, 2021-22]

### Evidence from historical cases

- Early in advertising history, an agency tested reason-why ads that promised a clear emotional payoff against the usual news-style product announcements. Within four months, the client raised its budget with the agency from $15,000 per year to $30,000 per month. The lesson for copy: find the real reason customers buy, and make the emotional core of the promise the opening. [L0756, undated]
- In a 2004 Carnegie Mellon study on charity donations, participants shown the story of one child in poverty gave an average of $2.38. Those shown only poverty statistics gave an average of $1.14. A group shown both the story and the statistics gave an average of $1.43, less than the story alone. Adding facts to a story can lower emotional response and reduce the amount people give. [L1365, 2019+]
- Ads that appeal to emotion and tell a vivid story outperform ads built on endorsements and factual statements. In one comparison, the emotional version tripled and quadrupled the returns. Use authority statements as support, not as the main message. [L1369, undated]
- Sensational, emotional content tends to outsell sober content in print. A tabloid-style approach was cited as selling about ten times as many copies as a clean, well-edited paper. Use this to justify a more emotional headline, but test it against your own audience. [L1369, undated]
- Starting point: a writer had a self-development book that sold poorly under letters and ads describing its contents, and its original publisher failed. A former bookkeeper for that publisher bought the plates and remaining stock for a low price and believed the book would sell if the appeal went to the reader's ambition rather than a description of contents. With only 200 dollars left, he spent all of it on a mailing that appealed to ambition, which brought 2,000 dollars of direct orders by mail. He reinvested every dollar of that money in more mailings, and over the following fifteen years the book sold more than 2,000,000 dollars. [L1369, undated]
- Starting point: a publisher's etiquette book had lain in its stock room for ten to fifteen years without demand. The publisher's letters and advertisements then appealed to the fear that a reader might commit an unconscious social blunder and cause themselves embarrassment. The book sold about a million copies, and the demand came from that fear rather than from any change in public taste. [L1369, undated]

### Test before committing budget

- In a documented side-by-side test, a positive version of an ad showing the desired result out-pulled its negative version about four to one. Test positive and negative framings against each other before committing budget. [L1261, undated]
- Keep a personal spreadsheet of psychological copy tactics, such as loss aversion, future pacing, and social proof. Apply these on top of problem, solution, and outcome copy, and use the spreadsheet as a reference bank. [L0919, undated]

### Meme-style and native-looking ads

- Meme-style image ads look native to the Instagram feed and feel less like ads, so they tend to earn high attention and click-through rates. Replace the text with a provocative hook or a major product benefit, then swap the image to test new products quickly. [L0341, 2023; L0343, 2023]
- Meme ads can hold attention and generate clicks, but in one case they still sold less than user-generated content. Use them as a testing format and scale the ones that respond well. [L0343, 2023]
- Meme-style video ads produced an attention rate around 60% and a click-through rate around 4% in one case. They were kept running because they improved Google Ads performance, even though they did not show direct Facebook sales. This was observed once and should be checked before relying on it. [L0995, 2023]
- Static meme-style image ads perform well on Facebook. Build them in Canva by placing the product image with a short line of text and an emoji on top. Meme-style image ads performed well in the lesson's test. [L0773, 2023; L0774, 2023]
- Simple image ads: the product photo with a short caption that gives the reason to buy, placed on top of the image. [L0774, 2023]
  - Example 1: "The shoe you wanted, now in your size." [L0774, 2023]
  - Example 2: "Ready for winter nights? Get yours today." [L0774, 2023]
- Jokes that rely on outdated trends or references lose their effect quickly. Check whether a reference is still current before building a video around it. [L0372, 2022]
- Trend-format pattern: adapt a popular format in which people list what they like about a subject, replace the subject with your product, then name its main benefits. Viewers already understand the pattern and can focus on the benefits. [L0372, 2022]
  - Example 1: "Things I love about my cordless vacuum: it fits under the couch, it charges in a minute, it finally keeps the stairs clean." [L0372, 2022]
  - Example 2: "Why my commute got easier: the foldable backpack fits a laptop, it dries fast in the rain, and it has a USB port." [L0372, 2022]
- Story hook pattern: open with a relatable incident, such as a robbery or a theft caught on camera, then cut to the product as the solution. Viewers want to see how the story ends. [L0372, 2022]
  - Example 1: "Someone walked into the garage at 2 am and the owner's first clip was caught by a light bulb camera." [L0372, 2022]
  - Example 2: "A package disappeared from the porch, so the homeowner set up a hidden camera that shows the whole story." [L0372, 2022]
- Visual pump-and-dump product: a plain product showcase with short overlay text and no listicle or advertorial can be enough, since the product itself is the message. Short clips of about 15 to 20 seconds with simple text worked as winners in the example. [L0347, 2026]

### Listicle, advertorial and editorial formats

- Three editorial styles are common: listicle (a numbered list of benefits), long-form blog-style article, and news-article style with bold claims, big numbers and research data. Choose the style that fits the product and the audience's problem. [L0578, 2023+]
- Listicle pattern: a numbered list of benefits, often 7 or 10 points, about a trending or little-known product. It is framed as why it is taking over or why people should own it, and the product is pitched only at the end. The reader meets neutral content instead of an ad, and the trend framing makes an unknown product worth checking. [L0578, 2023+]
  - Example 1: "7 Reasons Compact Ergonomic Mice Are Taking Over Home Offices". [L0578, 2023+]
  - Example 2: "10 Things Physiotherapists Wish Their Patients Knew About Morning Back Pain". [L0578, 2023+]
- Blog-style pattern: a long-form article that opens with a relatable character's problem, agitates it, explains why common fixes fail, introduces a new approach, and only then presents the product in the last part. Trust grows when the author byline shows an expert, such as a doctor or vet, and when photos break up the text. [L0578, 2023+]
  - Example 1: "Why Your Sleep Keeps Breaking at 3 AM: What a Sleep Specialist Found in Her Clinic". [L0578, 2023+]
  - Example 2: "The Kitchen Habit That Dulls Knives in Weeks: How One Chef Changed Her Routine". [L0578, 2023+]
- News-style pattern: the layout and headline imitate a news article, using a bold claim, large numbers and cited research data, with the product pitched after the story. The reader trusts the apparent reporting and sees the problem as urgent and proven. [L0578, 2023+]
  - Example 1: "Study Finds 6 in 10 Remote Workers Lose Focus Before Lunch, and a New Fix Is Gaining Ground". [L0578, 2023+]
  - Example 2: "Data From 4,000 Home Gym Owners Shows Why Most Resistance Bands Snap Within a Year". [L0578, 2023+]
- Advertorials also come in a listicle format: a numbered list of reasons, such as eight or five reasons a product solves a problem. It works like a story-based advertorial: it builds interest first, then bridges the visitor to the product page. Problem-solving products, such as toys, suit this format best. [L0691, 2024]
- Listicle advertorial headline: "Here are [number] reasons why [product] is on [audience]'s top wish lists", followed by a numbered list of points and then a sales page. The format promises a quick, ordered read that makes the product feel chosen by people like the reader. Test headline variants, because the first line decides whether visitors keep reading. [L0995, 2023]
  - Example 1: "Here are 7 reasons why this compact pet-hair roller is on busy dog owners' top wish lists". [L0995, 2023]
  - Example 2: "Here are 5 reasons why this personal alarm keychain is on night commuters' top wish lists". [L0995, 2023]
- Cold audiences: send paid clicks first to editorial-style advertorial pages that look like articles, to warm the audience up before they reach the sales landing page. Then run the sales page with messaging matched to the same audience. Use this when a product needs more explanation or trust than a short ad can give. [L0918, 2023]
- Physical products are more visual. An image, a short product video or a product explainer that shows the feature, benefit and outcome often converts better than a long sales letter. A long-form sales letter fits products that need more explanation, and the right format depends on the market. [L0643, 2015]
- Dialogue-style videos, where the creator responds to a viewer's comment or promises to feature the top-liked comment, drive strong engagement and follower growth, especially on new accounts. They usually convert less well than hooks that name a viewer problem and present a product solution right away. [L0795, undated]
- A warehouse or behind-the-scenes video showing the brand's own logo on packaging adds proof and trust. [L0950, 2023]
  - Example: "See our packing team ship every order in its branded box." [L0950, 2023]
  - Example: "Watch how we pack your order before it leaves our warehouse." [L0950, 2023]
- Winner-and-envious-rival scene: show the buyer succeeding in a visible moment while a peer watches with envy, and let the product be the implied reason without making a claim. [L0340, undated]
  - Formula: "[Buyer] using [product] in a moment of success, [rival] in the background watching or waiting." [L0340, undated]
  - Example: "A landscaper unloads a truck with the company name visible while a rival crew sits in a jam behind them." [L0340, undated]
  - Example: "A home cook serves a tray of bread to friends while a neighbour stands at the door with store-bought loaves." [L0340, undated]
- Long-running story case: a Facebook video story of about nine minutes, far beyond the length usually recommended for that platform, was posted with a $187 spend in the first week, then paid promotion stopped. The video was a personal story about a pet and a hard time, filmed on location, with no sales pitch. It reached 2.4 million views, 59,000 likes, 31,000 shares and over 4,000 comments, many of them long personal replies. The creator later noted that deeply emotional storytelling built lasting loyalty that a shorter, safer clip could not match. The same story opened the email sequence to build a bond with new subscribers. [L0378, 2019+]

### Image angles for demand generation

Demand Gen image angles:

| Angle | Goal | Directions |
|---|---|---|
| Informational | Create curiosity without solving the problem | Problem-focused (everyday struggle); Clinical/Editorial (anatomy or article style); Curiosity-driven (unusual or intriguing) |
| Comparison | Imply that someone has done the research | Editorial flat-lay; Lifestyle evaluation scene; Side-by-side hierarchy |
| Offer-focused | Make the product the hero | High-contrast product hero shot with negative space for headline or urgency text |

All three angles: photorealistic, no heavy filters, no clutter. Only the offer-focused angle carries text on the image. [L0882, 2025-26]

- Informational images that worked as headline partners include a medical-textbook style anatomy illustration, an X-ray style image, a doctor reviewing anatomy, and a pain-point lifestyle photo that looks like stock imagery. A headline such as "doctors explaining hidden reasons a treatment fails" pairs well with a clinical anatomy image. [L0882, 2025-26]

### Retargeting ads

- Reminder retargeting ad: a short question that points at what the visitor viewed, followed by a reason to return, such as free shipping. It reminds the visitor of their interest instead of pitching cold. [L0649, 2019]
  - Example 1: "Still thinking it over? Your saved items are waiting, with free shipping today." [L0649, 2019]
  - Example 2: "Left something in your cart? Come back and check out in one click." [L0649, 2019]
- Informant ad: a retargeting ad that promotes an existing, valuable article instead of the product directly. Formula: the article's feature image, a headline in the form "Learn how to [main outcome the article delivers]", body copy that names the main problem the reader faces and says the article solves it, and a CTA such as "Read now" for text or "Watch now" / "View now" for video. It works because the ad speaks to a problem the viewer already feels and promises a useful answer rather than a sale. [L0415, 2021-22]
  - Own examples: "Learn how to stop a squeaky office chair in five minutes" (body: squeaks break your focus at work and the guide shows the three quick fixes; CTA "Read now"). [L0415, 2021-22]
  - Own examples: "Learn how to pick a hiking backpack that fits your torso" (body: most packs sit wrong and cause shoulder pain; CTA "Learn more"). [L0415, 2021-22]
- Informant ad steps:
  1. Choose the existing content piece the ad will lead to.
  2. Use that content's feature image, checking it is high quality and directly matches the content on the page.
  3. Write a headline that names the main thing the reader will learn from the content.
  4. Write body copy that describes the primary problem the content addresses, so the viewer feels the connection.
  5. Pick the CTA: "Read now" for text content, "Watch now" or "View now" for video, "Learn more" as a general alternative.
  6. Keep the same image, headline and CTA across all placements, changing only the copy length and format per network.
  7. Set up the ad in the ad platform's interface once the audiences exist.
  [L0415, 2021-22]
- The feature image should show people who look like the target audience and be directly relevant to the main problem of the content, so it stands out in a feed. A generic or off-topic image weakens the ad, because it does not signal that the content solves the viewer's problem. [L0415, 2021-22]
- Since the content already exists, most of the work is done and the ad takes little time to produce. In practice, one such ad takes around 10 minutes to create, and once the initial audiences are set up the process is quick. [L0415, 2021-22]

### Lead magnet ads

- Lead magnet ad: name a useful free resource in the headline, add a short benefit line, and end with a button such as "Learn more". The formula sells the free item instead of the product. [L0649, 2019]
  - Example 1: "Free weekly meal plan for busy parents, grab yours here." [L0649, 2019]
  - Example 2: "Free fit guide for trail runners, get it now." [L0649, 2019]

### Introduction and owner-led ads

- Showing the business owner in an introduction ad builds trust and a personal connection that many e-commerce stores lack, which helps them stand out from competitors. It works best when the owner introduces themselves in their own voice, rather than using a professionally produced ad with no personal link to the business. [L0488, 2021-22]
- Avoid phrasing such as "thanks for visiting our site" in retargeting ads, because it feels creepy to visitors. Instead, have the owner introduce themselves and invite people to get in touch. [L0488, 2021-22]

**Introduction ad layout**

```
Ad layout for an introduction ad, listing what each field must contain. Image or video: show yourself on camera. If you choose video, say who you are, describe your business and how you assist people, then add a call to action asking the viewer to phone your business. Headline: address the customer in a manner that catches their eye; this can be quite open-ended. Body copy: urge the customer to reach out to your business. With a video ad, simply restate the video's main message. With more room, add a short background story and cover the business, your unique selling point and the way you help people. Call to action: test wording such as "Call Us", "Get In Touch" or "Call Me".
```
[L0488, 2021-22]

- If you are dropshipping and cannot film yourself with the products you sell, use a plain image of yourself alone instead of product images. A video with the owner showing the products is even better when possible. [L0488, 2021-22]
- Use the body copy to encourage the viewer to contact the business and build the relationship. Experiment with call-to-action wording such as "Call us", "Get in touch" and "Call me". [L0488, 2021-22]
- Placements for an introduction ad concept:
  - A static Google Display Network remarketing banner with a short text and a call-to-action button.
  - A Facebook news feed video ad.
  - A Google Display leaderboard banner (the wide banner shown at the top of a website).
  - An Instagram video ad with a Contact Us button.
  The same image and text are reused across placements. [L0488, 2021-22]

### Concept briefs for new-customer ads

Ad concept requirements (applies to at least 5 concepts aimed at new customers who have never heard of the brand):

- Each concept must contain: a name; positioning strategy (new mechanism, superior product or identity marketing); format (static image, video, carousel or UGC); primary avatar; key desire or pain point; 5-7 hook variations using proven patterns; visual concept (benefit, problem, product or product in action); ad structure; full script; visual proof elements; call to action; urgency element if appropriate; handling of the top 2-3 objections; and an explanation of why it works, citing research findings. [L0613, 2026]
- Each ad concept should focus on one desire or problem only. It must show understanding of the viewer's desires, problems and identity, use language found in the research, pre-empt objections, use hooks proven in the niche, and create belief through visual proof. It should also stand out from competitor creative. [L0613, 2026]

Concept architecture (for at least 5 concepts):

1. Name the concept and target avatar with distress level.
2. Opening emotion (0-3 seconds) and whether it narrows or broadens attention, with the reason.
3. Agitation emotions, intensity and duration.
4. Transition trigger, permission language and bridge emotion.
5. Solution emotions and the identity transformation promised.
6. Action emotion (urgency or possibility) and the final push.
7. 5-7 hook variations, each tagged with its emotion, narrowing or broadening, and the distress level it suits.
8. Negative label addressed and positive label offered.
9. Mirror moment within 3 seconds.
10. Emotional permission granted.
11. For high distress: how positive impairment is handled, how long to stay negative, and what validation comes first.
12. Full script with [NARROWING] and [BROADENING] tags and a clearly marked transition.
13. Visual concept supporting each phase.
14. Emotional objections and fears addressed.
15. Why it works, citing research findings and the avatar's profile.

[L0613, 2026]

Brief per avatar, in this order:

1. Concept name.
2. Emotional architecture: opening emotion (narrowing or broadening), agitation emotions, transition trigger, solution emotions, action emotion.
3. Five hook options, each tagged with emotion and type.
4. Identity strategy: negative label addressed, aspirational label offered, transition presentation.
5. Mirror moment within 3 seconds.
6. For high distress: how long to stay in negative territory, what validation is given and what permission is granted.
7. Objections pre-empted.
8. Best format and best time from the data.
9. Script timing:
   - 0-3 s: hook (emotion)
   - 3-15 s: agitation (emotional development)
   - 15-25 s: transition (permission to hope)
   - 25-45 s: solution (positive emotion building)
   - 45-60 s: CTA (action emotion)

[L0613, 2026]

### Choosing the format: static, image and video

- Static formats include static image ads, catalog ads, advertorials, and listicles. Static image ads are the simplest starting point before custom video production. [L1195, 2026]
- Test a book with several ad formats: long-form copywriting, native ads, catalyst ads, image ads and AI UGC ads. Each format needs heavy customization before the test reaches a usable stage. [L1175, 2026]
- Video works best for products that need storytelling, use-case demonstration or an emotional or spiritual connection, while simple attractive products can work well with carousel or image ads. [L1158, 2023]
- Image ads with the offer printed on them can be sufficient for some products, and they were the strongest creative in the lesson's own example. Whether a product needs video depends on the product itself. [L1219, undated]
- Fashion items can usually be tested with picture ads only, because they do not need video to sell. Compare CTR and store reaction before adding video. [L1219, undated]
- For fashion products targeting an audience aged 45 and over, picture ads (single image, carousel, or slideshow) often outperform video ads. This was observed on fashion products in Australia, so it should be tested rather than assumed. [L1212, undated]
- Test social-proof creatives alongside value-proposition creatives instead of relying on one format. [L1016, 2026]
- Show the product in use in short video, because motion and experience carry the emotional part of the message. [L1016, 2026]

### Testimonial and review ads

#### Structure and length

- A workable testimonial ad states the core benefits first and then adds testimonials as proof. Placing the testimonials after the benefit claims lets social proof back up what the ad has already promised. [L1348, 2023]
- Use no more than three testimonials in a single ad. A short slideshow of three brief testimonials works well, but more than three wastes viewer time. For long, full video testimonials, use only one per ad so the viewer is not bored. [L1348, 2023]
- Testimonial length determines how many to show: short one-line testimonials can be stacked, while a long story-style testimonial (a customer describing their full journey) should appear only once in an ad. [L1348, 2023]
- Testimonial ads can run as static images or as video, and both formats performed fairly well in testing. Animated testimonial sequences, where one quote rolls into the next with subtle movement such as a slight breathing motion, were reported to work well. [L1348, 2023]
- A pop-up social proof format shows many comments or reviews appearing one after another (pop, pop, pop) until the screen fills. This displays a lot of social proof without boring the viewer. Build it with real or designed comment cards and sound effects in a video editor. [L1348, 2023]

#### Sourcing and testing

- Real customer comments taken from your own store or product page are a lower-risk option than invented comments, because the feedback is genuine. Recreating a comment card by hand takes more design effort, so real feedback is the simpler starting point. [L1348, 2023]
- Run customer reviews and testimonials as ad creatives on Meta, even when there are only around 11 reviews. Split test two or three review or UGC creatives inside one ad set. [L1225, 2025]
- Testimonial and review-style videos work particularly well for retargeting, because viewers have already seen the ads but not acted. Pair them with an offer in the ad copy, headline, or description. [L1335, 2023+]

**Procedure: learn to produce a testimonial ad**

1. Find examples from large established brands known to run testimonial ads. [L1348, 2023]
2. Search for "testimonial ads" in Foreplay and Google. [L1348, 2023]
3. Study which testimonial ads are clean and well spaced. [L1348, 2023]
4. Build your own version from these references instead of starting from scratch. [L1348, 2023]

### Low-cost creatives

- Two low-cost ad creatives need no production team: a screenshot of the checkout page showing the discount or savings, and a screen recording of browsing the site with a voiceover from the customer's point of view. Both can run alongside other creatives in the same test. [L1225, 2025]

### Problem and demonstration ads

- A problem-scene creative shows the target buyer in a situation where the product's problem occurs, such as a rider whose headphones do not fit under a helmet, and then shows the product solving it. Name the specific problem in the ad copy as well, so the viewer recognises the situation quickly. [L1225, 2025]
- Ads that open with a relatable before-and-after story about a specific pet problem attract owners who are in a similar situation. Emotional storytelling was used to make people feel the problem before the advertorial. [L1131, 2018+]
- A lifestyle video in which a creator presents a product as their own new tech and shows it in daily use performed strongly organically. The video showed the product as a solution in the creator's own routine rather than as an advert. [L1444, 2022]

### Us-versus-them and comparison ads

- An us-versus-them ad positions the product against a clearly weaker alternative, such as an inferior method or a poor competitor, so the product looks better by comparison. This works through anchoring, because the viewer judges the product against a low benchmark. [L1348, 2023]
  - Example: "Our 3-layer hiking socks vs. cotton socks that soak up sweat" [L1348, 2023]
  - Example: "Our cordless mop vs. a bucket and rag that just spreads grime." [L1348, 2023]
- Comparison ads can work well: start by comparing against an everyday alternative, such as a café coffee at about $6 a cup, rather than attacking an established brand directly. [L1016, 2026]
- A before-and-after comparison can show the time and effort of a competitor's option, such as a barista coffee bought on the run versus a quicker product, matching the time-saving value lever. [L1016, 2026]
- Direct brand-on-brand comparison carries pushback risk and is best used once the brand has enough traction, and only when the brand is ready to respond to a counterattack. [L1016, 2026]

### Listicle and advertorial ads

- List-style advertorial headline. Formula:
  1. Promise a specific count of reasons the product matters. [L0999, 2023]
  2. Tie it to a status signal the reader wants. [L0999, 2023]
  3. Present the points as a numbered list before the sales page. [L0999, 2023]
- This works because a numbered promise sets clear expectations, the status tie makes the reader want to see if they belong, and the list makes reading easy so the sales page feels earned. [L0999, 2023]
- Examples: "7 reasons home bakers are switching to a magnetic spice rack" and "5 reasons outdoor runners keep a reflective vest in their car." [L0999, 2023]

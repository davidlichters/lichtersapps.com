### Social Proof and Bandwagon

- Social proof: people follow what others do as a shortcut. Show customer counts such as thousands of owners, viral popularity, or high ratings from existing customers. [L0516, 2023]
- Social proof pattern: point to what many people already do, so the viewer follows the group. This works best when the claim is specific and true, such as a share of users or a popular bestseller.
  - "Most people in your city switched to a lighter running jacket this season, and the sales show why."
  - "Most home bakers still buy their flour from the same store, but the bakers who switched say the loaves rise better."
  [L0524, 2023]
- Statement pattern with scale and emotional outcome: include a specific number of satisfied customers ("50K+", "10,000+", "25,000+") paired with an emotional descriptor of their resulting state ("relieved and relaxed", "sleeping soundly", "pain-free"). The number is concrete rather than vague like "thousands," and the emotion shows the actual benefit, not just "satisfied customers."
  - "100,000+ customers waking up refreshed."
  - "75,000+ users standing taller and confident."
  [L0313, 2026]
- Popularity claims: state a verified ranking or a preference by a specific group, so the reader infers that many others chose the product. Use a specific number and a named audience, such as a "most recommended by" claim tied to a real professional group. Write new claims only where the ranking or survey behind them can be documented. [L0340, undated]
- Comparison cue: people feel pressure to conform to what friends, family, and peers choose. Show the product being chosen by a clearly defined group that the target buyer belongs to or wants to join. [L0340, undated]
- Bandwagon effect: the rate of adoption of beliefs increases as more people adopt them. Apply it by showing the reader is not alone ("join over 10,000 happy customers"), and/or that they may be left behind if they don't act (FOMO). Use phrases such as "Most people already know," "Thousands have figured out," and "Join hundreds of happy customers." Include many testimonials. Show real-time signals such as "Richard just signed up" or "Twelve people are viewing this room." [L0196, 2020+]

### Authority, Liking, and Reciprocity

- Cialdini's six principles, to use in copy: reciprocity (give value or free resources first so the customer feels they owe something); commitment and consistency (ask for a small commitment first, such as an email for a discount, then build to bigger asks, and label the customer's identity so they stay consistent with it); social proof (testimonials and customer numbers); authority (credible experts, publications, awards, or endorsements); liking (similarity such as "made by dog owners for dog owners," genuine compliments, and cooperation toward the customer's goals); scarcity (limited stock or rare availability makes the product seem more valuable). [L0515, 2023]
- Reciprocity: people feel they should return a favor, so offering value first, such as free information, education, or a free sample, creates a sense of obligation to buy later. [L0516, 2023]
- Reciprocity in ads: give useful information or value first, and the viewer feels more inclined to act in return. Pair the value with a clear call to action. [L0524, 2023]
- Authority: people trust credible, knowledgeable sources. Use industry awards, magazine recognition, approval from professionals in the field (such as vets), and celebrity endorsements to transfer credibility to the product. [L0516, 2023]
- Authority cue: people accept claims from official-looking or expert figures, so a credible expert endorsement can replace much of the reader's research. Testimonials and short expert videos are common ways to use it. [L0340, undated]
- Liking: people say yes to those they like, through three routes: similarity ("I am like you"), genuine compliments, and cooperation (showing you help the customer reach their goals). "Made by dog owners for dog owners" uses similarity and cooperation together. [L0516, 2023]
- Liking cue: people buy from those they like, including representatives, characters in ads, and other users. Attractive faces are judged more trustworthy and likable, and people tend to identify with models of their own gender. [L0340, undated]

### Commitment and Consistency

- Commitment and consistency (Four Walls), in order. In print, the chain must be built into the copy, since there is no social pressure from a live person.
  1. Ask a question the prospect will certainly agree with.
  2. Ask a second question that follows logically from the first agreement.
  3. Continue with a chain of questions that each lead to the next, so the reader has said yes several times.
  4. Finish with the purchase request, which the reader now feels bound to honor. [L0340, undated]
- Start with a small, low-cost ask, such as an email signup in exchange for a discount, then ask for more later. Questions the reader will almost certainly answer yes to build a chain of small agreements. [L0516, 2023]
- Commitment through an agreed opening statement: start with a point the reader will almost always agree with, such as a shared goal for their pet, home, or family, then ask for a small next step. Once the reader agrees with the first statement, saying no to the next request feels inconsistent with what they just said.
  - "Of course you want your kitchen to stay grease-free between cleanings, so try the first step free for seven days."
  - "Of course you want your kids to get to school on time, so join the list for a weekly routine checklist."
  [L0515, 2023]
- Choice-supportive bias (commitment bias): people retroactively ascribe positive attributes to the option they chose and downplay its faults, while amplifying the faults of rejected options. Readers make a choice by engaging with your copy, so help them feel good about it, especially before high-price purchases. Use positive language near the CTA ("Good move!", "Great choice!"), after the sale ("Awesome!", "Thank you!"), and send unexpected bonuses around the refund period to turn potential refunds into happy customers. [L0196, 2020+]

### Scarcity, Loss Aversion, and FOMO

- Loss aversion and fear of missing out: people dislike losing something they could gain, so frame the offer as something they would lose by waiting. Limited-time discounts, limited stock, and low-stock messages make people act to avoid losing an offer. They are strong when combined with a real deadline or a real stock limit, and they link closely to the stakes shown in the story. [L0515, 2023; L0516, 2023]
- Scarcity: limited stock or rare availability makes the product seem more valuable. [L0515, 2023]

### Envy, Status, and Keeping Up

- Envy and keeping up with others are strong desire triggers: people want what others have and feel uneasy about being behind. Use them carefully, since blunt versions can read as manipulative. A softer version is to show that most respected or successful people in the niche already use the product. [L0515, 2023]
- Envy and scorn: people envy those who have what they lack and look down on those who lack what they have, so this can motivate buying. Use it indirectly, because direct comparisons with other people come across as manipulative and can drive customers away. [L0516, 2023]
- Keeping up with others: people do not want to fall behind their peers or status group, so show that the people they admire already use the product. Phrasing such as "why haven't you caught up with other top owners" triggers this need. [L0516, 2023]

### Curiosity, Shock, and Effort

- Curiosity, shock, and specific claims pull readers into the body copy. Curiosity can be created with a question or an open loop. Shock must be qualified so it attracts the right audience, not just any attention. [L0515, 2023]
- Shock grabs attention, but shocking statements used only for effect attract the wrong audience, who will not buy. Use shock only when it connects directly to the product's problem and the buyer you want. [L0516, 2023]
- Reduce time, effort, and sacrifice in the copy. Show how fast the result comes (for example, a few swipes instead of hours), how little effort is needed, and what the customer does not have to give up, such as time with family or other activities. Use the word "just" to make effort feel small. Ask what the customer would have to give up without the product, and make clear they no longer need to give it up. [L0515, 2023; L0516, 2023]

### Breakthrough Advertising Tactics

- Intensification raises the reader's desire and need for the product by making the current pain and the wanted result feel more urgent and vivid, without adding new facts. Use it in a headline or opening line when the audience already knows the problem but has not yet acted. Use it to sharpen wording around the real benefit, not to add claims the product cannot support. [L0515, 2023; L0516, 2023]
- Identification ties the product to how the buyer wants to be seen, including status and self-image. Work out what the buyer wants to become, then frame the product as a step toward that self-image. It works best when the market has heard many similar claims, so copy speaks to who the customer is and who they want to become, not to product features. Copy that ties the product to a desired identity, such as a certain kind of owner or host, connects more strongly than copy that only lists features. [L0515, 2023; L0516, 2023]
- Gradualization starts from a belief the reader already holds and moves them step by step toward the belief you want them to adopt. Each sentence first accepts the reader's current view before shifting it, instead of stating the new belief all at once. It applies when the reader does not yet believe your core claim. For example, a pet owner who already agrees that fur cleaning is tiring will accept a claim about a faster method more easily than a bold claim with no common ground. [L0515, 2023; L0516, 2023]
- Redefinition handles objections. It reframes how the reader defines the problem or the cost so the objection stops being a reason to say no. Write down the objection the customer already has, then restate it in terms that favour the product. For example, a higher price can be framed as cost per use. List the questions a prospect is likely to ask before buying, and answer each one directly in the copy. [L0515, 2023; L0516, 2023]

### Self-Image, Ego, and Position

- Illusory superiority: most people think they are better, or different, than average, so objections like "maybe for them, but not for me" are the default. Address this by validating the concern first, then reframing it positively. [L0196, 2020+]
- Forer (Barnum) effect: people rate vague, generic descriptions as if they were tailored to them personally. Use Barnum statements such as "you're the kind of person who [generic positive trait]" to validate reader emotions and transfer your intended emotional state. This works because everyone identifies with well-chosen universal values. [L0196, 2020+]
- Good vs. evil frame: position your brand or offer as the hero or powerful ally (the real hero). Position conflicting forces as the enemy, either internal (mindset, habits, ignorance) or external (inferior products, time, government). [L0196, 2020+]
- Us vs. everyone else ("community frame"): position joining your community through purchase as taking a stand for a positive value that "everyone else" has not yet joined. [L0196, 2020+]
- "Unique in a sea of the same" frame: exploit innovation bias by presenting your offer, approach, message, or brand as new and innovative. Build an argument for why the new way is better and why the old way leads to problems. [L0196, 2020+]
- "Because I'm worth it" frame: position your offer in terms of the customer's entitlement to benefits. For example: "You've worked hard to build this; you deserve the peace of mind that comes with knowing it's in the right hands." [L0196, 2020+]
- Pro-innovation bias: the belief that an innovation should be adopted without need for alternatives. The champion may not see its limitations but keeps promoting it. Readers have "shiny object syndrome," so polish the offer by explaining why it is innovative and new. Compare it to "the old way" or state directly why it is innovative. Use the word "innovative" in headlines, such as "Innovative New Approach Unlocks Hidden Potential." Frame even non-innovations as new to the customer, such as "Innovative new device lets you call people while in your car." [L0196, 2020+]

### Repetition, Familiarity, and Persuasion Limits

- Mere exposure effect: people develop preferences for things merely because they are familiar with them. Each impression of an ad or brand is rated more favorably when seen regularly. Repeated exposure to new or unfamiliar products is most helpful. Frequency + Impact + Time = winning message. It takes an average of 21 impressions before a brand is remembered. [L0196, 2020+]
- Repetition builds familiarity and trust, but there is an upper limit. Research showed agreement rising after three exposures and falling sharply after five. Cap the frequency of the same message and monitor for audience fatigue. Where sources differ on frequency: 21 impressions ([L0196, 2020+]) measures brand recall, while the three-to-five exposure range ([L0340, undated]) measures agreement with a message. Use the first to plan awareness and the second to cap a persuasive message. [L0196, 2020+; L0340, undated]
- Use redundancy instead of plain repetition. Express the same message in different wording, format, and sources so the reader sees a fresh version. Multiple different voices making the same point build conviction faster than one voice repeated. [L0340, undated]

### Handling Beliefs and Objections

- Beliefs can be changed in two ways: by changing focus (using emotion or evidence to present a different view of reality) or by changing importance (strengthening beliefs the reader already holds). Strengthening existing beliefs with facts and examples is easier and more reliable than trying to overturn them. [L0340, undated]
- Advertising does not win arguments with beliefs. Accept them as they are and build from them using the prospect's own kind of logic, to show that the product satisfies their desires, works, is relied on by people like them, and beats other products on the need they have. [L0330, undated]
- Telling a reader they are wrong triggers a defensive reaction that ends the sale. Compare facts and evidence side by side instead, and let the reader reach the conclusion without feeling attacked. [L0340, undated]
- Inoculation copy, in order:
  1. Warn the reader that a competitor or opposing view is coming.
  2. Present a weak version of that opposing argument.
  3. Encourage the reader to defend their current choice with reasons they can use.

  Competitor criticism answered this way strengthens the reader's loyalty. [L0340, undated]
- The counter-argument in an inoculation must be weak. A strong attack can change the reader's mind against you. [L0340, undated]
- Preemptive comparison: tell the prospect what to check when comparing offers, such as how a competitor cuts corners, then show that your method avoids the problem. Name specific, verifiable differences such as ingredient sourcing, process time, or guaranteed service time. Write new examples in this same form for your own market. [L0340, undated]
- Comparison charts that show advantages at a glance are effective because they give peripheral thinkers a ready conclusion. Include only differences you can document, and cover benefits such as speed, ease, cleanliness, health, and price. [L0340, undated]
- Two-sided messages persuade more than one-sided ones only when they defend the advertiser's own position while openly weighing the competitor's strengths. Acknowledge what the competitor did well in the past, explain what has changed, and then present your own advantage. [L0340, undated]
- Naming the buyer's thought: before you pitch, name the thought your audience is already having about you, such as age, appearance, credibility, or past bad experiences with sellers. Agree that the thought is reasonable, often with light humor, then move straight to your evidence. This lowers resistance and gets people to listen.
  - "You're probably thinking this is one more brand promising overnight results, and you'd be right to be skeptical. Here's what the numbers actually show."
  - "You've likely seen a dozen stores this week shouting about huge savings, so here's what I'd check before buying anything."
  [L0378, 2019+]

### Sequencing the Emotional Message

- Emotional sequence for high-distress buyers, in order. Tag each script section [NARROWING] or [BROADENING].
  1. Open with a negative emotion that holds attention.
  2. Stay in the negative state long enough to validate it.
  3. Make the transition with permission language that gives the viewer leave to hope.
  4. Present the solution with positive emotions and identity transformation.
  5. Close with a CTA that matches the viewer's state: urgency for narrowed attention, possibility for broadened attention. [L0613, 2026]
- Sequence the message from acknowledgment to hope: negative emotional hooks hold attention on the problem; once the buyer feels understood, permission language and positive, identity-based framing can open up the transformation. Use the same order for the rollercoaster structure (humor, vulnerable story, clear CTA) when the audience is not highly distressed. [L0221, 2026; L0613, 2026; L0378, 2019+]

### Research the buyer's emotions first

- Collect at least 30 direct quotes from Amazon reviews. Research attention-broadening emotions in four sub-types each, with at least 10 sourced statements per sub-type: hope (immediate, long-term, permission, rekindled), relief (daily, major, social, future), pride (personal, social, identity, legacy), belonging (community, family, identity, acceptance), and love or connection (self, friendship, family, and romantic where relevant). [L0613, 2026]
- For every hope statement, judge how fragile or robust it is, what could extinguish it, what would strengthen it, and how its strength changes with the person's distress level. [L0613, 2026]
- Identify the emotions the customer feels about the problem and use them in product descriptions, ads, and other copy. This is the key input for marketing that connects with the customer. [L0872, 2021-22]
- A problem does not always produce negative emotions. A new parent who needs a safe, portable place for the baby to sleep may feel excited and hopeful rather than frustrated, so pitch the marketing to those feelings. [L0872, 2021-22]
- People move away from pain or toward pleasure. Record what the customer wants to avoid, and write copy from both the pain angle and the pleasure angle. [L0907, 2021-22]
- For expensive products, status is often a major problem driving purchases, meaning how other people see the buyer. This is a very powerful problem to target. [L0872, 2021-22]

### Choose one feeling and one dominant motive

- Decide the single feeling you want the reader to have when they finish before writing any copy. Emotion drives action, and a list of benefits without the right feeling rarely produces orders. [L1369, undated]
- Logic convinces the reader but does not make them act. Give logical reasons as the justification, but base the real pull on a primary emotion such as pride, love, fear, or gain. [L1369, undated]
- There are six primary buying motives: love, gain, duty, pride, self-indulgence, and self-preservation. Identify which one the reader feels most strongly before choosing the appeal. [L1369, undated]
- Strengthen a dominant motive with supporting motives. For example, a pride-driven purchase gains force when reinforced with self-indulgence, duty to family, and a measurable gain. Show how repair bills and higher fuel and oil use on an old product erode the price difference of a new one. [L1369, undated]
- Separate the motive that creates the desire from the motive that triggers the action. A reader may want to keep their money but act to protect their credit standing, so the action needs its own motive. [L1369, undated]
- Gain is an easy motive for readers to accept, but never disguise self-interest as concern for others, because readers resent it. Use a love-based appeal only when the product truly protects someone the reader cares about. [L1369, undated]
- There are two reasons readers act: they want something strongly enough to reach for the order, or they fear losing something worth having. The close should trigger one or both. [L1369, undated]
- Quantify the waste the buyer already accepts, such as using six ounces of oil where one is enough, to make them dissatisfied with the current situation. A specific multiple is more persuasive than a general claim that things could be better. [L1369, undated]

### Turn the surface problem into the core emotion

- Negative emotions such as fear, shame, or frustration can make a prospect feel isolated. Acknowledging the problem builds trust and makes the seller credible enough to provide the solution. [L0756, undated]
- Problem-Solution pitches work best when they target the core emotion behind the surface problem. Relief from guilt, shame, self-doubt, embarrassment, loneliness, or the fear of future regret often sells better than relief from the visible problem alone. [L0756, undated]
- Benefits that tap into the most universal personal desires and fears are the most powerful, since everyone shares them: relief from fears, frustrations, and feelings of inadequacy; wanting to feel loved, special, and important; and wanting leisure and pleasure. These map to the seven deadly sins (lust, gluttony, greed, sloth, wrath, envy, pride) as benefit themes. [L1356, 2019+]
- The most effective promise often lies in how the product makes the buyer feel about themselves and how others will see them, not only in what the product does. Emotional promises such as relief, status, confidence, safety, inclusion, and admiration from peers tend to sell more than functional features. [L0756, undated]

## High Probability relationship building and discovery

### Relationship phase: purpose and sequence

- Do not discuss your product during relationship building. Your purpose is to learn who the prospect is, what motivates him and how he reached his current role, so you can decide whether you trust and respect him. If you do not trust him, leave politely, even if you cannot yet say why. [L0770, undated]
- The relationship phase usually happens in the first meeting, and there is no fixed script or question list. The point is to let the conversation produce the information you need, so the exact questions depend on what the prospect says. [L0770, undated]
- Prospects also judge the salesperson during relationship building, and trust shows through how you behave rather than what you say. A relationship of trust and respect gives you an advantage over competitors, because most buyers prefer to deal with people they trust. [L0770, undated]
- Do not go on with a long relationship phase when the prospect has not yet shown trust or respect. Leaving early costs little, while investing time in someone you do not trust wastes the rest of the process. [L0770, undated]

### When the prospect wants to skip the relationship phase

Use this in a flat, unemotional tone when a prospect in the relationship phase wants to jump straight into business:

```
Before we talk about my product, I like to learn how my customers operate, because that tells me how to serve them. I also need to find out whether I trust and respect the people I work with, and you have the same chance to judge me. So, are you willing to continue this kind of conversation, or would you rather I leave?
```

If he chooses to leave, end the visit politely and treat him as a low-probability prospect. [L0770, undated]

### Opening the relationship

- To open a relationship, make a neutral observation about something visible or known, such as the building, the location or the company, and attach a question to it. Then base each follow-up on the prospect's last answer rather than a fixed list. [L0770, undated]
  - Example: The loading area here is unusually wide. Was it built for this kind of product flow? [L0770, undated]
  - Example: You mentioned the new site opened last spring. What made you move the team there? [L0770, undated]
- Use open-ended questions while building the relationship, such as why, what did you do, how did you handle that, and was that something you wanted to do. Open questions let the prospect explain himself instead of answering yes or no. [L0770, undated]
- Prospects tend to answer almost any authentic question truthfully when they sense you genuinely want the answer and are not manipulating them. Sincere interest cannot be faked, and prospects will cut short a conversation if they sense you are only going through the motions. [L0770, undated]
- Answering a personal disclosure with an unrelated small-talk question, such as about family or hobbies, signals that you are not really interested and damages trust. Respond with a follow-up on what he just said, such as asking why it happened or how he handled it. [L0770, undated]

### Listening and note-taking

- Ask a question, then listen to the full answer before thinking about your next question. If an answer is incomplete, ask a follow-up to clarify it for yourself and sometimes for the prospect. [L0770, undated]
- Keep your own talking to about 25 percent of a meeting. If you are talking more than that, the meeting is probably going badly; ask why the prospect is unresponsive, and end the meeting if you cannot fix it, unless he is naturally quiet. [L0770, undated]
- Take complete notes at every contact, recording the prospect's facts and opinions in his exact words. Exact wording is valuable later when you define his requirements, and it shows him that you value what he says. Missing details from a first meeting are hard to recover later. [L0770, undated]

### Handling negativity

Deal with anger, sarcasm, threats, abuse or any other negativity before anything else. Speak in a flat, neutral tone, without judging and without sympathy.

1. Say neutrally that he seems upset. [L0770, undated]
2. If he is still upset after explaining why, repeat the observation in the same flat tone, without stressing any word. [L0770, undated]
3. If he is still upset, suggest that you discuss the matter at another time. [L0770, undated]
4. If he calms down, continue the conversation. If he becomes upset again, return to step 1 and keep your tone even. [L0770, undated]
5. If he stays upset, tell him you are not willing to meet right now because it would not serve either of you, and ask whether he wants to schedule another appointment or not. [L0770, undated]

- Do not respond with anger to a low-probability prospect. He is simply not a fit, so recognize it, end the meeting politely and move on to the next prospect. [L0770, undated]

### Discovery and disqualification questions

Use the wording below in this fixed order, swapping "this product" for your own product or service, and do not mention your company by name. [L0770, undated]

1. **Need:** Why do you need this product? [L0770, undated]
2. **Want:** Do you want it? If yes, ask why; if no, end the meeting. [L0770, undated]
3. **Financial:** This product will cost about [price]; are you prepared to spend that? [L0770, undated]
4. **Time:** If you decide to proceed, when would you want this to start, and what happens if it doesn't start then? [L0770, undated]
5. **Decision-makers:** When you make a decision like this, who do you usually talk it over with? Then ask whether he'll let you speak with them before you prepare a proposal. [L0770, undated]
6. **Authority:** If you went ahead, who else would have to agree? Handle this the same way as decision-makers. [L0770, undated]
7. **Commitments:** What would happen if you don't buy this product? [L0770, undated]
8. **Brand preference:** If you had to decide right now, without talking to anyone, which brand would you buy? [L0770, undated]
9. **Supplier preference:** Is there someone you'd rather do business with? [L0770, undated]
10. **Internal procedure:** What is your process for issuing a purchase order? Have him write it down and read it back before you move on. [L0770, undated]
11. **Personal motivation:** What would it mean to you personally if you didn't purchase this product? [L0770, undated]
12. **Personal prejudices:** Is there any reason you wouldn't want to do business with me? Anything we haven't covered yet, or any emotional reason? [L0770, undated]
13. **Hidden obstacles:** What could be lurking in the background that would prevent this from happening? [L0770, undated]
14. **Close:** Once questions 1 to 13 are answered satisfactorily and he hasn't disqualified himself: if I can meet all your criteria for this product, what will you do? [L0770, undated]

- Discovery questions are general, not about your brand, so the answers describe the prospect's own needs. Ask each question in the exact same words every time and write down the answers precisely, since the wording and tone of his answers can matter later. [L0770, undated]
- The prospect can disqualify himself at any of the fourteen discovery questions. Each answer he gives without disqualifying himself builds the relationship and the mutual commitment, and each problem you handle early prevents a deal-breaker later. [L0770, undated]
- Do not disqualify a prospect after an objection such as a price limit without first asking why he holds that position. The reason may be solvable, and asking why is better than guessing at his thinking. [L0770, undated]

### Decision-makers and approvers

Request to speak with the decision-maker or approver before preparing a proposal:

```
I need to talk with [name or role] before I prepare a proposal. Are you willing to arrange that?
```

If he refuses, reply in a flat tone:

```
That's not the way I work. I won't prepare a proposal without understanding their concerns and objectives, because I don't want a proposal rejected over something we could have handled up front. What would you like to do?
```

If he still will not arrange it, disqualify him. [L0770, undated]

### Unclear, evasive and conflicting answers

When a prospect's answer is unclear or evasive, do not move on until you are fully satisfied.

1. Ask for clarification, for example by saying you do not understand what he meant and asking what he means. [L0770, undated]
2. If he is still unclear, state that his answer seems evasive and ask whether there is a problem. [L0770, undated]
3. If he still avoids answering, tell him you will not move on until the question is answered. [L0770, undated]
4. Carry on only when you have a clear answer, because an unresolved issue usually returns later as a problem. [L0770, undated]

- When a prospect gives conflicting timing, such as urgent on the phone and six months away in the meeting, ask him directly about the discrepancy before going further. Then either reach a clear commitment for the timing or set a specific return date that he chooses. [L0770, undated]

## High Probability commitment, conditions and closing

### Sequence of the Sale

- Do not present a product, quote, or proposal until the prospect has committed to buy if the product meets his stated conditions. The order is: questions, agreement on conditions, commitment, and only then the demonstration or proposal. [L0770, undated]
- Selling in this approach means reaching a series of agreements with prospects who first acknowledge that they need, want, and can afford the product, and who commit to buying at a set time if their conditions are met. It is not about persuading anyone to act. A deal exists only when both sides have agreed. [L0770, undated]

### Closing Through Mutual Commitment

- Closing means negotiating mutual commitments, never asking for the order. The prospect chooses yes or no, and you are equally satisfied with either answer, because your commitment is to the process rather than to making a sale. [L0770, undated]
- Ask only questions whose answers you need. Avoid rhetorical or trial closes, including small either-or choices meant to push a sale. Prospects recognize these tactics and resent them, and they undermine trust. [L0770, undated]
- Traditional selling offers a choice of yes or yes: the seller argues for a sale no matter the prospect's view. High Probability selling offers a choice of yes or no and accepts either answer, which is why it produces commitments that hold. [L0770, undated]
- Example of a real decision: "Does a one-week delivery window work for you, or not?" Compare "Shall we get you set up?", which only invites a reluctant yes. (Own illustration.) [L0770, undated]

### Defining the Conditions of Satisfaction

Open the conditions phase with this template after discovery is complete:

```
It is time to define your Conditions of Satisfaction. I would like us to write down exactly what you require, every requirement, because that is how I determine whether we can provide it. Are you willing to do that?

After he agrees, read back what he said during discovery and ask whether it is correct and whether he wants to add anything.
```
[L0770, undated]

Establish the Conditions of Satisfaction in this order:

1. Confirm that the discovery questions are fully answered and that every decision-maker and approver is involved. [L0770, undated]
2. Tell the prospect that you now need to define exactly what he requires, and ask whether he is willing to do that. [L0770, undated]
3. Ask what he will do if you return with a proposal that meets his conditions. [L0770, undated]
4. If he does not give a clear commitment, tell him that you will only prepare a proposal with a commitment to buy if the conditions are met, and disqualify him if he refuses. [L0770, undated]
5. Once he commits, read back what he said during discovery and ask whether it is correct and whether he wants to add anything. [L0770, undated]
6. Ask for each condition in his exact words and record them. Raise any conditions he has not mentioned that need addressing. [L0770, undated]
7. Specify exactly what he will and will not accept. Agree how each condition will be measured, such as a timed test or a signed sample. [L0770, undated]
8. Tell him clearly what you can and cannot provide, and offer alternatives where possible without hiding the negatives. [L0770, undated]
9. Negotiate any conditions you cannot meet. If an unmet condition cannot be negotiated, disqualify him. If you agree on conditions you can meet, you have a deal. [L0770, undated]
10. At the next meeting, present a proposal that shows you meet all conditions, and complete the transaction. [L0770, undated]

### Making Conditions Measurable

- Make each condition measurable so both sides can check it. "Easy to set up" becomes a time limit for a shipping clerk to set up the package, including taping. "Vivid four-color art" becomes a signed sample of the minimum quality the buyer will accept. [L0770, undated]

### References

When a prospect wants references from existing customers:

1. Ask how many references he wants. [L0770, undated]
2. Ask whether he will call those references while you are present. [L0770, undated]
3. Ask what he will do if the references give positive endorsements. [L0770, undated]
4. Record the agreed answers in his exact words as a condition to be met. [L0770, undated]

### Budgetary Quotes

- When you present a budgetary quote to a prospect who has not committed, ask in the same breath what he will do if the price is acceptable. This ties the estimate to a real commitment instead of sending a free number he can shop around. [L0770, undated]

### Testing the Commitment

- After a prospect commits, test that commitment by asking whether he is sure and why he wants to proceed. His reasons often surface, and the answer strengthens his commitment. Testing also brings hidden doubts into the open before you leave. [L0770, undated]

### Handling Requests You Cannot Meet

When a prospect asks for something you cannot deliver, such as a finish you do not offer:

1. State plainly that you do not offer it, and give the reason in one sentence. [L0770, undated]
2. Offer your available option and ask whether he will accept it. [L0770, undated]
3. If he insists on the unavailable option, end the meeting instead of hoping he changes his mind. [L0770, undated]

Use this template for follow-up replies to question 14, delivered in a flat tone, depending on his response:

```
Follow-up replies to question 14 are delivered in a flat tone, depending on his response. If he answers 'What do you mean?', reply: What do you think I mean? If he says he will seriously consider it, reply: I'm not willing to prepare a proposal unless I have a commitment that we will do business if I meet your criteria. What would you like to do? Unless he clearly states that he will give you his business if you meet his criteria, disqualify him.
```
[L0770, undated]

### Pushback and Disqualification

- Commitments tend to hold when they are freely made. A prospect who says yes will usually honor it, while an order that rests on pressure is likely to be cancelled. [L0770, undated]
- A prospect who breaks a commitment should be disqualified in most markets. In markets with few buyers, you may have to keep working with him. [L0770, undated]
- When a prospect pushes back hard against being disqualified, still disqualify him if you have real doubts about his commitment. A serious buyer rarely accepts disqualification without a struggle, but the struggle alone does not prove that he is qualified. [L0770, undated]

### Common Mistakes

- Do not move from discovery straight to the prospect's requests without first asking for commitment. Otherwise the prospect takes the lead, you end up doing what he asks, and the process loses its structure. [L0770, undated]
- If you find yourself answering the prospect's questions instead of asking your own, return to asking questions and asking for commitment. It is harder to regain control once you have lost it, but a commitment request can still restore it. [L0770, undated]
- Do not assume the sale is made because discovery went well or the prospect seems friendly. Until he commits in writing or aloud to the conditions, he has not committed, and acting as though he has leads to lost orders. [L0770, undated]
- Do not try to convince a prospect to change his mind after he has declined a commitment or set a limit you cannot meet. Ending the meeting saves the effort, and trying to talk him out of his conditions creates resistance. [L0770, undated]

### Worked Cases

- **Toy manufacturer, testing the commitment:** A seller for a large toy manufacturer reviewed the buyer's conditions, then asked what the buyer would do if a demonstration on the following Tuesday met all of them. The buyer gave a noncommittal reply. The seller said he would not prepare a proposal without a commitment and that a no was acceptable. After the buyer pushed back, the seller held his position calmly, and the buyer then committed to an order if the demonstration met the conditions. The seller asked whether he was sure, then asked why. The buyer gave his reasons and said there was no doubt that he would order if the conditions were met. [L0770, undated]
- **Packaging supplier, existing buyer for Star:** A packaging supplier with an existing buyer for a brand called Star learned that a rival supplier was failing on another brand, Sun, which had 17 packages needing new artwork. The seller did not pitch. He asked about the problem and what the buyer needed.
  - The product manager wanted four packages within five weeks and said the other 13 could come later, with an expected lead time of 8 to 15 weeks.
  - The seller agreed to complete artwork for the four in one week, to deliver them four weeks after artwork approval, to fax proofs every morning because the work would run on overtime, and to quote the price the next day in line with the Star line.
  - The product manager was not going to cancel his existing order. He promised at least half of his packaging requirements if delivery was met. He required top quality, on-time delivery, no shortages, and no more than 2 percent overage.
  - The buyer's purchasing manager signed the first order the next day, after the product manager approved the quote. [L0770, undated]
- **Advertising manager, point-of-sale packaging:** A seller who had established a relationship with an advertising manager asked whether the packaging would be worth it if it gave attractive point-of-sale advertising for about one dollar more per unit. The buyer asked about the cost and then asked for samples. The seller said samples of similar packaging could be shown, but custom samples would only be made against a commitment. He offered to credit the cost of a sample run against the first production order. The buyer agreed, and the two wrote the purchase requisition together. While writing it, the seller went through each condition the buyer had for the packaging, which confirmed the order. [L0770, undated]

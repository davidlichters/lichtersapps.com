### Visualization and mental rehearsal

#### Timing and daily practice

- Give any new mindset practice at least 21 days before judging whether it works, because roughly that long is needed for an old mental image to fade and a new one to settle. During this window, do not keep checking your progress or debate whether the method is valid. [L1206, 2015+]
- During the first 21 days of a new mindset practice, review the core principles about three times a week and look for examples in your own life and in people you observe. Do not measure your progress or argue with the method during this period. The point is to test the practice, not to judge it early. [L1206, 2015+]
- Practice visualization every day, since consistency matters more than length. Sessions of 10 to 15 minutes can produce noticeable change, and 30 minutes can be built up to once the daily habit is established. [L1206, 2015+]
- The nervous system responds to a vividly and richly imagined experience almost as it does to a real one. Mental rehearsal can therefore build skills and confidence that carry over into action. [L1206, 2015+]
- Spend most of your time focused on the process and the next steps toward a goal. Revisit the end result briefly, once or twice a day, during visualization, then return to the process. [L1206, 2015+]
- Forcing the answer to how a goal will be reached, before the goal is clearly defined, blocks the mind from finding it. Define the goal first; the practical steps tend to surface when you are relaxed. [L1206, 2015+]
- When willpower and imagination conflict, imagination usually wins. Goals should therefore be pictured calmly rather than forced through strained effort. [L1206, 2015+]

#### Daily 30-minute visualization

1. Set aside about 30 minutes alone and undisturbed each day. [L1206, 2015+]
2. Sit or lie down comfortably and close your eyes. [L1206, 2015+]
3. Picture yourself on a large screen, or see the scene from your own eyes, whichever feels more vivid. [L1206, 2015+]
4. Add small sensory details to the scene: sounds, smells, textures, objects, and the surroundings. [L1206, 2015+]
5. Picture yourself acting, responding, and feeling in an ideal way in the situation. Ignore how you acted yesterday. [L1206, 2015+]
6. Do not tell yourself you will act this way tomorrow. Imagine acting this way now, during the session. [L1206, 2015+]
7. Let the emotion of already being that person come up as you watch the scene. [L1206, 2015+]
8. If your mind wanders, return to the picture calmly without self-criticism. [L1206, 2015+]
9. Repeat the session every day, starting with 10 to 15 minutes if 30 is too much. [L1206, 2015+]

#### Relaxation before imagery

1. Sit comfortably, or stand. Lying down works, but it may lead to falling asleep and reduce the benefit. [L1206, 2015+]
2. Scan your body for tension and consciously let go in the head, neck, torso, and legs. [L1206, 2015+]
3. Smile inwardly to help the body relax. [L1206, 2015+]
4. Breathe deeply and follow each inhale and exhale. [L1206, 2015+]
5. Spend about five minutes relaxing with conscious effort, then stop controlling the muscles and let imagery bring on the relaxed state. [L1206, 2015+]
6. Use one relaxation image: heavy limbs as if made of concrete sinking into a surface, a body held loosely like a marionette with slack strings, or a body deflating slowly like a balloon. [L1206, 2015+]
7. Or recall a calm past scene in detail, noting background sounds, temperature, and textures. [L1206, 2015+]

#### Anchoring a positive self-image

1. Relax first, using the breathing and relaxation steps. [L1206, 2015+]
2. Recall any past occasion when you did something well. It can be trivial, such as learning a basic skill, and its size and date do not matter. [L1206, 2015+]
3. Replay the memory in detail until it brings back a positive, pleasant feeling in the present. [L1206, 2015+]
4. Picture yourself in a future situation the way you want to be. [L1206, 2015+]
5. Attach the same feeling from the past memory to that future picture, adding emotion to what you see. [L1206, 2015+]
6. If attention drifts, bring it back without judging yourself. [L1206, 2015+]

#### Motor rehearsal

1. Form a clear mental image of the shot, movement or task you are about to perform. [L1206, 2015+]
2. Rehearse it in your mind in full detail, including how it feels when it goes right. [L1206, 2015+]
3. Step up and perform, relying on the practiced pattern rather than consciously controlling each movement. [L1206, 2015+]
4. If the result misses, note the difference and adjust the mental image before the next attempt. [L1206, 2015+]

#### Evidence from studies and cases

| Study or case | What was practiced | Result | Ids |
|---|---|---|---|
| Basketball free throws, controlled study | Imagined shooting for about 20 minutes a day over 20 days | Scoring improved 23 percent. Physical practice improved 24 percent. No practice showed no gain. | [L1206, 2015+] |
| Basketball free throws, correction of misses | Imagined practice that included correcting each missed shot after it happened | 23 percent scoring gain over 20 days, close to the 24 percent gain from physical practice | [L1206, 2015+] |
| Darts, controlled experiment | Sitting before a target each day and imagining throws | Accuracy improved about as much as real throwing practice, suggesting mental rehearsal can match physical rehearsal for well-defined motor skills | [L1206, 2015+] |
| Salespeople rehearsing conversations | Imagined customer conversations and objections, with the best reply worked out for each | Sales increases of 100 to 150 percent in some groups and up to 400 percent for individuals. These figures are anecdotal, not a reliable benchmark. | [L1206, 2015+] |

- Mental practice works best when it includes correcting errors, not only replaying successful shots. [L1206, 2015+]
- Pitching academy: a coach taught high school and college baseball players mental imagery and relaxation exercises to help them reach 90 miles per hour. Many players could not reach it by effort alone, but many hit 90 for the first time once their bodies relaxed. Their self-images then adjusted to the new velocity, and within 18 months the number of players at the facility who broke 90 mph rose from 18 to 98. The coach's account is anecdotal and not a controlled study. [L1206, 2015+]
- Golf: a beginner who had never played sat in an easy chair for five minutes each day and visualized the key points of a correct swing, with no practice on the course. Several days later, with no physical preparation, the beginner joined a regular foursome and shot nine holes in even par, 36. The instructor's core rule was that a clear mental picture of the correct action must come before performing it. His claim that the mental side makes up 90 percent of golf, the physical side 8 percent and the mechanical side 2 percent is an unverified estimate. [L1206, 2015+]
- Violin: a violinist was thinking of retiring because a wrist injury made regular practice difficult. A coach asked him to play the violin in his mind without holding the instrument. A week later he gave what he considered the best concert of his life, and he dropped the idea of retiring. [L1206, 2015+]
- Chess: a challenger reportedly prepared for a championship match by playing only in his mind for three months, after giving up smoking and drinking and doing calisthenics. He defeated a champion who had been expected to win easily. The case suggests that structured mental play can prepare a competitor for a specific contest. [L1206, 2015+]
- Dentist fear: a visualization exercise did not work until the person added concrete sensory details, such as the smell of antiseptic, the feel of the chair's armrests, and the sight of the dentist's hands approaching. Once the imagined scene was detailed enough, it produced an effect close to real experience. Vague imagery gives a much weaker result than detailed imagery. [L1206, 2015+]
- Interview preparation:
  1. Plan the interview in advance and list the questions that are likely to be asked. [L1206, 2015+]
  2. Think through a clear answer to each question. [L1206, 2015+]
  3. Rehearse the whole interview in your mind, including questions you did not prepare for. [L1206, 2015+]
  4. Practice reacting spontaneously, because rehearsal builds confidence and the ability to adapt even when the actual questions differ. [L1206, 2015+]

### Roles and self-view in practice

- Ignore the outside verdict and measure progress against your own standard, because the goal is a stable self-image rather than external approval. [L1206, 2015+]
- Practice the role before the proof arrives, then collect each completed piece of work as evidence for the new identity. [L1041, 2023]

#### Further details

- Success loop: 1. Take action. 2. Collect feedback on what the action produced. 3. Correct your approach based on that feedback. 4. Take action again with the correction and repeat the cycle. [L0831, 2019]

- Follow the success loop described by Jim Rohn in this order: 1) take action, 2) get feedback from the result, 3) make corrections based on that feedback, 4) take more action. More action produces more feedback, which allows better-informed actions in the future. Most people stall because self-limiting thoughts stop them from taking the first action. [L1513, 2019]

19.3 Break-even and target ROAS, CPA and allowable ad spend

Core definitions: ROAS, CPA and cost per purchase

  • ROAS is revenue divided by ad spend. Examples: $100 ÷ $25 = 4x; $1,000 ÷ $100 = 10x; $1 spent that returns $1.60 gives 1.6; $10 spent that returns $80 gives 8; $1 that returns $2 gives 2. [L0327.011, L0327.012, L0445.003, L0645.007, L0875.003]
  • Source B defines ROAS as revenue attributed to the ads divided by ad spend, so 15x means 15 units of revenue for every unit spent on ads. [L0719.016] (older, 2021-22)
  • Source B also defines ROAS as sale price ÷ cost per acquisition. A $2,000 product at a $500 CPA gives 4x (400% in Google Analytics). [L0446.006] (older, 2021-22)
  • Google Analytics shows ROAS as a percentage: 4x appears as 400% and 10x as 1,000%. Compare analytics ROAS with calculated targets in the same format. [L0446.018, L0686.008] (older, 2021-22)
  • Source A gives the same ROAS definition with a 10x example: $100,000 revenue from $10,000 spend equals 10x. [L0808.033]
  • Cost per acquisition (CPA), cost per purchase (CPP) and cost per sale describe the same measure: the cost to win one customer or one order. They are used interchangeably in ad platforms and reports. [L0446.019, L0875.030]
  • CPA = total ad spend ÷ number of purchases. Read it in Meta Ads Manager or calculate it from Shopify data. [L0445.007, L0875.007]
  • Cost per sale = total ad spend ÷ orders from that spend in the same period. Example: $14,000 over 366 orders is about $38.25 per sale. This figure should fall when paid traffic converts better. [L0400.018] (older, 2021-22)
  • The total campaign spend for cost per sale must include clicks on every product in the campaign, including products that never sold. Otherwise the figure is understated. Example: $152 of spend with 2 sales gives $76 per sale. [L0748.017]
  • Compare ROAS against CPA: a $20 CPA acquisition that generates $100 in revenue is more profitable than a $10 CPA that generates $50, because profit per sale matters more than cost per acquisition. [L0327.010]
  • Source A: minimum product price = cost per sale × target ROAS. At a 10x target, a $74 sale needs a product priced at least $740, and a $52 sale needs at least $520. Any product priced above this threshold gives a ROAS above 10x. [L0748.002]

Break-even ROAS and break-even CPA

Definition and calculation

  • Break-even ROAS is the minimum ROAS at which revenue exactly covers COGS and ad spend, leaving zero profit and zero loss. Above it the campaign is profitable; below it is a loss. Knowing it shows which campaigns are viable. [L0327.014]
  • Formula (Source A): Break-even ROAS = Revenue ÷ (Revenue − COGS) = Revenue ÷ Profit. Example: $1,000 revenue with $400 COGS gives $600 profit, so $1,000 ÷ $600 = 1.67x. [L0327.015]
  • Simple example: $1,000 revenue with 50% COGS ($500) leaves $500 profit. If all profit is spent on ads, break-even ROAS = $1,000 ÷ $500 = 2.0x. Above 2.0x is profitable; below is a loss. [L0327.016]
  • Conflict within the Source A source: one formula line reads "(Revenue − Cost of Goods) ÷ Revenue", which as written gives 0.6, not 1.67. The worked result of 1.67x matches Revenue ÷ Profit. Use Revenue ÷ (Revenue − COGS). [L0225.015, L0327.015]
  • Sauna example: $400 sourcing cost, $1,000 revenue, $600 gross profit. A $300 ad budget gives a target ROAS of 3.3x (1,000 ÷ 300) and a break-even ROAS of 1.67x (1,000 ÷ 600). [L0225.033]
  • Google Ads version (Source A): Breakeven ROAS = Revenue ÷ (Revenue − COGS & Fees). Example: revenue $10,000, COGS plus fees $6,000, profit before ads $4,000, so break-even ROAS = 2.5x. [L0329.001]
  • Your margin before ads, as a percentage of revenue, equals the maximum ad spend percentage at break-even. A 40% margin lets you spend up to 40% of sales on ads and still break even. Reducing COGS and fees raises the profit available for scaling ads. [L0329.003]
  • Source C: Break-even ROAS = Selling Price ÷ (Selling Price − Expenses). At the start, Expenses = product cost + shipping. This is an early estimate that does not yet cover other costs, so calculate it before launch and judge every ad set against it. [L0375.009, L0375.010]
  • Break-even ROAS = Selling price ÷ (Selling price − COGS). For $60 with $20 COGS this gives 1.5. Dividing by the $20 cost alone would give 3 and is wrong. [L0875.026]
  • Conflict within the Source C source: L0445.004 and L0875.004 write the formula as Selling price ÷ COGS, which gives 3 for $60 and $20, yet both state the result as 1.5. The 1.5 matches Selling price ÷ (Selling price − COGS), as L0875.026 says. Use the corrected form. [L0445.004, L0875.004, L0875.026]
  • Source C tool example: COGS $10, selling price $40, break-even ROAS 1.3x. This matches Selling price ÷ (Selling price − COGS). [L0229.009]
  • Gross-margin shortcut: Break-even ROAS = 1 ÷ gross margin %, since at that ROAS the margin covers ad spend. [L0693.050]
  • Break-even ROAS before fees = selling price ÷ (selling price − product − shipping). Payment and app fees raise the number, so include them for the real break-even. [L0645.020]
  • Dropship Toolkit example: a product costing $18 sold at $39.99 has a break-even ROAS of 1.85 in the tool. A plain price-minus-cost calculation gives 1.82 (39.99 ÷ 21.99), so the tool likely deducts some extra cost. [L0661.065]
  • Example with a $39.90 price and $17 fulfilment cost: $22.90 margin gives 1.74. A stated profit of $22 gives 1.81. Check which profit figure a calculator uses before relying on it. [L0440.066]
  • Source C KPI sheet formula: Break-even ROAS = AOV ÷ (AOV × (1 − fee %) − COGS). Example: 60 ÷ (60 × 0.96 − 20) = 1.60. The daily business cost is not part of this ROAS formula. [L0900.006]
  • Source E: Break-even ROAS = new-customer AOV (tax-inclusive, as shown in ad platforms) ÷ cost per order that makes contribution margin zero (gross profit per order). Example: $238.49 ÷ $130.27 = 1.83. At 1.83 the business neither makes nor loses money on first orders. [L0433.009]
  • Break-even ROAS of 1.3-1.5 is the stated ideal range for any product. A break-even near 1.9 means only a thin profit even at a ROAS of 2. [L0645.018]
  • Aim for a break-even ROAS of 1.6 or lower, which gives roughly three times the margin on the product. This leaves room for ad spend, app and staff costs and payment fees. [L0875.015]
  • For a product with break-even ROAS 1.5, a ROAS of about 1.6-1.8 or higher is profitable. A ROAS of about 1.3-1.4 or lower is a loss. Small ROAS changes decide profit or loss. [L0875.028]

Break-even cost per purchase (margin per order)

  • Break-even cost per purchase = selling price − (product cost + shipping cost). Example: $6 product + $4 shipping, $40 price: break-even margin $30 per purchase. This $30 is the minimum the ad set must spend before you decide to continue, pause or kill it. [L0229.002]
  • Break-even cost per purchase = selling price − COGS. Example: $60 − $20 = $40. CPA below $40 yields profit; above $40 yields a loss. [L0445.005, L0875.005]
  • Break-even cost per purchase is the profit margin per product. Spend at least this amount on an ad set or ad before judging it, and judge at ad level later for more precision. [L0640.014]
  • For first tests, fees and other expenses can be ignored, but subtract them later as they become known, for example transaction fees. Example: $30 price − $10 product − $10 shipping = $20. [L0648.030]
  • Full break-even CPP with the KPI sheet: AOV × (1 − average fee %) − average COGS including shipping − daily cost of running the business. Example: 60 × (1 − 0.04) − 20 − 0.9524 = $36.65. This is lower than the simple $40 margin because fees and daily costs are included. [L0900.001]
  • Break-even CPP must be checked against actual cost per purchase. If CPA is $40 and break-even CPA is $36.65, the seller loses about $3.35 per sale (the source text says $3.50). [L0445.016, L0875.018]
  • Procedure: calculate break-even cost per acquisition, write it down, and keep the actual cost per purchase below it. Any cost above it means the product is losing money. [L0692.022]
  • Per product: know COGS, break-even ROAS and break-even cost per purchase before running paid ads. These are the baseline KPIs that show whether a product can make money at all. [L0445.001]
  • Source C formula for break-even CPP as the margin, plus the note on kill rules: use it to set kill rules for campaigns. [L0693.050, L0692.022]

Break-even CPA and max cost (Source B, older)

  • The maximum you can pay through paid traffic is the break-even CPA, equal to net profit before marketing when you count one purchase per customer. If you use customer lifetime value, the maximum can be set against it, which may mean a loss on the first order. [L0446.003] (older, 2021-22)
  • Break-even is not a good target for every sale, because a business that only breaks even earns nothing. Set the ideal target CPA below break-even so the chosen margin stays in the business. [L0446.004] (older, 2021-22)
  • Formula: Break-even CPA = net profit before traffic per sale. Target CPA = net profit before traffic − profit to keep per sale. Example: $500 net profit before traffic and $300 wanted profit gives a target CPA of $200. [L0446.005] (older, 2021-22)
  • Worked example: a product sold for $200 and bought for $100 leaves $100 gross profit. Free shipping and other per-sale costs take $20, so net profit before marketing is $80. Keeping $40 as profit and spending up to $40 per sale on ads gives a target CPA of $40. [L0446.002] (older, 2021-22)
  • The unit cost is the total cost of one sale before marketing: product cost, shipping, staff costs such as customer service, and all other overheads divided down to a single sale. Net profit before traffic = selling price − this full unit cost. [L0686.004] (older, 2021-22)
  • Break-even max cost per sale is normally calculated on a single first purchase. A store with repeat buyers can instead use customer lifetime value, which may allow a maximum above break-even and means accepting a loss on the first order. [L0686.002] (older, 2021-22)
  • Before scaling paid traffic, know the maximum cost per sale and the ideal cost per sale for each product, or at least for product groups on average. Without both numbers, decisions to scale or pull back are guesswork. [L0686.001] (older, 2021-22)
  • The ideal cost per sale sits below the maximum so that each sale keeps a chosen margin. Running every sale at break-even means the business never earns money. [L0686.003] (older, 2021-22)

Break-even by units and volume

  • Break-even units for a one-time sponsorship fee = fee ÷ revenue per unit. Example: a $10,000 fee at $100 per unit needs 100 units sold to recover the fee in revenue, not profit. [L0492.051]
  • Break-even sales per month = total monthly fixed costs ÷ margin left per sale after product, fees and acquisition costs. This shows how many sales cover overhead. [L0436.011]
  • Paid-ad dropshipping customer acquisition cost is often about $5-15 per new customer, depending on niche and market. Use your own measured figure once ads run. [L0436.005]
  • Paid ads cost roughly $7-12 in ad spend per conversion for the products discussed. Include this when judging whether a product can sell at a profit with ads. [L0706.008]
  • Include more costs than COGS and ads once orders are numerous: payment processing (around 4% for Shopify Payments, Stripe or PayPal), app subscriptions, Shopify transaction fees, and virtual assistant or staff costs. Add them to the break-even calculation after the first few profitable weeks. [L0875.014]
  • For a store with several products, calculate one break-even ROAS from the average selling price and average fulfilment cost. Keep prices and fulfilment costs close across products so this one number stays accurate. [L0642.049]
  • Calculate for each acquisition channel the cost to acquire one customer, the time to break even, and the point at which that customer becomes profitable. These three numbers set how much you can safely spend on traffic. [L0378.085]

Target and desired ROAS from profit allocation

Target ROAS with a profit share (Source A)

  • Target ROAS = Revenue ÷ (Profit × Allocation %), where Allocation % is the share of profit you choose to spend on ads. Example: $1,000 revenue, $600 profit, 50% allocated to ads gives 1,000 ÷ 300 = 3.3x. [L0327.018]
  • Procedure:
    1. Calculate break-even ROAS first (e.g. 1.67x).
    2. Decide the percentage of profit to spend on ads (e.g. 50%).
    3. Calculate target ad spend = Profit × Percentage (e.g. $600 × 50% = $300).
    4. Calculate target ROAS = Revenue ÷ Ad spend (e.g. $1,000 ÷ $300 = 3.3x).
    5. A higher target ROAS means a lower ad spend allocation and more profit remaining. [L0327.019]
  • Example: $1,000 revenue with $600 profit before ads. Allocating 50% of profit to ads gives $300 ad spend and a target ROAS of 3.3x. $300 of profit remains after paying for ads. [L0327.020]
  • Spreadsheet columns for break-even and target ROAS, in calculation order, with the lesson's example values:
Column Example value
Revenue $1,000

COGS and fees (%) 40%
Profit before ads ($) $600

Target ROAS 3.3x

[L0327.026]

  • Break-even is the point where all profit before ads is spent on advertising. With $1,000 revenue and $600 profit before ads, spending $601 on ads loses $1, while spending $599 keeps $1. Every dollar of ad spend above profit before ads is a dollar of loss. [L0327.025]

Desired ROAS for a profit target (Source C)

  • Desired ROAS is the ROAS a campaign must reach to hit a chosen profit target, such as 15% or 20%. Calculate it from COGS and average order value, not only from break-even ROAS. [L0445.006]
  • Desired ROAS = AOV ÷ Profit Target CPP. Example: 60 ÷ 27.65 = 2.17. [L0900.007]
  • Desired ROAS for a target margin uses COGS, AOV and the chosen profit percentage (for example 10%, 15% or 20%). It is read from a KPI calculation sheet. [L0875.006]
  • Required ROAS for a profit target = 1 ÷ (margin after COGS and all non-ad costs, as a share of revenue, minus target profit share). Non-ad costs include payment fees, app fees and staff costs. Example: 66.7% margin, 4% fees, 15% target gives 1 ÷ 0.477 ≈ 2.1. A sheet with extra costs can land slightly higher, such as 2.17. [L0875.032]
  • For a 15% profit target with COGS $20 and a given AOV, the target ROAS is 2.17. If Facebook ads alone run at 2.17 ROAS, the 15% margin is reached. Higher ROAS means a larger margin and possibly a reason to scale. [L0445.015, L0875.017]
  • A cost per purchase near $27.65 corresponds to roughly 15% profit under the same COGS and AOV, so it is a good target when using the calculation sheet. [L0875.019]
  • Source C benchmark ROAS formula: Benchmark ROAS = BE-ROAS + 20% margin. Your own benchmark can use a different margin, because not everyone aims for 20%. [L0376.020]
  • Scaling target: aim for a ROAS above break-even by at least one point (break-even ROAS + 1). [L0650.045]

Desired ROAS from first-order contribution (Source E)

  • Target ROAS for a desired first-order contribution margin: amount to keep = AOV × target margin %. Required ROAS = AOV ÷ (gross profit per order − amount to keep). Example with $238.49 AOV and $130.27 gross profit: for 15% margin, keep $35.77, and required ROAS = 238.49 ÷ (130.27 − 35.77) = 2.52. For 25% margin, required ROAS = 3.38. [L0433.010]
  • Break-even ROAS shows the minimum ad return to avoid losses, but target ROAS sets the margin you want. Set targets from the profit you want to keep, and compare them with ROAS in the ad account. If ad ROAS meets the target but profit is still low, the problem is unit economics, not advertising. [L0433.011]

Maximum acquisition cost and the 10% rule

Maximum acquisition cost

  • Maximum acquisition cost = profit per sale × share of profit you are willing to give to the ad platform. Example: $50 product with $15 profit gives a maximum of $7 if you keep 50% of profit (the source says $7; 50% of $15 is $7.50), or up to $14.95 with a much thinner margin. [L0643.043]
  • Lifetime value changes the ceiling. A $50 product with a historical customer lifetime value of $500 can afford $450 to acquire that customer. Without lifetime value data, the same business can only justify spending up to its first-order profit. [L0643.042]
  • Know two dollar figures immediately: the maximum you can afford to spend to acquire one customer, and the target cost per acquisition for your ideal profit position. Both depend on your specific business; there is no universal correct amount. [L0414.011] (older, 2021-22)
  • Use the maximum affordable acquisition cost and the target CPA to decide where to advertise, where not to, and where to spend more. Check these numbers across the full customer journey and against customer lifetime value. [L0414.012] (older, 2021-22)
  • Keep a financial system that shows at any moment whether actual CPA is at or below target, and why or why not. Hitting the target CPA is the marketing-side indicator of profitability. [L0414.013] (older, 2021-22)
  • To find the maximum you can spend per sale in the Profitable Traffic Calculator, enter 0 as target profit per sale. Enter the profit you want to keep as a currency amount (e.g. $300), not a percentage. The break-even results then show the highest CPA, ROAS and max CPC you can accept. [L0686.018] (older, 2021-22)
  • Ad cost per sale should be compared against average order value. A 10x ROAS target allows ad cost of one tenth of the order value. [L0472.062] (2025)

The 10% rule

  • Set ad spend at up to 10% of product price per sale. A $200 product supports up to $20 of ad spend per customer; a $1,000 product supports up to $100. [L0358.027] (older, 2020)

  • Keep customer acquisition cost (CAC) at a maximum of 10% of product price. For a $1,000 product, up to $100 of ad spend per sale. [L0759.018]

  • The 10% CAC limit is a rough average rule. Real acquisition costs fluctuate between campaigns, so judge it over many sales rather than one. [L0759.019]

  • Maximum customer acquisition cost is about 10% of the sale price, which is the 10x ROAS target. On a $1,000 product, spend up to $100 per customer. [L0855.032] (2023)

  • Source A 2022 budget per sale:

    1. Set the minimum advertised price (MAP) at least 2X wholesale cost. Example: $500 wholesale means a $1,000 MAP.
    2. Aim for a target ROAS of 10X, so every $1 on ads returns at least $10 in sales. Example: a $1,000 product allows up to $100 ad spend per sale.
    3. Typical cost structure per sale: COGS about 50% of selling price, a 2.9% transaction fee, shipping covered, leaving about 10% of the price for traffic. The source does not itemise the remainder, so check it against your own costs.

    [L0429.044] (older, 2022)

  • Use a minimum ROAS of 10x when budgeting ads for a product, leaving margin for shipping and the merchant fee. [L0834.098] (older, 2021)

  • Low ROAS on lower-priced products has three possible causes: ads not targeted tightly enough, keywords too broad, and low website conversion rate. Review all three before deciding a niche is wrong. Focus first on costs you control, such as shipping rates and ad spend. [L0358.029] (older, 2020)

Revenue per click and maximum CPC

Core formulas

  • Revenue per click (RPC) = total conversion value (revenue) ÷ total clicks. It is the average revenue each click generates and the basis for all bid calculations. [L0299.003, L0454.003]
  • Predicted revenue per click comes from historical performance data for each product. Combined with target ROAS, it determines the maximum CPC you can profitably bid. [L0299.002]
  • Target CPC (maximum bid) = Revenue per click ÷ Target ROAS. Divide revenue per click by target ROAS to get the highest CPC that still keeps you profitable. [L0299.011, L0510.053]
  • Example: 100 clicks generating $1,000 revenue gives $10 RPC. At a 10x target ROAS the target CPC is $1. [L0454.003]
  • Maximum CPC for a target ROAS = RPC ÷ target ROAS. Example: a $1,000 budget earning $2,000 has ROAS 2. With RPC $2, the maximum CPC for ROAS 2 is $1. [L0510.053]
  • Source B: Maximum CPC = target CPA × conversion rate. At a $500 CPA and 1% conversion, break-even max CPC is $5. At a $200 target CPA and 1% conversion, max CPC is $2. [L0446.007] (older, 2021-22)
  • Source B: a higher bid is only profitable if conversion rate rises in step. Paying above target max CPC pushes profit below your target per sale, and a falling conversion rate makes existing bids unprofitable. [L0686.011] (older, 2021-22)
  • Bid, conversion rate and ROAS are linked: a high bid stops being profitable when conversion falls too low, so check every bid level against the conversion rate it actually produces. [L0740.013] (older, 2021-22)

Worked case: competitor and optimised advertiser (Source A)

  • Competitor baseline: $1,000 budget at $1 CPC gives 1,000 clicks. At a 1% conversion rate that is 10 conversions. At $200 customer value, that is $2,000 revenue, ROAS 2 and RPC $2. The highest CPC that still holds ROAS 2 is $1. [L0510.054]
  • Optimised advertiser with the same $1,000 budget: shopping title and quality score lowered the rate to $0.80 CPC, giving 1,250 clicks. Facebook retargeting raised conversion to 1.26%, giving about 16 conversions. The email lift raised customer value to $220, so revenue is about $3,520, and the maximum CPC for ROAS 2 becomes $1.41. [L0510.055]
  • The source quotes $3,500 ÷ $1,000 as 4. The correct result is 3.5. Use exact figures and recalculate with your own revenue before setting a bid target. [L0510.056]
  • The competitor and optimised advertiser both had a $1,000 budget. The optimised one reached about $3,500 revenue (a stated 76% increase from better titles, dynamic retargeting and email opt-ins, which is 75% on the given figures), with a much higher ROAS. The gap made it hard for the competitor to match the bid. [L0510.057]

Profitable traffic calculators

Source B Profitable Traffic Calculator (older)

It calculates break-even and target CPA, ROAS and max CPC from price, cost, target profit and conversion rate. It works for Google Ads, Facebook or any cost-per-click channel.   • Formulas used by the calculator:

Net profit before traffic = product price - unit cost
Break-even CPA = net profit before traffic
Break-even ROAS = product price / break-even CPA

  • Worked example: product price $2,000, unit cost $1,500, net profit before traffic $500. Target profit $300, conversion rate 1%. Results: break-even CPA $500, break-even ROAS 4x, break-even max CPC $5, target CPA $200, target ROAS 10x, target max CPC $2. [L0686.006] (older, 2021-22)

  • At a conversion rate of 0.5%, target CPA stays $200 but target max CPC falls to $1. The lower rate halves the affordable bid. [L0686.007] (older, 2021-22)

  • If a single product's conversion rate is unknown, use the store-wide rate. The result is much closer to reality than running no calculation. [L0686.016] (older, 2021-22)

  • Setting targets for a large catalogue:

    1. List the prices of all products.
    2. Group them into price buckets, e.g. around $2,000, $1,700, $1,500 and $1,300.
    3. Run the calculator once per bucket price.
    4. Use the stepped results as average guidance for max CPC and related targets, and avoid limits so tight they block profitable sales.

    [L0686.017] (older, 2021-22)

  • Before any bid change, check target max CPC against current bids and conversion rate (see L0686.010 steps 6-7). [L0686.010]

KPI baseline sheets

Baseline procedure per product

  • Before running paid ads, know three numbers for each product: COGS, break-even ROAS and break-even cost per purchase. [L0445.001]

  • Procedure to calculate the KPI baseline for a product:

    1. Add COGS (product price plus shipping).
    2. Add average order value.
    3. Read the break-even KPIs (minimum ROAS and maximum CPA that avoid a loss).
    4. Set a profit target (e.g. 10% or 15%) and read the profit-target KPIs.
    5. Compare live campaign ROAS and CPA against these numbers before deciding to scale, hold or kill the product.

    [L0445.012]

  • Build the KPI sheet in this order:

    1. Enter average order value, average fee % and average COGS including shipping per order.
    2. Enter the daily cost of running the business (the sheet's value is total monthly cost ÷ monthly orders: 400 ÷ 420).
    3. Enter the profit target %.
    4. Calculate break-even CPP, then the profit-target CPP by subtracting profit target % × AOV.
    5. Enter add-to-cart %, reached-checkout % and purchased %.
    6. Work backwards from CPP to CPIC, then CPATC, then CPVC using the funnel rates.
    7. Calculate break-even ROAS and profit-target ROAS.
    8. If the ad account is not in USD, enter how many units of your currency equal 1 USD.
    9. Compare the goal CPC with actual CPC and funnel rates.

    [L0900.011]

  • Breakeven KPIs cover the costs of the sale and leave no profit. Profit-target KPIs are the stricter limits to aim for. Each funnel-stage cost is derived from the next stage's cost times the conversion rate between them. Higher conversion at any stage allows a higher cost at the stage above. [L0900.012]

Funnel formulas (KPI sheet)

  • Profit Target CPP = Breakeven CPP − (profit target % × AOV). Example: 36.65 − (0.15 × 60) = 36.65 − 9.00 = 27.65. [L0900.002]
  • Cost Per Initiate Checkout (CPIC) = CPP × (purchased % ÷ reached checkout %). Example: 36.65 × (2 ÷ 4) = 18.32 at breakeven; 27.65 × (2 ÷ 4) = 13.82 at the profit target. [L0900.003]
  • Cost Per Add to Cart (CPATC) = CPIC × (reached checkout % ÷ add to cart %). Example: 18.32 × (4 ÷ 6) = 12.22 at breakeven; 13.82 × (4 ÷ 6) = 9.22 at the profit target. [L0900.004]
  • Cost Per View Content (CPVC) = CPATC × add to cart %. Example: 12.22 × 0.06 = 0.73 at breakeven; 9.22 × 0.06 = 0.55 at the profit target. [L0900.005]

Reading results and acting on them

  • Compare actual ROAS with break-even and target ROAS. Above target: performing well. Between target and break-even: profitable but short of goals. Below break-even: losing money. [L0327.022]
  • Calculate break-even ROAS before launching any ads, because it is the reference number for profit or loss. Compare every campaign, keyword and product against it. [L0327.023]
  • Break-even ROAS depends on each product's COGS and fees, so calculate it per product or per product category rather than one number for the whole store. This can be a single spreadsheet covering all products or products grouped by category. [L0327.024]
  • Campaigns below break-even lose money on each order; the funnel must be fixed or the product removed. Campaigns above break-even but below the profit target need optimisation before scaling. [L0875.013]
  • Judge an ad set against break-even cost per purchase. Use ad-level data later for more precise decisions. [L0640.014]
  • Use the break-even cost per purchase to set kill rules for campaigns. [L0693.050]
  • Know four numbers before reviewing ad performance: target cost per sale, target ROAS, break-even ROAS and break-even cost per sale. Work them out at least roughly; without a target, report data means nothing. [L0719.017] (older, 2021-22)
  • Track cost per product and cost per creative alongside ad spend to see which products and creative types cost the most to support. [L0885.021]
  • True ROAS = attributable revenue ÷ (Meta ad spend + cost to produce the creatives). Including creative cost gives a more accurate return than ad spend alone. [L0885.020]
  • If ad ROAS is at or above target but profit is still low, the problem lies in unit economics, not advertising. [L0433.011]
  • A ROAS of 2.6-3 on the same margins was reported to give about 35% profit, against about 15-20% at a ROAS of 2-2.5 with break-even of 1.3-1.5. [L0645.021]
  • Example: a ROAS of 3.5-4.5 on CAD 1,000 daily spend and CAD 3,500 sales gives a stated net daily profit of CAD 1,100-1,200. [L0298.003]
  • Winter ad spend of about $5,000 produced about $18,000 in sales in November. The brand roughly broke even overall, which was accepted because the strategy was long term. [L0492.052]
  • Source C example: $1 spent that returns $1.60 gives a ROAS of 1.6. [L0445.003]
  • Ad cost per conversion should always be read against the margin per sale before deciding whether a product sells profitably with ads. [L0706.008]

Core metrics: ROAS, CPA, CPC and the KPI

  • A KPI is the goal you set before advertising, such as cost per sale or the return you need, so every campaign has a clear number to reach. Without a defined target you cannot judge whether a campaign is working [L1021.001].
  • ROAS is the most important KPI in the reporting view [L1024.042].
  • ROAS = revenue generated by the ads ÷ ad spend over the same period [L1020.010, L1049.017, L1110.047, L1111.148, L1038.009, L1135.052, L1215.037, L1217.052, L1508.008, L1002.074, L0969.048, L0970.147, L0972.054, L1203.002].

  • CPA = amount spent on the campaign ÷ number of purchases. Example: $104.49 spent with one purchase gives a CPA of $104.49 [L1124.009].
  • Ad spend = cost per click × number of clicks. CPA = ad spend ÷ number of sales. Example: $3 × 100 clicks = $300 spend. At 2% conversion (2 sales), CPA is $150. At 3% (3 sales), CPA drops to $100 [L0923.003].
  • Cost per sale = cost per click ÷ conversion rate. At $1 CPC, 100 visitors at 5% gives 5 sales for $100 total, or $20 per sale. 500 visitors at 1% gives 5 sales for $500, or $100 per sale. The lower-CTR, higher-conversion version is far cheaper per sale [L1401.017].
  • CPA = cost per click ÷ conversion rate. At 0.5% conversion, a $0.25 click gives a CPA of $50, while a $1.50 click gives $300. The same term is profitable at one bid and not at the other [L1328.036].
  • Paid traffic math: ad spend = clicks × CPC; conversions = clicks × conversion rate; revenue = conversions × average value per conversion; ROAS = revenue ÷ ad spend. Example: 500 clicks × $1.20 = $600 spend; 500 × 2% = 10 conversions; 10 × $300 = $3,000 revenue; ROAS = 5 [L1455.002].
  • Paid traffic is only worth scaling when ad revenue exceeds ad spend, so the target is a positive return on ad spend. A campaign that earns more in sales than it costs can justify a higher budget [L1455.003].
  • Stores with a profitable paid setup often run budgets without a fixed cap, as long as the return stays positive. The key control is knowing conversion rate and value per conversion, so the spend limit follows from the numbers [L1455.004].
  • Before launching a campaign, know your average cost per click, your site conversion rate and the value of one sale. These three numbers decide whether the campaign can be profitable, and without them you cannot calculate break-even or the ROAS you need [L1455.013].
  • Your cost per click or acquisition must fit the conversion rate your site achieves on that traffic. If you pay more for traffic than your conversion rate supports, sales will not be profitable. Set financial targets for paid-traffic sales before scaling spend [L1507.006] (older, 2021-22).
  • Filtering out buyers unlikely to convert, through ad text and pre-click qualifiers, improves cost-to-revenue more than a higher click-through rate does. Judge ad changes by cost per sale or revenue per dollar spent, not by CTR alone [L1401.018].

Break-even ROAS: definition and rules

  • Break-even ROAS is the minimum return on ad spend an offer needs to avoid a loss. Any ROAS above it makes money and any ROAS below it loses money [L0986.014].
  • Break-even ROAS is the point where a campaign is neither profitable nor loss-making. A ROAS above it is profitable; below it means a loss [L1217.053].
  • The break-even ROAS is the point where revenue equals COGS plus ad spend. Profit turns from negative to positive above it [L1203.003].
  • Break-even ROAS is the return at which a campaign covers its costs. The mentor gave no fixed target above it, but described a ROAS of about 1.7 as acceptable and about 3 as great [L1107.007].
  • Before running ads for an offer, compare its break-even ROAS with the ROAS you expect or measure. Keep ads running only while measured ROAS stays above break-even. Do this for every offer, including bundles and buy-X-get-Y offers [L0986.015].
  • Calculate the minimum ROAS for each campaign, keyword and other segment before launch, and let that minimum guide every bidding and budget decision [L1203.001].
  • Compare ROAS with break-even: above it the campaign is profitable, below it the campaign is at a loss [L1024.042].
  • Knowing the break-even ROAS for every candidate product before testing is essential, because it sets the minimum return a product's ads must generate [L1162.007].
  • Break-even ROAS depends on margin, so always compare ROAS with break-even before deciding whether to keep or cut an ad set [L0950.024].
  • Break-even ROAS changes with the product's margin and costs. Calculate your own floor for each product rather than using another store's figure [L0977.027, L1203.004].

Break-even CPA and cost per purchase

  • Break-even CPA = selling price − product cost − other per-order costs. Example: $10 product cost and a selling price of at least $30 gives a margin of about $20, so break-even CPA was about $20 to $21 [L1124.010].
  • Break-even CPA = selling price − (product cost + shipping cost). Example: $30 price with $10 cost gives $20. $40 price with $10 product cost gives $30 [L1436.059].
  • Break-even cost per purchase (the most you can spend to acquire one customer) = selling price − product cost − shipping cost. Example: $30 price, $10 product plus shipping gives $20. A $40 price with a $10 product cost gave $30 before shipping [L1382.039].
  • Target cost per purchase = the maximum you can spend on ads to acquire one sale and still profit. Example: $150 price, $35 COGS gives $115 (price − COGS − other per-unit costs as the sheet defines them). Any cost per purchase above it is unprofitable [L1354.006].
  • Cost per purchase = listing price − COGS. Set the cost-per-purchase and ROAS targets inside the ad account from your actual margin [L1225.065].
  • Target CPA in the Ecom Margin Calculator = dollar margin per order (listing price − total cost). Cost per purchase in the ad account must stay below it to be profitable. Example: $200 listing, $60 total cost gives a target CPA of $140 [L0987.021].
  • Profitable target CPA: $70 or less when the listing price is $100 and total cost is $30 [L0987.011].
  • Break-even CPA check for a product: compare CPA with profit margin per sale. With a $30 margin, target a CPA of roughly $16 to $20 at most. For VAT-adjusted markets, include the extra VAT (for example 20%) in the break-even calculation before running ads to that market [L0950.023].
  • Calculate break-even CPA before judging whether an ad set is working [L0950.022].
  • Plan customer acquisition costs of roughly $15 to $20 per customer on paid platforms, with some estimates as low as $10. Product margin must be high enough to cover these costs [L1174.012].

  • Target ROAS at a given margin is calculated the same way as break-even, with profit set to that margin [L1182.015].
  • Target ROAS for profitability in the ad account is 1.43 or higher when the product has a 70% gross margin [L0987.010].

Margin left for ads and ad budget per product

  • Margin left for ads = MAP − wholesale cost − shipping cost − 2.9% merchant fee − other expenses. Wholesale cost can be estimated as MAP ÷ 2 for a ballpark figure [L0961.037] (older, 2020).
  • Example: MAP 1,000 USD, estimated wholesale 500 USD, shipping 100 USD. About 400 USD remains for ads, the 2.9% merchant fee and other expenses [L0961.038] (older, 2020).
  • Set the ad budget per product from the margin left after wholesale and shipping are subtracted from MAP, not from revenue. Calculate that amount before choosing a daily or per-order ad spend [L0934.119].
  • Derive a target CPA from margins:
    1. Write down the selling price of the product.
    2. Subtract supplier cost and other costs per sale.
    3. Decide your profit target. The remaining amount is what you can pay Google per sale, which is your target CPA.
    4. Repeat for each product, since margins differ [L1328.014].
  • Use break-even math for each product. A higher margin lets you outbid competitors on paid clicks [L1153.074].
  • On Amazon, a $1.50 bid per click is affordable when the product earns about $9 profit before ads and the competitor's conversion is lower [L1153.073].

Revenue per click and max CPC

  • Profitability per click: revenue per click must exceed cost per click. The more revenue per click relative to cost, the better. Example: $6 revenue per click at $1 CPC is a 6x ratio, the target to aim for [L1328.015].
  • Reducing cost per click by 30% has a very large effect on return on ad spend. Lower click costs are one of the strongest levers for profitable paid traffic [L1226.012].
  • Derive the maximum CPC from the target CPA and the conversion rate (see [L1328.036] for the CPA = CPC ÷ conversion rate relation and [L1328.014] for the target CPA procedure).

Calculators, models and the ROAS chart

Procedure: build a ROAS chart in Excel [L1203.005]:

  1. Enter how much was spent on paid traffic in a period (example: $1,000).
  2. Enter the revenue that spend generated (example: $10,000, a 10x multiple).
  3. Plot in the COGS for that revenue, i.e. what it costs to sell $10,000 worth of goods.
  4. Calculate the final profit after COGS and ad spend.
  5. Plug in other scenarios and find the switch point where ROAS moves from negative to positive profit.
  6. Use the resulting minimum ROAS to judge every campaign.
  • In the example cost structure, break-even ROAS was 2x. Above 2x the campaign makes money; below 2x it loses money. Calculate break-even from your own store's COGS, because COGS differs per store [L1203.004].

Measuring what pays: cost per reply, cost per customer, cost per dollar of sale

  • Cost per reply = ad spend ÷ replies. Cost per customer = ad spend ÷ customers acquired. Cost per dollar of sale = ad spend ÷ sales revenue, the most useful measure for deciding whether an ad pays [L1261.012].
  • Example: two ads for a $5 product cost $0.85 and $14.20 per reply. At 250,000 replies a year, the first costs about $212,500 and the second $3,550,000. Cutting cost per reply directly protects profit [L1261.013].
  • An ad producing replies at an average of $0.41 for two years is the benchmark that new ads must beat [L1261.014].
  • Final conclusions about an ad should rest on cost per customer or cost per dollar of sale, not on the number of responses. An ad can bring many worthless replies, while another brings fewer valuable ones [L1261.015].
  • Use sample requests and coupon counts to measure interest, but judge each lead's final value by cost per customer. Cheap inquiries that never convert are not savings [L1261.022].
  • Direct mail: calculate the response rate needed to break even before the mailing goes out. A mailing needing about 0.5% response failed entirely at one-tenth of that rate, so the number must be known in advance [L1366.036, L1366.097] (merged: true duplicates).
  • Cost per order = cost per mailed piece ÷ response rate. Example: a $0.04 piece with a 4.5% response gives about $0.89 per order [L1369.086].

  • Set KPI benchmarks from your own history, such as last year or the previous month. Check whether current add-to-cart and other numbers are above or below that benchmark, to judge whether a campaign is improving or declining [L1508.013].
  • Stable ROAS of 20x is a good target when the store is profitable. Big budget changes can destroy profitability [L1283.098] (2023).
  • Before a mailing, calculate the break-even response rate and check whether a test can measure it [L1366.036].

Break-even sales and targets for a first campaign

  • Source A's break-even example: $100 revenue ÷ $25 net profit per order = 4. Every $1 of ad spend needs $4 in sales to break even, so $25 of spend needs $100 in sales [L0920.006, L1357.005] (merged: true duplicates).
  • Give this break-even ROAS to your paid advertising agency as the target. Any campaign running below it is losing money at that margin [L0920.007].
  • The same break-even comparison applies to every campaign and offer. Compare ROAS with break-even before deciding to keep or cut [L1024.042].

Further details

  • To calculate break-even ROAS: 1. Determine revenue target (e.g., $1,000). 2. Calculate COGS in dollars (e.g., $400) and percentage (e.g., 40%). 3. Calculate profit before ads = Revenue - COGS (e.g., $1,000 - $400 = $600) and as percentage (e.g., 60%). 4. Calculate break-even ROAS = Revenue ÷ Profit (e.g., $1,000 ÷ $600 = 1.67x). 5. ROAS above this number is profitable; below is a loss. [L0327.017]

  • Calculating and using the target cost per sale: 1) Calculate the target cost per sale using the traffic calculator. 2) Write down the resulting numbers. 3) Compare these targets with the actual figures in the financial tracking system. 4) Check profit on a day-to-day basis against the targets.

19.11 Test budgets, kill rules and scale decisions

Starting budgets and first spend

  • Older advice (2019): start with $10 to $30 USD per day across all campaigns. The goal is to reach return on ad spend (ROAS) within two to three days. [L0243.032]
  • Older advice (2021): a starting daily ad budget of about $10 to $30 per day is typical for new stores. Promotional ad-credit vouchers can cover roughly one week of advertising at no cost. [L0247.118]
  • Older advice (2019): a few hundred dollars is enough as a starting ad budget for the first months, provided ad spend is monitored closely and optimized. Profits should then be reinvested into ads. [L1068.070]
  • Older advice (2019): leaving campaigns running without checking them can drain money quickly. For example, a $100 per day budget left unmonitored for a month would leak a large amount of money. [L1068.071]
  • For a new store, $1,000 in ad spend covers about 30 days of paid traffic. Free traffic methods can reduce how much must be invested in ads each month. [L0360.077]
  • Older advice (2021 to 2022): a daily budget of about $30 equals roughly $1,000 per month and is a reasonable entry level. Other examples mentioned are $50, $100 and $1,000 per day, depending on what the advertiser can comfortably afford. [L0749.007]
  • Older advice (2021 to 2022): only put a daily ad budget into a shared budget that you could lose completely in a single day without financial stress. If you cannot afford to lose a given amount daily, do not set it as the budget. [L0749.004]
  • Older advice (2021 to 2022): a daily budget set very low, for example $2 per day, produces too little data to learn anything. Sales may come only once every three or four months. The budget must be realistic enough to generate meaningful results. [L0749.006]
  • Older advice (2021 to 2022): ad spend aims at long-term profitability, not profit every day. Days with zero return or a full loss on ad spend will happen, so judge performance over time rather than daily. [L0749.005]
  • Older advice (2021 to 2022): beginners without advertising experience often spend money on ads that bring no return during trial and error. Plan a test budget for this learning period and track returns from the start so losses are limited. [L1014.013]
  • Treat money spent on ads and tests as a purchase of data. Every dollar spent should produce a lesson that brings the business closer to profitability. [L0631.011]

Lead generation and warm audience budgets

  • Daily lead budget = target leads per day × maximum cost per lead. Example: 10 leads per day at a $4 maximum gives about $40 per day. [L0649.042]
  • Start each lead generation campaign at $5 to $10 per day. The customer goodwill campaign can run on $5 per day because it only needs to show ads to a few people. [L0649.043]
  • As a starting point, the three warm audiences together need about $15 to $20 per day. The promotions budget depends on the size of the audience. [L0649.044]
  • When entering a promotion budget in Meta, the screen shows the audience size and the estimated daily reach. Try different budget amounts to see how reach changes before launching. [L0649.045]
  • Budget needs grow with traffic. Retargeting a large audience, such as 100,000 site visitors, costs far more to reach than a 1,000-visitor audience, so size budgets to the audience. [L0649.046]
  • Ad fatigue in cold lead generation took roughly half a year to over a year to appear. Earlier, ads had to be swapped out about every week. [L0649.048]

Product test procedure (budget and kill rule)

  1. Spend about $25 to $30 per day across 5 to 6 ad sets at $5 each. [L1171.022]
  2. Let the ads run for the first three days without interfering. [L1171.022]
  3. Gather data until about $100 is spent and check that CPM is below $25, CTR is above 1.5% (ideally 2%), and CPC is below $1. [L1171.022, L1171.020]
  4. If these numbers are met, check the store's conversion rate, which should be at least 1.5%. [L1171.022, L1171.021]
  5. If conversion is too low, improve the offer and store for two to three days. [L1171.022]
  6. If there are still no sales after $300 spent, switch the ads off and move to the next product. [L1171.022]

Price-based spend threshold (product page test)

  • Use this procedure when a product reaches its first spend threshold, about 10% of price, with no sale. [L0965.043]
    1. Do not switch the ads off yet. [L0965.043]
    2. Check the search terms for irrelevant traffic and add those keywords as negative keywords. [L0965.043]
    3. Open the product pages of your future competitors for the exact same product and compare them with yours. [L0965.043]
    4. Change your page to match or beat what the competitors do better, which is where the conversion rate can improve. [L0965.043]
    5. Keep spending while you optimize, and stop only at about three times the first threshold if there is still no sale. [L0965.043]
  • Example: for a $1,500 product, the first spend threshold is about $150, meaning 10% of price. If $150 is spent without a sale, keep the ads running and start optimizing. Stop at about $450 (three times the threshold, roughly 30% of price) if there is still no sale. [L0965.044]

Ad and ad set budgets by platform

Meta (Facebook)

  • Test ad sets with a daily budget of $5 each. The presenter starts 4 to 6 ad sets, so the test spends about $20 to $30 per day in total. [L0642.034]
  • Check ad performance after about four days of spend (about $20 per ad set), starting with CTR, then CPM, then sales. [L0642.033]
  • Ad testing budget: start with about 5 USD per ad set (or 5.10 USD). Run five ad sets at 5 USD per day for about four days, then analyze. Alternatively, 20 USD per ad set for two days. [L0773.033]
  • Ad budgets for a single ad start at $10 to $50 per day. The ad is judged when it has spent three times the desired cost per acquisition (CPA). Example: for a product bought at $15 and selling at $45 with a target CPA of $15, the ad runs with $45 per day until it is switched off or scaled. [L0995.017]
  • Budget rule for a single Facebook ad: daily budget = 3 × target CPA. Example: CPA $15 gives a daily budget of $45 per ad. Target ROAS example: product cost $15, selling price $45, CPA $15 = 3:1 target ROAS. [L0995.018]
  • Limit a Meta daily budget correctly. The daily budget shows three limits: average daily spend equals the daily budget, maximum daily spend equals daily budget × 1.75, and maximum weekly spend equals daily budget × 7. Example with a $25 daily budget: average $25.00 per day, maximum daily $43.75, maximum weekly $175.00. [L0348.032]
  • Set the spend limit for an ad set at the product's cost of goods, so the first test measures whether the product covers its cost. Return on ad spend (ROAS) is the revenue from the ad divided by the amount spent on it. [L0562.015]

TikTok

  • Starting daily ad spend for a new product test: break-even cost multiplied by 10, divided by 4, which equals the daily ad spend for one ad set with no targeting. [L1177.046]

Pinterest

  • Pinterest test budget: start with a daily budget of exactly $5 per campaign, with $10 as the maximum to start with. A single product can be tested for under $5 per day, and multiple products can run in parallel at $5 each. Test three to five products if the budget allows. [L1134.009]

Google and YouTube

  • A YouTube ads test needs a budget of at least $1,000 to $2,000. Expect to wait two to three weeks before judging results, and a single month of testing often only improves creatives. [L0788.038]

  • Do not judge a campaign after one day of data. Allow about 3 to 5 days of data before deciding. [L0639.006]
  • Judging a new campaign after less than a day or after a small spend, such as $35, is premature. Wait for several days of data before deciding whether it works. [L1107.036]
  • For a $24.99 product, four ad sets with about $3.50 spent each and a single sale is an acceptable early start. Judging the campaign after less than one day is premature. Compare results against break-even ROAS and wait for several days of data before changing the setup. [L1107.039]
  • Do not judge a product on the first day. During learning, CPMs tend to be high and CTR low, so the data is not reliable yet. [L1135.033]
  • Avoid abrupt launches and large budget changes. Performance fluctuates on the first day and usually stabilises around day three or four as the account learns the audience. [L1135.032]
  • Let each campaign run for at least five to seven days, ideally seven, and review it once it has reached around 100 visitors. [L1135.049]
  • Waiting for a longer run only makes sense when the early numbers show a plausible path to the benchmarks. [L0361.006]
  • Do not close a store just because ads have run for a few weeks without sales. Compare the clicks from each Google Ads campaign against the sales and decide on optimizations based on traffic volume. A store with very little traffic cannot yet be judged on its conversion rate. [L0837.006]
  • Judge a new Meta campaign after roughly three days of ad spend. [L0874.003]
  • A new campaign with little traffic may need several days before its results stabilize. Judge performance after two to three days and check whether the metrics have settled before changing anything. [L1177.049]
  • Typical Google Ads ramp (especially with low starting budgets): a first sale within a few weeks, about one sale a day by month two, and two to three sales a day by month three. (2023) [L1094.107]

Test cost before the first-sale gate

  • Test cost before the first-sale gate equals the daily budget times the number of test days. At $5 per day for six days, the spend is $30, so a product without a sale by then is dropped. [L1135.053]

Kill rules by platform

Meta (Facebook)

  • Use CTR as the first gate: a click-through rate of about 1.5% or higher suggests real interest in a product, and around 2% is a stronger sign. Give a product a longer test when the rate sits around 1.5%, and stop it when the rate is well below that. [L0661.040]
  • Primary metric for a new store is CTR: aim for at least 1.5%. The presenter sees 2 to 5% as a sign that the ads are working. [L0642.032]
  • A CTR above 1.5% is considered good for Facebook ads. Creatives at around 1.3 to 1.5% CTR can still be scaled if the ROAS is 2 or higher. [L0645.004]
  • Day-one ad-level optimization:
    1. After day one, review only the ads inside each ad set, not the ad sets. [L0648.032]
    2. Find ads that have spent at least 8 USD (normally 8 to 10 USD). [L0648.032]
    3. Mark an ad as poor if its CPC is above 3 USD or one USD above the average CPC of the account. [L0648.032]
    4. Turn off ads that have spent that amount with a poor CPC and no sales, so Meta moves budget to the other ads. [L0648.032]
    5. Keep ads with a good CPC running even if their CTR is lower. [L0648.032]
  • CPC and profitable spend matter more than CTR. A low CTR is acceptable when CPC is cheap, while a high CPC means the budget goes to the wrong ad, and CTR and creatives should be improved. [L0648.033]
  • Do not kill an ad set before it has spent its break-even cost per purchase. At a 5 USD daily budget and a 20 USD break-even, wait 4 days (20 ÷ 5) before judging it. [L0648.034]
  • Use the break-even cost per purchase as the maximum spend an ad set should reach without a sale before it counts as unprofitable. Without a sale after spending the break-even amount, the ad set is unlikely to become profitable in the long run. [L0648.031]
  • Decision after an ad set reaches its break-even cost per purchase:
    1. If there was no sale and the CPC and CTR are poor, turn the ad set off. [L0648.035]
    2. If there was no sale but the CTR is above average and the CPC is low, give it one more day, then turn it off if it still has no sale. [L0648.035]
    3. If there is at least one sale, let it spend another break-even cost per purchase to check whether the sales are consistent. [L0648.035]
    4. Two sales by day two can mark a potential winner. [L0648.035]
    5. Repeat the same check after each break-even amount until the audience reaches four or more sales with a profitable ROAS, then it is ready to scale. [L0648.035]
  • Meta campaign spending around 150 EUR over three days across several ad sets with only one add-to-cart: stop the campaign and investigate rather than leaving it running. Such a result points to a broken setup, tracking or a fundamentally weak offer. [L0361.001]
  • The purpose of stopping a losing test early is to cut losses before money is spent on a product that does not work. [L0361.006]
  • A product with 350 USD spent, a ROAS of 0.42, a link CTR under 1% and a conversion rate under 1% was judged as not worth more spend. Investing the energy in the next product was the recommended choice. [L0363.041]
  • Do not judge a test ad by CTR alone, because a high CTR can still be unprofitable. Compare the spend per ad set with the break-even cost per purchase and the resulting sales and ROAS. [L0692.018]
  • Facebook ads should be judged after the test has spent enough to give data. Without enough spend the data is not reliable, so one can only rely on signals such as add-to-carts for early judgment. [L0995.048]
  • Cut losing ads early using soft metrics: cost per ATC, CTR, CPC, CPM and engagement rate ranking. Keep an ad only if these metrics improve day over day, otherwise cut it. [L1019.042]

TikTok

  • Product kill thresholds for a TikTok test:
    • If no video passes 10,000 views in the first week after consistent uploading, the product is probably dead. [L0501.022]
    • If you cannot reach around 500 followers or a few thousand views in the first week, kill it. [L0501.022]
    • If you cannot reach 1,000 followers within two weeks, or if revenue per million views is under 100 USD, drop the product. [L0501.022]
    • Kill early to save time. [L0501.022]
  • Revenue per million views after the second week:
    • Keep products that reach at least about 1,000 USD per million views on average. [L0501.025]
    • At 500 USD per million, it is still worth it. [L0501.025]
    • A product with 10 million views and 5,000 USD in sales (50% profit) is still considered good. [L0501.025]
  • Strong product test performance (example): CPM $4 to $6 on Facebook ($10 in Germany), under $5 on TikTok; CTR 1 to 2% on Facebook, under 1% on TikTok; add-to-cart ratio 13 to 18%; 4 sales total (1 from Facebook under $100 spend, 3 from TikTok $120 spend). These top-tier metrics indicate creative resonance and audience fit before scaling. [L0307.022]

Google and YouTube

  • If a Google Ads campaign spends budget early but produces no sales, delete it and create a fresh campaign rather than making multiple bid or audience adjustments. The algorithm struggles to recover from poor learning phase performance, so restarting from scratch allows more effective optimization. [L0210.040]
  • Pause a product or ad group when it has accumulated clicks equal to about twice the expected clicks per conversion without a conversion, provided there is enough data (roughly 10 to 15 conversions per month in the campaign). Example: with 38 expected clicks per conversion, pause at about 70 to 76 clicks without a conversion. [L0745.015]
  • Judging a campaign after only three sales over three months and concluding the ads are not working is a mistake. Three sales with a cost per sale that is too high relative to product prices is still usable data, and it points to product selection rather than a lack of data. [L0748.015]
  • Older advice (2020), procedure when ads are unprofitable:
    1. If ads are unprofitable (ROAS is too low), first try cutting negative keywords and removing underperforming products. [L0245.057]
    2. Monitor for two weeks. [L0245.057]
    3. If ROAS still does not improve, consider pausing ads or significantly cutting budget. [L0245.057]
    4. Do not pause ads if ROAS is stable just because traffic is low. [L0245.057]

Checks before pausing or cutting

  • Before pausing a product, verify its performance across all channels and touchpoints. Check Google Analytics data, Shopify transaction data, and email conversion sequences to confirm the product is truly underperforming and not driving conversions through other customer journeys. [L0299.018]
  • Do not pause a low-performing product variant without checking similar variants of the same product. A weak-converting color or size might be pulling down overall metrics while other variants of the same SKU are strong sellers. [L0299.019]
  • Test and analyze one change at a time when performance drops, because changing creatives, pages and audiences together makes the cause hard to find. [L0950.074]
  • A creative that worked in the first days but dropped suddenly after a new asset was added suggests a test of the newest creative by cutting it for a few days to see whether the old ads recover. [L0950.073]
  • Optimize in this order: first check the creatives, then the account structure, then the store. Stop the product only after these steps have been tested within the test budget. [L0363.043]
  • Before the store is blamed, check the funnel. If the product does not reach its breakeven KPIs, treat that as a signal to check the funnel and, if no fix works, consider killing the product. [L0445.018]
  • Evaluate ads at two-week intervals rather than switching them off early. A campaign should only be stopped after months of optimization fail, and in the meantime fix negative keywords, bids and budgets. (older advice, 2022) [L0429.046]

Product-level kill thresholds

  • Set a kill threshold for each product: stop spending when conversion stays below 1% after optimization, since a conversion rate under 1% is treated as a fatal sign. Test budgets should be limited to about 200 to 250 USD before the decision. [L0363.040]
  • Seriously consider dropping a product if its CTR falls below 1.5%, sales are very hard to get, and it is still not profitable after about $10k in sales. Before that threshold, improve the offer and the store first. [L1017.014]
  • A product with a barely profitable cost per acquisition should be tested with new interests and a changed offer before it is dropped. Keep testing until the margin is positive. [L0661.043]
  • Accept break-even in the early stage if the goal is proof of concept and brand positioning, not immediate profit. Judge the product over two to three months or a full year. [L0492.053]
  • Decide whether to continue a product by its numbers, not by attachment: continue if the numbers are good and move on if they are not. [L1176.022]
  • Falling in love with a product and spending too much on it causes losses. Ask what the data says instead of why the product is not working. [L1176.023]
  • A product that only just breaks even on ROAS and does not reach the break-even point will eventually die out. Do not scale it. Scale only after you find a clear winner. [L1176.024]
  • Ad-level judgment: a single product was tested with two Facebook interests. The interest around personal-care buyers reached about 40% attention rate but produced no add-to-carts and no sales. The home and garden interest had a poor attention rate but generated the sales, so the decision was based on sales rather than attention. [L0995.054]

Scale-or-hold decisions

Proof required before scaling

  • Before scaling an ad set, wait until it has about six or more sales. Several ad sets with six or more sales are a stronger signal than one ad set with a single high-performing result. [L0349.017]
  • Scale an interest only after it has produced about six or more profitable sales. Below that, the pixel does not yet have enough purchase data to optimize. [L0639.013]
  • A winning audience means two to three interests that each produce at least three purchases at a ROAS above break-even within about 3 to 5 days of data. Test ads with winning creatives together with winning interests, because a winning asset is the combination of an interest and a creative. [L0645.002]
  • Four or more sales on one audience with a profitable ROAS is the consistency signal to scale that audience. Three to four sales is an early sign that needs more spend to confirm. [L0648.036]
  • A product is ready to be scaled once a winning interest reaches more than six sales. Scaling should start only after the sales signal appears, not after a few clicks. [L1172.034]
  • A product has proof of concept when it earns about $100 profit per day consistently for three days, as shown on the profit and loss sheet. This consistency, not a single good day, is the signal to act on. [L1175.032]
  • Do not scale the ad budget while the metrics are inconsistent or fluctuate strongly. Scale only when the metrics hold steady, because a lack of stable results means no break and no scale. [L1175.033]
  • Judge ads by hard metrics first (sales, profit and add-to-carts). Soft metrics matter only if an ad is not making money. [L0999.023]
  • Scale only the ads that are profitable. Do not overspend on unprofitable ads. [L1184.056]
  • Check margin and return on ad spend before any ad budget is increased. Spending on ads that do not produce profit drains budget quickly. [L1131.018]
  • Ad spend is a real cost, so before scaling any ads, check that the spend is justified by the numbers. Spending on ads without reading the metrics properly burns money without a clear return. [L1038.022]
  • For a clear winning product, track only ROAS and sales, and ignore CTR, CPC and CPM. Turn off or cut budget for any ad set that stops performing. [L1173.036]
  • Use daily optimization decisions on purchase-related metrics: cost per purchase, total spend, purchase value and ROAS. CTR, CPC, CPM and conversion rate alone should not drive major decisions. [L0290.008]
  • In the first testing days, do not judge the whole campaign by profitability, because product, interest, creative and website are all still variables. ROAS per ad set on daily data is the main decision metric during testing. [L0645.005]
  • A low CTR combined with a high ROAS shows there is room to improve the creative, but the ROAS still decides whether the ad set stays. Profit comes from ROAS, not from CTR alone. Example: CTR 0.92% with ROAS 3.21. [L0645.006]
  • Break-even cost per purchase is the reference for whether a campaign is working. A test campaign that spends about one to one-and-a-half times the break-even without a sale should be stopped. [L0349.028]

Budget ramps

Doubling and step ramps

  • 4-Hour Budget Doubling Method:
    1. Start one ad with £200 budget. [L0308.015]
    2. After 4 hours, calculate ROAS from sales in that 4-hour period only (ignore previous sales). [L0308.015]
    3. If ROAS is profitable (above break-even), double budget to £400. [L0308.015]
    4. After 4 more hours, calculate ROAS for the new 4-hour period. [L0308.015]
    5. If profitable, double again. [L0308.015]
    6. Repeat until budget reaches target or ROAS drops below threshold. [L0308.015]
    7. Example progression: £200 → £400 → £800 → £1,600 → £3,200 → £4,000. [L0308.015]
  • Black Friday 2023 scaling: started at £200 and scaled over the day to approximately £4,000. Final weekend (Friday to Sunday) ad spend was £13,491. Total weekly ad spend was £33,661. Revenue was £76,000. [L0308.018]
  • Pinterest scaling rule: once a campaign is profitable, double the daily budget (e.g. $5 to $10). Require consistent profit for two days, for example ROAS above 2.5 while breakeven ROAS is 1.5 to 1.6. Increase the budget at the start of the day, around 12:00 midnight UTC or up to 4 to 5 a.m. UTC, not in the evening, to avoid a sudden spend spike. [L1134.032]
  • Step-by-step ramp (Source A): a first-month test with a $1,000 ad budget produced $20,000 in sales, a 20× return. After optimizing, the budget was raised step by step. At the next step, sales doubled while the return stayed above 10×. A later large increase of tens of thousands of dollars dropped the return well below 10×, which shows why big jumps are risky. [L0359.097]
  • Avoid abrupt launches and large budget changes, because performance fluctuates on the first day and usually stabilises around day three or four as the account learns the audience. [L1135.032]
  • Ads with a budget of at least 20 USD per day in a dedicated structure: Meta ad sets were fragmenting after a sales milestone, a week of weak results followed, raising the daily budget from 5 to 7 for three to four days did not help, and combining ad sets at about 20 per day still gave a good CTR but no sales. Earlier the store had at least 200 sales a day. Afterward it dropped to zero or one sale a day, which led to a funnel and offer review. [L1017.041]
  • One student reached $1,300 in sales in a single day at a profit margin of about 40%. This shows that a product with healthy margins can produce strong daily sales, but scaling should begin only after the numbers are checked. [L1017.040]

Horizontal and advanced scaling (Pinterest)

  • Horizontal scaling on Pinterest: after a winning interest is found in the Targeting breakdown (Interest view), create new campaigns that target the winning sub-interests, or add 50 to 80 keywords with audience sizes of 10 to 30 million, then judge each new campaign by its profitability. [L1134.033]
  • A more advanced scaling option on Pinterest is CPA-based targeting (a cost-per-acquisition cap, e.g. sales under $20), used after about 10 to 15 days of consistent results, similar to the bid cap on Facebook. [L1134.034]

Bottom-of-funnel and campaign-level ramps

  • If you are not profitable at $50 per day on a bottom-of-funnel campaign, the ad concept or product validation is likely the problem, not the budget level. De-scaling further (to $30 or $20) does not solve this. Test new ad concepts, new products or stop and focus elsewhere. [L0316.035]
  • If you have a video campaign spending $300 per day with a given ROAS, run bottom-of-funnel at roughly $100 per day ($300 × 30%). Monitor ROAS and scale bottom-of-funnel only if it stays above your comfort threshold (e.g., 2.5× if breakeven is 1.5×). This prevents overspending on a limited warm pool. [L0316.043]
  • Keep ROAS stable as the primary success metric. If costs drop 20% but lead volume increases 20%, and ROAS stays the same, you are succeeding even if absolute profit has not changed. (older advice, 2020) [L0245.056]
  • Track the daily pixel purchase volume. That number determines when ABO becomes an option. [L1019.016]

Efficiency vs scale quadrant

Kill Low Low Turn off immediately
  • Goal: more than 70% of spend should go to ads above target ROAS. ROAS segments: exceeds, meets, below, failing. CPA segments: excellent, good, acceptable, poor, failing. [L0613.055]

Scale score ranking formula

  • Scale Score = (ROAS Index × 30) + (CPA Index × 30) + (Spend Velocity × 20) + (Fatigue Index × 20).
  • ROAS Index = Ad ROAS / Target ROAS, capped at 1.5.
  • CPA Index = Target CPA / Ad CPA, capped at 1.5.
  • Spend Velocity = Ad daily spend / account average daily spend.
  • Fatigue Index = 1 − (Frequency / 6), with a minimum of 0. [L0613.057]

Hard versus soft metrics

Hard metrics decide, soft metrics diagnose

  • Hard metrics such as sales outrank soft metrics. If an ad makes sales, a high CPC is acceptable, and you should keep improving the soft metrics. If there are no sales, improve all the metrics across the funnel. [L0639.032]
  • Hard metrics such as profitable sales matter more than soft metrics such as attention rate or click-through rate. An ad that makes sales should be kept even if its soft metrics are weak, and soft metrics are improved afterwards. [L0995.016]
  • A low attention rate and low click-through rate can still be acceptable when the ad makes sales, because the people who engage may be more qualified buyers. A high attention rate with no sales is not a good sign. [L0999.024]
  • Soft metrics are early funnel signals: link clicks, add to cart and initiate checkout, together with their cost columns. Hard metrics are purchases and ROAS. Read the soft metrics first to judge whether an ad is worth scaling or needs more budget. [L1030.003]
  • Read soft metrics (engagement and early signals such as cost per click and CVR) alongside hard metrics (ROAS, CAC and profit) to decide what to fix. A product that fails may need better landing page, creative or product detail page work before it is judged dead. [L1038.014]
  • Add-to-cart activity signals real buyer interest. If CPC, CPM and CTR look bad but add-to-carts are happening, let the campaign run another day before cutting it. [L0874.016]
  • The primary decision metric for a new Facebook product test is CTR in the first phase. CTR is read before sales, since sales need more spend and time to show up. [L0562.001]

Conflicts on the metrics that matter

  • Daily decisions (Source E, 2025 to 26): use only purchase-related metrics for daily optimization. Do not make major decisions based on CTR, CPC, CPM or conversion rate alone. These secondary metrics inform analysis but should never drive primary decisions. [L0290.008]
  • Early test phase (Source C): CTR is the main early metric in the first phase of testing a product on Facebook because it compares the people who saw the ad with those who clicked it. [L0562.001]
  • New store (Source C): primary metric for a new store is CTR, at least 1.5%. [L0642.032]
  • Add-to-cart classification: Source C lists add-to-carts among hard metrics (judge ads by sales, profit and add-to-carts). [L0999.023] Source D lists add-to-cart among early soft signals, with purchases and ROAS as the hard metrics. [L1030.003] Use add-to-carts as early signals and not as the final verdict, since the sources differ on the label.
  • Phase 5 ad testing: optimize using four metrics: CTR (click-through rate), CPM (cost per thousand impressions), ATC (add-to-cart rate) and conversion rate. These indicate creative strength, targeting efficiency, funnel engagement and sales conversion. [L0199.013]
  • Ad metrics targets for a test: CPM below $25, CTR above 1.5% (ideally 2%), and CPC below $1. [L1171.020]
  • Store conversion rate target during a test is at least 1.5% of visitors buying. [L1171.021]
  • For a clear winning product, track only ROAS and sales. [L1173.036]

Cut-early signals and metric discipline

  • Cut losing ads early using soft metrics. Keep an ad only if these metrics improve day over day, otherwise cut it. [L1019.042]
  • CPC and profitable spend matter more than CTR. [L0648.033]
  • Do not judge a test ad by CTR alone. [L0692.018]
  • Judge ads by hard metrics first; soft metrics matter only if an ad is not making money. [L0999.023]
  • Judge performance using a product's numbers, not by attachment. [L1176.022]

Seasonality and volatility

  • Many products sell less in summer because customers spend more time outdoors, so many stores see a dip in that season unless they sell summer products. Plan lower spending for that period rather than treating it as a store failure. (older advice, 2021 to 2022) [L0949.027]
  • Seasonality changes both demand and ROAS for a product. Outside the selling season, traffic and data are lower. A product that reached 4 to 6:1 ROAS at $50 per day dropped to about 4:1 at $10 per day, which was not enough to scale. Rising in-season demand can lift the same ad's ROAS again. [L0995.057]
  • Seasonality affects results: demand can be lower outside the peak season, which reduces traffic and ROAS, and rises again during the season, which can push ROAS higher and make scaling easier. [L0999.061]
  • Outside the season, a product reached about 4:1 ROAS at $10 per day, which was not enough to scale in its own campaign with an aggressive budget. [L0995.022]
  • With small order volumes, sales vary randomly from day to day and week to week even when traffic and ads stay the same. Do not react to a few quiet days. (older advice, 2021) [L0671.103, L0671.239]
  • Do not increase ad spend because Pinterest or Google account representatives push for bigger budgets. Their pressure does not lead to more sales. (older advice, 2021) [L0671.235]
  • Monthly sales are volatile: a few large orders can drive a month, so judge by sample size over several weeks, not one month. (older advice, 2022) [L0969.053]
  • A campaign with a few large orders can show a very high return in one month and a much lower one the next, so one month of results is not a reliable basis for decisions. Make judgments on a larger sample, usually several weeks of data. (older advice, 2022) [L0969.115]
  • Early results are unstable, because the pocket and the pixel are still learning. [L0639.006]

Testing structure and tracking

  • Set a weekly spend limit for product testing before any campaign launches. The limit determines how many products you can test in a week. A test that runs out of budget without a winner should be cut quickly. [L1194.058]
  • Run each product in its own campaign rather than sharing one campaign. Testing two products in a shared campaign can record the same sale for both products, which makes it hard to see which product drove add-to-cart, checkout and purchase events. [L1347.028, L1347.029]
  • Install UTM tracking so every sale can be traced to its source campaign. Without it, Business Manager figures alone are not reliable for product comparison. [L1347.028, L1347.029]
  • Analyze results in Business Manager and in your tracking data to see which product performs better. [L1347.028]
  • Change only one variable at a time, such as the creative, the audience or the store, so you can see what caused a result. Once a store and product have proven themselves, test creatives while keeping the audience and settings identical. [L1377.004]
  • Optimize in steps so each change can be attributed to a single fix. [L1377.005]

Scale or hold

  • Scale a product or ad set only when its ROAS is at least breakeven ROAS plus 1. For example, with a breakeven ROAS of 2.5, scale once the ROAS reaches 3.5 or higher. [L1185.029]
  • Scale a campaign only based on its break-even ROAS. Campaigns below break-even ROAS are not profitable, and campaigns above it can be scaled. [L1476.041]
  • During a sale, check ROAS every four hours to decide whether to add budget. Continue scaling only while the ROAS is still strong. [L1363.048]
  • Judge ads only after testing a batch of creatives over about a week, not on a single day. A ROAS that holds at or above break-even over that period signals profitability. [L1354.008]
  • Products of this type can show a high ROAS when they are scaled fast. Speed of scaling matters because the product's window of demand is short. [L1211.009]
  • A high ROAS after two or three purchases usually falls back to a lower plateau. Expect a drop from an early high (for example 9 ROAS) toward a more realistic level (for example 4 to 5) and plan around that, not the early number. [L1315.024]
  • Facebook ads were expensive and inconsistent even early on, which made profitable scaling difficult. Test budgets and expectations with this in mind before committing large spend. [L1251.034]
  • A successful TikTok ad winner is not required to be a unique product, since a widely dropshipped product can perform well if the ad itself is well executed. Judge the ad on execution, not on product uniqueness. [L1374.008]

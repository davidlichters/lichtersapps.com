17.1 Designing the support operation: scope, channels and ownership

Scope: which channels support covers

  • A store's support scope is live chat, inbound phone and email, plus social messaging if the store uses it. At low volume one person can handle all of these. (Older advice, 2021.) [L0834.090]
  • Cover three channels: email, live chat on the store, and social media messages and comments. Social media means Facebook page messages, Instagram DMs and comments on TikTok organic videos. [L1358.020, L1360.023]
  • Answer direct messages and comments on time. If you run organic TikTok videos, assign someone to answer the comments on them. [L1358.024]
  • Staff live chat or another support channel, because new visitors ask questions before they buy. Publish your support hours and an email address so buyers know a real person answers. [L1346.029]
  • At launch, let customers reach the store through live chat, email and phone. Their questions show how the business actually works and what needs to be clarified. [L1446.011]
  • A customer service role covers live chat, phone calls, and order shipping and tracking. Social media and content writing go to a separate person, because combining them breaks down quickly. [L0970.134]
  • Automating about 30% of tickets lowers first response time and cost, and frees the team for complex cases that can lead to sales. Instant self-service answers also beat making customers wait for an agent. [L0752.034]
  • TikTok direct messages now transcribe voice memos automatically as they are sent, which makes person-to-person conversations easier to read and follow. [L1504.013]
  • A structured customer service setup answers questions quickly, prevents refunds caused by confusion, builds trust in the brand and supports sustainable store growth. [L0911.002]
  • Phone volume in a small dropshipping store can be about one or two calls per day, and nearly all of them can be sales-related. At that volume, phone can be an additional task for a freelance assistant who already works at a computer, rather than a full-time role. [L0910.061]

Scope of post-sale support in a store sale

  • A typical post-sale scope for a store sale is about one month of email support plus two calls of at least one hour each over that month. Agree the scope before the deal closes. [L0553.019]
  • Pre-selling means the calls and emails a buyer needs before committing to a purchase. Products that need a lot of pre-selling add workload for the owner, so avoid them when building a low-effort store. [L1517.017]

Who handles what at each stage

  • Early on, the owner answers every chat, email and call personally. Quick replies to stock and order questions turn clicks into sales. (Older advice, 2020.) [L1111.132]
  • Handle all customer service yourself at first, so you learn which questions customers ask and how to solve their problems. (Older advice, 2019.) [L1110.040]
  • The store owner handles customer contact and makes sure customers are satisfied, while the supplier usually stores and ships the product. Returns reduce the total sales figure in the store report. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0771.051]
  • Move customer service off the owner's plate as early as possible, even if only part of it is delegated at first. This covers phone calls, emails, online chat and sometimes supplier contact. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L1247.002]
  • Typical contacts that interrupt an owner are shipping time questions and order-status questions (where is my order), arriving by phone, email and online chat. These are low-value, unpredictable inquiries and should go to a support person. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L1247.021]
  • A virtual assistant can handle emails and phone calls as order volume grows. (Older advice, 2020.) [L0468.145]
  • Hire a virtual assistant working about 4 to 8 hours a day only after you know the questions and answers yourself. (Older advice, 2019.) [L1110.040]
  • Owners who run the business from abroad need phone and email coverage, handled personally or outsourced, to keep buyers who are ready to purchase. [L0923.053]

  • As order volume grows, split customer service into two teams:
    • Pre-purchase (sales, inquiry response, incentive-based compensation).
    • Post-purchase (fulfilment, returns, support). [L0263.050]
  • Pre-purchase staff must understand order profitability, discount authorisation limits and real-time stock status before speaking with customers. Without this knowledge, they promise excessive discounts or unrealistic delivery dates, creating losses and operational chaos for the fulfilment team downstream. [L0263.050]

Workload by price point

  • Products priced at $2,000 or more typically require higher levels of pre-sale and post-sale customer support. (2024.) [L0253.004]
  • High-ticket products raise the customer service workload, mainly in pre-sale questions and post-sale follow-up. Plan for this before selling products above about $2,000. (Older advice, 2022.) [L0854.083]
  • Customer service is central in a business with expensive items, since more support is needed as prices rise. Plan staffing and support around the price and return rate of the niche. (Older advice, 2021.) [L0801.124]
  • Customer service volume rises with higher-priced items, so plan support for those products. (Older advice, 2020.) [L0468.145]
  • Lower-priced products attract more pre-sale calls, such as customers asking whether an item is in stock. Higher-priced products mean fewer calls, but you need to be prepared to answer or return calls quickly. (2023.) [L0835.051]
  • Sources differ on higher-priced products. Some describe a higher overall level of support (L0253.004, L0854.083, L0801.124). Others describe fewer calls but a need for fast replies (L0835.051). Both apply: plan for more total support and faster response time per contact, even if the number of calls is lower.
  • Expect a slightly higher volume of inbound support requests for electronics and complex products. A high order value, such as an $8,000 sale, can justify handling that extra support. (Older advice, 2018.) [L1274.017]
  • Fewer orders mean fewer tracking numbers, fewer shipping problems and less customer service work. This workload reduction is a main reason to sell higher-priced products. [L0474.006]
  • A low-ticket store of $40 to $50 products sourced from China generated high order volume but became a support nightmare. High order count with low-value products is not better than fewer higher-value orders. (Older advice, 2021.) [L0962.127]

Workload by order volume

  • Early on, support contacts in a new dropshipping store start slowly, often with only a few calls and sometimes none on a given day. Workload grows only once sales and momentum build, so early volume is usually manageable. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0909.001]
  • At high order volume a store can receive hundreds of customer emails per day. This is a reason to outsource support early. [L1358.019]
  • Many customers buy without ever contacting support, so slow replies affect only some of your sales, mainly early on. Sales continue while you handle the backlog. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0909.016]
  • The share of buyers who call support before purchasing varies by market. In markets where many customers ask questions first, plan more phone capacity. In others, expect most buyers to purchase without contacting you. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0909.029]
  • The early small-volume and later high-volume figures describe different stages of the same store. Both apply: plan for a quiet start and for a large inbox once sales grow. [L0909.001, L1358.019]

Hours, response windows and coverage

  • Buyers who are ready to purchase will leave for a competitor if calls or emails go unanswered for more than a few hours. (2023.) [L0923.053]

  • Some customers will not wait for a callback, so slow responses will cost some sales. There is no perfect fix, so treat it as an accepted trade-off while the business is small. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0909.005]

  • Pick up a call immediately only when you can take it from a quiet place. Otherwise let it go to voicemail and answer emails or chats in set blocks, such as about half an hour during the day. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0909.004]

  • Email is easier to handle in batches, because there is a natural gap between a customer's message and your reply. Answer emails after work or in set blocks during the day. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0909.015]

  • Daily coverage routine when working in a far time zone:

    1. Check emails right before going to bed, around 21:00 to 22:00 local time.
    2. Fulfil any orders that came in during the day so they do not wait a full day.
    3. Send tracking numbers, either personally or through a VA.
    4. Check emails again first thing in the morning.

    About 20 minutes at night and a morning check cover the start and end of a normal business day, so suppliers and customers see replies during their working hours. [L0910.006]

  • Check a competitor's support quality by searching its store name and URL on Google and reading the reviews. Complaints about unanswered calls or emails show a gap you can close by replying quickly. [L0894.014]

  • Do not invite overseas customers outside your shipping area to contact you. Shipping costs often exceed the product price, and such inquiries are frequently fraudulent. (Older advice, 2021.) [L0801.120]

Setting up support step by step

  1. Handle all customer service yourself at first, so you learn which questions customers ask and how to solve their problems.
  2. Add answers to frequent questions (shipping times, warranties, stock status) to your website, so fewer people ask.
  3. Set up automated post-purchase emails with tracking updates, for example with the AfterShip app.
  4. Hire a virtual assistant working about 4 to 8 hours a day only after you know the questions and answers yourself.

(Older advice, 2019.) [L1110.040]

Keeping support separate for each store

  • Keep customer service and support separate for each store, because each store has its own customers and its own problems. Mixing support across stores creates confusion that is hard to undo. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0949.034]

Support and growth: what to expect

  • Good customer service protects reputation and can bring referrals and positive reviews. It will not multiply store traffic or customer numbers on its own, so owners should not expect support to be a major growth driver. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L1247.020]
  • Customer service is a growth lever. Buyers with a good experience come back, while a bad experience stops repeat purchases. Treat service quality as a core part of building a business that lasts. [L1472.001]
  • Fast answers to customer questions, especially before purchase, can raise the conversion rate. Customer support therefore affects sales, not just service. [L1358.021, L1360.020]
  • Sources differ on how much support can grow the business. One view says support does not multiply traffic or customers (L1247.020). Another says it raises conversion and repeat purchases (L1472.001, L1358.021, L1360.020). Both apply: expect support to protect and convert the customers you already have, not to bring in new visitors.
  • Customer service is a core success factor. E-commerce businesses that offer no service either fail or stay small. Expect to answer phone calls and emails, because the belief that you never have to do this is wrong. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L1480.027]
  • Long shipping times combined with a poor customer experience caused the 2016 to 2018 approach to fail, even when cheap Facebook ads brought in orders. Consider shipping speed and customer experience alongside traffic. [L1459.019]
  • For a newly launched dropshipping store, customer service and shipping are the operational areas where owners most often have questions. Sorting out these two areas early is a common starting point for daily store management. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L1499.002]

1.20 Planning, vision, goals and operator mindset

Backwards planning and the destination

  • Plan the business by starting from the 10-year end-point and working backwards to the current position. Then place checkpoints along the way to that destination. (Older advice, Source B 2021-22.) [L0439.003]
  • Planning is easier when you start from the target and work backwards, because the steps between today and the target can then be derived. Working forward from the current position without a defined destination is harder. (Older advice, Source B 2021-22.) [L1256.005]
  • Plan backwards from the target in this order: [L1256.006]
    1. Define the end-point target, such as monthly revenue, yearly revenue, or yearly profit. [L1256.006]
    2. Work backwards from that target to identify the intermediate metrics and benchmarks that must be reached along the way. [L1256.006]
    3. Arrange these benchmarks as progressive steps and determine what each step requires. [L1256.006]
  • A target such as $10 million in annual revenue is too abstract to plan for directly when starting out. Set the destination first, then break it down into the milestones needed to reach it. (Older advice, Source B 2021-22.) [L1256.007]
  • Break a revenue goal into milestones, such as targets two months and four months ahead, then list the actions needed in the next few weeks. Treat this as a simple business plan for the launch. (Older advice, Source A 2021.) [L0962.080]
  • Worked planning example: a store with $50,000 monthly revenue (about $600,000 per year) that needs to reach $3 million in year three can set $1 million as the year-one target, $2 million for year two, and $3 million for year three. This means adding roughly $1 million of revenue each year. (Older advice, Source B 2021-22.) [L1519.006]
  • Detailed strategy and tactics become practical only when the target is three years or less away. A 10-year vision works best as a destination that is broken into 3-year and 1-year milestones. (Older advice, Source B 2021-22.) [L1519.018]

Ten-year vision

  • Set the vision questions in this order before writing the vision document: (Older advice, Source B 2021-22.) [L0439.010]
    1. How much revenue will the business produce per year? [L0439.010]
    2. What profit margin, and what profit per year, will it make on that revenue? [L0439.010]
    3. Which products will be sold? [L0439.010]
    4. What will the website look like? [L0439.010]
    5. How will customers be engaged? [L0439.010]
    6. What will the marketing look like? [L0439.010]
    7. How big will the team need to be? (for example 5, 10 or 20 people) [L0439.010]
    8. How involved will the owner be day to day: still working in the business, or in an advisory or management role? [L0439.010]
    9. What will the owner's day-to-day life look like? [L0439.010]
    10. What personal income and personal financial situation does the owner want? [L0439.010]
    11. How will the business finances be managed? [L0439.010]
    12. How well known will the brand be, and who will the customers be? [L0439.010]
    13. What are the 3 to 5 core values? [L0439.010]
  • Describe the traffic mix in 10 years, for example a shift from heavy paid traffic from Google Ads or Facebook ads toward referrals and organic traffic. Alternatively, paid ads can be maximized if they stay profitable. (Older advice, Source B 2021-22.) [L0439.018]
  • A vision can include selling the business within 10 years. In that case, the owner should describe what business or work they want to do afterwards, because the vision still needs a plan beyond the current company. (Older advice, Source B 2021-22.) [L0439.009]
  • Think big-picture about doubling a business instead of focusing on small monthly gains. Ask what could double revenue overnight, and plan for those large levers, such as major retail distribution, a new clinical or patent asset, a direct-response TV or radio campaign, or a new sales platform. [L0334.049]
  • A long-term goal can be a brand that retires the owner over a four to five year period by building revenue and then selling it. [L1212.047]

Documenting goals and vision

  • Document goals and vision as follows: (Older advice, Source B 2021-22.) [L0414.014]
    1. Write a documented set of goals and a vision covering the next month, the next year, three years out, and beyond. [L0414.014]
    2. Treat this document as the roadmap that all marketing work is planned against. [L0414.014]
    3. Do this before building the marketing system, because skipping it leads to a lack of focus and weaker results. [L0414.014]
  • Define a specific income target in your currency before choosing a business model or strategy. The target determines how much profit per sale and how many orders are needed to reach it. [L1503.009]

180-day plans and goal setting

  • Build the 180-day plan in this order: (Older advice, Source B 2021-22.) [L0447.006]
    1. Copy the Google Sheet planner into your own Google Drive. Never edit the shared original. [L0447.006]
    2. Enter the plan end date, which is also the next review date. [L0447.006]
    3. Set revenue goals for the six months: a total, a monthly target such as by end of June or July, or a weekly figure. [L0447.006]
    4. Set profit goals, either keeping the current profit margin or raising it if the past year showed the need. [L0447.006]
    5. Set no more than five goals or priorities. Fewer is fine. [L0447.006]
    6. For each priority, set measurables with target numbers at the six-month mark. [L0447.006]
    7. Write a short description of the business and life as they will look when the goals are reached. [L0447.006]
    8. For each priority, list every task needed, including recurring weekly tasks. [L0447.006]
    9. Break the plan into two 90-day quarters (first and second quarter rocks), with 90-day priorities and tasks that are smaller steps toward the six-month targets. [L0447.006]
  • Set revenue and profit targets that are realistic for the six-month window, because unrealistic short-term targets are demotivating. In high-ticket dropshipping, a business doing $50,000 per month cannot realistically reach $1,000,000 per month within six months, but reaching $100,000 per month in six months is achievable. (Older advice, Source B 2021-22.) [L0447.008]
  • Set long-term goals (10-year and 3-year) very ambitiously, because people overestimate short-term progress and underestimate long-term progress. Goals under one year should be more realistic. (Older advice, Source B 2021-22.) [L0447.009]
  • Expect a new dropshipping business to need a planned first phase before the next stage. Use the 7-day and 60-day plans as short-term steps and the 12-month plan as the long-term effort. With AI, the 7-day and 60-day plans can be drafted in a few hours, while the 12-month plan is a long-term effort. This assumes the site audit and its fixes are already done before the planning starts. [L1389.001]

Annual review of fundamentals

  • Review and update the core marketing and business fundamentals at least once every 12 months, whether the business is new or several years old. Anything never done before must be completed for the first time. (Older advice, Source B 2021-22.) [L0761.001]
  • Some fundamentals should be checked more often than once a year. Competitor analysis, business figures and goals change over time and must be kept current, so they need the most frequent review. (Older advice, Source B 2021-22.) [L0761.002]

Focus on one business

  • Do not overcomplicate the dropshipping business model. Focus on the fundamentals: find the right product, build a basic store, run ads, collect emails, and scale. [L0280.043]
  • At this stage, run one niche and one store at a time. Do not start several stores in parallel. (Older advice, Source B 2021-22.) [L0490.025]
  • Splitting effort across several parallel ideas produces weaker results than putting 100% of the effort into one business model. [L0631.002]
  • Focus on one business at a time. Running several projects at once divided attention and slowed growth. Cutting side projects to concentrate on the one with the highest revenue potential was the decision that let growth accelerate. [L0790.006]
  • Improving one strategy incrementally is usually more effective than switching strategies altogether when a current approach shows partial results. [L0691.030]
  • Not every advanced strategy suits every store. Choose which levers to pull based on what the store's own numbers show it needs. [L0544.002]
  • Tactics that work for one operator may not work in another niche, so test each one in your own business before relying on it. [L1357.046]
  • Make the most of what you have to start. Build the store with the resources you have, and keep improving rather than waiting for ideal conditions or premium tools. (Older advice, Source A 2021.) [L0801.169]
  • Early in the business, focus on the tasks that bring the next level of revenue, such as adding products and suppliers and running ads. Skip polish and low-impact projects until the store is generating sales. (Older advice, Source A 2022.) [L1281.029]
  • Once a store works, the work is continuous improvement, not switching niches. Keep reviewing operations, supplier quality, product pages and ad efficiency. (Older advice, Source A 2021.) [L0853.070]
  • Drop shipping is a long-term business that depends on continuing to improve. Keep auditing ad accounts, adding suppliers and expanding into new niches over time. (Older advice, Source A 2021.) [L0801.173]
  • No online store is fully passive, because real products, customers, and suppliers all need attention. Income can be semi-passive but still requires regular oversight. [L0826.018]
  • When starting a new project next to an existing business, use a 90/10 time and investment split. Keep 90% on the proven business or career and 10% on the new venture, so the proven income is not harmed while the new idea can grow. (Source A 2024.) [L0805.051]
  • If you cannot change the game you play, change the rules you play by. If you can do neither, you are probably in the wrong market. [L0378.011]
  • Product testing done only to read metrics carries low emotional risk, which is why many sellers stay stuck in testing indefinitely. Start a brand with the intention of building a real business that can be sold later. [L1042.011]

Realistic timelines and patience

  • Accept that there is no reliable timeline for reaching a specific monthly profit, because many variables affect results. The priority is to build consistent traffic and sales each month rather than waiting for sales to arrive by chance. (Older advice, Source A 2019.) [L0960.058]
  • Progress builds up in small steps, for example zero to one, then one to two, then two to three. Constant small progress reaches a large goal. Do not let time pass without taking action. (Older advice, Source A.) [L0359.008]
  • Build the business with a horizon of about 12 months rather than a few months. Businesses that make the most money are often the ones that stay around the longest. [L1251.009]
  • Expect at least six months before a new brand shows a clear picture of whether it can succeed. Do not treat the first months as the verdict on the business. [L1508.023]
  • Expect a new dropshipping business to take six months to one year, or longer, with paid guidance, before results are consistent. Review each failure with the question why it failed, and let each failure yield concrete takeaways. [L1440.002]
  • Expect one to two years before the brand really gets going. The brand was founded in 2017, but full-scale operation only started around 2019, so plan for a long, slow build with a lot of trial and error. [L1438.003]
  • Build the business around a long-term vision and keep moving toward it in steps, rather than trying to force fast results. Patience combined with a steady income is what lets a founder survive the early failures. [L1438.004]

The timeline figures measure different things and do not contradict each other. Six months is the minimum before a clear verdict, six months to one year or longer is the range for consistent results with paid guidance, and one to two years is the range for the brand to get going at full scale. [L1508.023, L1440.002, L1438.003]

Income floor and steady income

  • Keep a fixed monthly income floor from a small, low-hour job while the store is still unstable, because dropshipping revenue can vary sharply from month to month. This lets the owner cover bills and avoid being forced to make business decisions out of financial pressure. [L0690.004]
  • Decide in advance on a minimum monthly amount the business must reliably reach. Go full-time only once that minimum can be hit comfortably every month. [L0690.006]
  • Keep cash reserves and be ready to start a new store or product at any time, because a single store can collapse quickly. Treat the current winner as temporary and keep building the next test in parallel. Running several products or a second store at once reduces the anxiety caused by depending on one winner that may fade. [L0690.005, L0690.044]
  • Keeping a steady income source while the business is young gives you financial stability and freedom to experiment without pressure. The job can also provide skills, contacts and a daily routine that help the side business. [L1343.029]
  • While a new business is still small, keep a steady income source, and choose that job for transferable skills such as research, web design or copywriting. The income protects you from financial pressure, and the skills can be applied directly to the side business. [L1343.045]
  • A business owner can increase income in steps by growing profit, for example from $3,000 to $4,000 or from $7,000 to $8,000 per month. An employee rarely gets a raise of $1,000 per month, so business ownership is the more realistic path to that kind of income increase. [L1126.011]

Lifestyle versus corporate choices

  • A typical lifestyle drop shipping store reaches about $10,000 to $60,000 in monthly sales and $4,000 to $10,000 in monthly net profit, with the owner working around 15 hours per week. [L0413.001]
  • A lifestyle business is built to return the most money for the least owner time invested, so time cost should be weighed alongside revenue when choosing a business model. [L0585.001]
  • For a lifestyle business, aim for the smallest input of effort and time that produces the largest output of results, profit and scalability. Choose niches with this ratio in mind from the start. [L1517.001]
  • Decide early whether you want a lifestyle business that gives you location freedom, or a larger corporate-style company with employees and many locations. Both are realistic, and the choice determines where your effort and energy go. [L1039.005]
  • Online businesses selling physical products without holding stock let the operator work from almost any location and travel while running the store. This location freedom is one of the main reasons to choose e-commerce over a local business. (Older advice, Source B 2021-22.) [L1014.004]
  • Early on, working for a small owner-operated business, even outside e-commerce, teaches management, operations and entrepreneurial habits. These skills matter most later, when staff handle day-to-day work and the owner oversees them. (Older advice, Source A 2020.) [L0852.002]
  • Typical life-design targets for a drop-shipping owner, starting point versus goal: [L0827.002]
Area Starting point Target
Monthly expenses $4,000-5,000 $1,000-2,000
Tax burden 40%+ around 5%
Weekly working hours 40+ under 20
Responsibilities many almost none
Vacations one every few years more frequent
Monthly savings $200 $3,000-8,000

[L0827.002]

Business as an asset

  • Build the business as an asset that keeps running without the owner's daily work, because buyers pay for self-running businesses and not for a job. Cash flows from short-lived trends are worth less to buyers than lasting assets. [L1359.001]
  • Test whether a business is an asset by checking whether it keeps running and earning while the owner stays away for one to two months. If income stops when the owner stops working, the business is a job, and buyers will value it as one. [L1359.045]
  • Drop shipping and online sales generated nearly all income, while offline businesses took far too much time for too little money. Offline businesses were sold off once online operations were established. (Older advice, Source A 2020.) [L0852.009]
  • Once a repeatable process works, the same steps can be applied to build another store. This makes losing a store less threatening, because the process itself is the valuable asset. [L0826.021]
  • Once a business generates money, you can reinvest in a second opportunity, build multiple income streams, or keep the one that gives you energy. A dropshipping store can also serve as a base to pivot into work you actually want to do. [L0701.029]
  • Case: a seller made printed t-shirts for many hobby audiences (scuba diving, snowboarding, skating) on the platform Teespring and had some results, such as about 50 sales for some designs and a couple of shares for others. He struggled to see why he cared about the shirts beyond needing the money, and other changes in his life pushed him to decide on a change. He first worked on personality and found he already knew his proficiencies well, but his problem was matching his self-confidence and choosing the market he wanted to serve. His values were connecting people and building communities, and helping others master their craft. He then moved to hobby t-shirts for older women, covering anything a grandmother or aunt does in her free time, and set up a Facebook group where sewers and knitters share patterns and techniques. He set up the group but did not look at it for about two weeks, because a virtual assistant (VA) manages it, and he uses a full-time designer for the designs. He still builds the analytical framework that defines target audiences and design direction, and an assistant manages the budgets, drawing on his background in PPC consulting. Working with this setup, the overall business grew about three times, and he described the change as going from pushing a rock uphill for three to four months to feeling gravity help him down once he understood himself. [L0701.030]

Anti-fragility and resilience

  • Anti-fragility is an ongoing process, not a fixed state. Turn every negative situation into a chance to find order, and build a repeatable process that gains value from each setback. [L1357.039]
  • A first store that does not perform is not doomed and should not be discarded automatically. Use what you learn to improve it, and build another store alongside it rather than instead of it. (Older advice, Source A 2020.) [L0961.077]
  • In business, reward generally rises with the risk taken, so a conservative approach usually produces small returns. Check your actual risk level by looking at your bank account and the commitments you have made. Think big, because building a large business requires about the same effort as building a small one. [L1513.018]

Advertising principles for testing

  • Treat advertising as a measurable sales investment, and judge each campaign by the sales and profit it actually produces, not by brand awareness or general exposure. Any spend that cannot be tied to results is a guess and should be justified or cut. [L1261.001]
  • Advertising is salesmanship at scale. Hold each ad to the standard of a good salesperson who must justify their time and cost. One ad reaches thousands, so a weak ad costs far more than a weak sales conversation. [L1261.002]
  • Only scale a channel, offer or ad once a small, traced test has shown the cost per customer and the profit per customer. Methods that consistently win in tests become standard rules for the business. [L1261.003]
  • The cost per customer measured in a small test is a reliable predictor for a large rollout, because averages hold when the test is representative. Knowing what the first thousand customers cost tells you what the next million will cost. [L1261.004]
  • Do not start national or wide advertising before the product can actually be found and bought by the people who respond. An ad that sends demand to places where the product is not available wastes its budget. [L1261.005]
  • Spending money to educate the whole market on a new habit usually costs far more than the sales it creates, and competitors often capture the benefit. Campaigns should target people who already have the need and are open to buying a solution. [L1261.006]
  • In one test, a habit-conversion campaign cost $20 to $25 per converted person, and much of the spend reached people who were already converted. Costs like this make one-time sales unprofitable unless repeat purchases are very likely. [L1261.007]
  • Mail-order sales of single products rarely cost less than $42.50 per customer, so a one-off product must be able to recover that cost through its margin or repeat purchases. Compare the expected revenue per customer with the acquisition cost before launching. [L1261.008]
  • The fastest path to national distribution often starts with direct response offers that create demand. Retailers then stock the product once customers ask for it, and they are more willing to stock a product when they can see proven demand. [L1261.009]
  • Small expenses on a guess rarely produce big gains, while small tested expenses can grow into large profitable campaigns. Scale is safe only when the unit economics have been proven at small size. [L1261.010]
  • Mail-order advertising is the most accountable form of advertising, because every reply, sale and cost is visible, so its ads are good models to copy. When a non-direct campaign departs from proven direct response methods, the departure should be deliberate and its extra cost known. [L1261.011]

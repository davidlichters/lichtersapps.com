1.21 Valuing and selling a store

Deciding whether and when to sell

  • Stores are usually sold for a few recurring reasons: the owner has better uses for the cash, a life event happens, the owner has lost interest, or the business has peaked and stopped growing. [L0858.001]
  • Common concrete reasons include wanting cash for a second store or another business; life events such as marriage, divorce, bereavement or adoption costs; boredom with the niche; and a growth plateau that a buyer with a different skill set may still push higher. [L0553.002]
  • Owners sell for concrete personal reasons such as paying off a mortgage, affording a baby, moving capital into rental property, or funding a larger business. Finding the real reason helps the seller negotiate the terms they actually need. [L1468.001]
  • Deciding whether to sell is personal. One option is to sell only some of the stores to build a cash reserve and keep the rest. (older, 2020) [L0468.012]
  • Sell when the business has growing or steady sales and profits. Two years of historical data strengthens buyer confidence, and the market trend does not need to be perfect. [L0231.007]
  • A flat business is also acceptable to sell. A declining business sells at the low end of the multiple range, and buyers are very nervous about it. [L0554.005]
  • Sell while revenue and traffic are steady, slightly declining, or rising, since buyers prefer those profiles. Letting a site decline through neglect makes it much harder to sell and lowers its price. [L1468.003]
  • Sell a store while its revenue and profit are rising every month, because buyers pay more for a store on an upward trend. If a store has a weak month, improve it before considering a sale. [L0826.014]
  • Do not sell just because a store reached a revenue milestone. Keep feeding a store that is still growing, and consider selling once it flatlines, because spending another 18 months growing it can add a large amount to the eventual sale price. (older, 2020) [L1277.005]
  • If growth is still climbing steeply three months before listing, consider delaying the listing until it levels off. [L1468.015]
  • Avoid listing at a peak you believe is final. Sellers often close on the expectation that a buyer will keep growing the business, and owners often do not know when they have reached a peak, so buyers may acquire at the top profit level to grow it further. [L0553.025]
  • The two positions above differ in emphasis: the 2020 advice (older) says to sell once growth flattens, while the peak warning says not to list when you believe the peak is final. In both, do not wait for the decline. [L1277.005, L0553.025, L1468.003]
  • Do not sell right after a peak season. [L0553.039]
  • A store is worth closing mainly when its customer service workload outweighs its sales, which is common with low-ticket products. A store with running ads but little traffic should be optimized first, because its conversion rate cannot be judged yet. (2025) [L0837.066]
  • Do not turn off ads or stop running the store once it is listed for sale. Buyers want running and growing businesses, so the business must keep performing during the sale process. [L0554.007]
  • Do not wait until you are disengaged from the business to sell. An actively managed, profitable operation commands a higher valuation than one run by a burned-out owner. [L0231.008]
  • A business run at 20 or more hours per week sells for the lowest multiplier on net profit or annual sales. A business run at five hours per week or less gets a significantly higher multiplier and attracts a larger pool of buyers who want a portfolio asset. [L1122.005]
  • A dropshipping site earning 1,000 USD per month that needs about 40 hours of personal work per month is hard to sell, because buyers see it as a job. Preparing a site for sale should start about six months before the sale. [L1359.002]

Valuation

Formula and pricing basis

  • Store valuation = average monthly net profit (revenue minus expenses) × multiple. Online dropshipping stores sold at 21x to 35x monthly net profit, which is roughly 2x to 3x annual profit. [L0554.001]
  • Example: a dropshipping store with $5,000 monthly net profit values at $105,000 using a 21x multiple or $175,000 using a 35x multiple. [L0231.003]
  • Use the 12-month average of net profit as the pricing basis, because buyers trust longer periods more. A 3- to 6-month average can fit a fast-growing business, but a steady $5,000 per month for 12 months is worth more than $5,000 in only the last month. [L0554.003]
  • Buyers who pay high multiples value an online store on its trailing 12 months of revenue and net profit, with the averages of the most recent three months weighted most heavily. (2025) [L0966.001]
  • Store value is not created by hours worked, money spent or future potential. Buyers pay only for the profit the store earns now, so owners should first increase profit and then sell. [L0858.003, L0553.040, L0553.001]
  • Sellers should focus on maximizing a store's value before listing it, because buyers pay for proven monthly net profit rather than for hours worked, invested money, or future potential. [L0553.001]
  • Buyers pay for a fast learning curve, for trading money for time, for established market demand visible in existing sales, and for applying their own unique advantage, such as paid social ad skills, to a store that lacks them. [L0553.003]

Profit-based examples

  • A store with an AliExpress/apparel model, $8,700 monthly profit, Facebook-only traffic, Chinese shipping and decreasing monthly earnings was listed at $183,000 (21x). It took 126 days to sell and sold for $120,000 (13x). The store was 12 months old, and buyers had the same negative view of AliExpress models. [L0553.029]
  • A dropshipping lifestyle store with $5,000 monthly net profit, 18 months old, using Google Ads, organic traffic and Facebook retargeting, with steady earnings, was listed at $142,000 (28x). It sold for $133,000 (26x) in 10 days from listing. [L0553.030]

Revenue-based and brand valuation

  • The valuation multiple depends on revenue, not profit, so an exit at a revenue multiple can be much higher than a profit multiple. [L1153.014]
  • A brand with its own store, email list, Facebook group and subscription service is valued much higher than a pure Amazon seller. An Amazon-only business sold for about 30 to 35 times monthly profit, while a true brand with those assets can reach about 2.5 to 3 times annual revenue. [L1153.012]
  • The first brand the course group sold achieved a 37 times multiple on monthly net profit. The sellers note that a revenue multiple of 2.5 times would have produced about double the sale price, which is why they built a brand. [L1153.013]
  • A brand with consistent revenue, returning customers and several traffic channels can be sold for about 3x to 5x its profit, even when profit margin is only 10 to 15 percent. Building the brand is therefore an asset, not only a profit source. [L0871.013]
  • Sales from a store sold on a verified marketplace are typically valued at about 5 to 7 times annual profit. Empire Flippers was mentioned as a marketplace to check, and the Shopify Exchange marketplace had closed. [L0642.039]
  • The 5 to 7 times annual profit figure is higher than the current 2x to 3x annual profit range for dropshipping stores [L0554.001]. Apply it to sales on verified marketplaces only, as stated for that source. [L0642.039, L0554.001]
  • A profitable high-ticket dropshipping store may be sold as an asset for anywhere from tens of thousands to around $1 million, typically a couple of years after it is built. Treat these as anecdotal ranges, not a valuation standard. (older, 2021-22) [L0912.003]
  • A store or network is typically valued at about 30 times its monthly net profit. A network making 25,000 USD net profit per month would sell for roughly 750,000 USD. (older, 2020) [L1277.004]
  • A dropshipping store that sells about 100,000 USD per year is treated as a successful benchmark, and the first sale is an early milestone on the way to it. Stores at that level can be valued well above 100,000 USD when sold. [L0826.024]

Factors that raise or lower the price

Factors that raise the price

  • Net profit alone does not set the value of a store; quality matters. A store with diversified traffic, several products and steady profit sells for more than a store with higher profit that depends on one channel and shows declining trends. [L0553.031]
  • Higher quality, meaning steady earnings, several traffic sources and a longer track record, produces a higher sale price and a faster sale. Quality matters more for the price than the owner's effort. [L0858.015]
  • Buyers want traffic from several sources, because a single source such as Google Ads creates risk if it underperforms. Organic and social traffic alongside paid traffic makes buyers more comfortable. [L0554.009]
  • Specialist stores are worth more than broad general stores. Specialist stores can build email lists, Facebook groups and push notification lists that buyers value. [L0554.012]
  • A specific niche with a recognizable brand protects valuation, because it differentiates the store from large retailers and creates assets competitors cannot replicate overnight. [L0231.005]
  • Store niches with broad consumer demand, such as dropshipping lifestyle niches, sell more quickly and at higher multiples than AliExpress-sourced stores that rely on Chinese shipping, poor product quality and Facebook-only traffic. [L0553.026]
  • Dropshipping lifestyle stores sell quickly at high multiples; member stores typically sell quickly at high multiples. (older, 2021) [L0270.007]
  • A business with documented, transferable systems is worth substantially more than one that depends on the owner. The lesson claims an increase of several times and sometimes up to about 10x. Systems also make the business easier to hand over. [L1473.035]
  • If 30% of your traffic comes from one forum account, buyers will expect that account to be included in the sale. Be ready to explain every major referral source to a buyer. [L1468.009]
  • A store with a strong general trend and a long financial history is easier to value, and the seller can explain irregular months. [L0553.053 is not used; see L0554.053 below] [L0554.053]
  • Sites in the same niche with overlapping suppliers are valued together, and a buyer will often purchase related sites as a bundle. Keeping them under one LLC makes that bundled sale simpler. (older, 2022) [L1092.023]
  • Strategic buyers, usually competitors in the same niche, generally pay the highest price. They can cross-sell to your existing customers, so they value the customer base more than the current profit alone. [L1468.004]
  • A brand built around proven sales can later be sold to another owner, so it is worth structuring the store and its records so the business can be transferred. This makes it easier to build several brands one after another. [L1439.056]
  • Build a brand that can later be sold: grow revenue with a brand, build infrastructure, connections and branding partnerships, and then sell the whole brand to a larger company. [L1212.046]
  • Stores with a full set of SOPs, training documents and documentation are easier to sell. A business with a virtual assistant running daily operations and documented SOPs is a clear example. [L0554.053, L0554.052]

Factors that lower the price

  • Buyers reject AliExpress-based stores because they lack a defensible moat (easily copied), offer poor customer experience from China shipping, depend entirely on finding winning Facebook ads, and have no established brand. [L0231.004]
  • The first question a broker asks when vetting a dropshipping store is whether it is a Source A store or an AliExpress-style store. Brokers stopped listing AliExpress-style stores because they were hard to sell. [L0554.055]
  • A store that depends on a single traffic source with falling earnings is hard to sell and sells at a much lower multiple. Diversifying traffic and keeping earnings steady protects the sale price. [L0858.014]
  • Buyers pay a lower price for stores with one channel because a single source creates risk. Diversifying traffic and suppliers reduces perceived risk for buyers because the business does not depend on a single channel or vendor. [L0231.009, L0858.014]
  • Avoid building stores around short-term fad niches. Buyers prefer sustainable markets with long-term product demand. [L0231.006]
  • Monthly dips are normal and do not hurt the sale if the general trend is steady or positive. Each dip should have an explanation, such as a supplier problem, because unexplained, sporadic earnings worry buyers. [L0554.006]
  • A heavily seasonal business such as Halloween supplies may receive a lower multiple when sold far outside its season, because buyers must estimate performance. Seasonal stores can still be sold. [L0553.039]
  • A store with no profit, weak assets and no email list or social following is worth roughly 1,000 to 3,000 dollars. A store with built-up assets such as an email list and social pages may reach about 4,000 to 5,000 dollars. Profitable stores are valued as a multiple of monthly net profit, not on a design-only basis. [L0941.004]
  • Non-English dropshipping or affiliate sites are avoided by buyers, unless the foreign-language portion is small. English-speaking countries pose no problem. [L0553.035]
  • Local services alongside ecommerce are not included in the valuation. Establish the local service as a separate legal entity. [L0231.027]
  • Only 100% online businesses can be sold through the marketplace. A local service run by the owner is usually not counted in earnings, although a local team that can be managed online may be considered. [L0554.011]
  • Letting a site decline through neglect makes it much harder to sell and lowers its price. [L1468.003]
  • A broker valued a site and a strategic buyer could pay more; the difference between a broker's valuation and a strategic price is a reason to approach competitors. [L1468.005, L1468.013]

Store examples that show the effect of neglect

  • A seller had a Twitter-background website earning a couple of thousand dollars of profit per month. The owner stopped working on it, and over a few years profit fell to a couple of hundred dollars per month. At its peak the site could have sold for about $40,000 to $50,000, but it was given away for free instead. [L1468.002]

Preparing the store for sale

Six months before the sale

  1. Make your final changes to content, link profile and SEO, including any change of monetization model, because Google needs a couple of months to recognize them. [L1468.014]
  2. Start tracking revenue, costs and owner time separately for each store. [L1468.014]
  3. Order or run a basic due diligence report to find problems buyers would flag, and fix what you can. [L1468.014]
  4. Cut exploratory paid traffic that has no ROI and focus on what produces net profit. [L1468.014]
  5. Speed up the site with quick code fixes rather than an expensive hosting upgrade. [L1468.014]
  6. Audit the backlink profile, disavow spam links, and reduce over-optimized anchor text by adding more varied anchors. [L1468.014]
  7. Replace duplicate product descriptions with unique ones. [L1468.014]
  8. Install Google Analytics (or an accepted alternative such as Clicky) and make sure you control or can grant admin access. [L1468.014]

Three months before the sale

  1. Compare current traffic trend (stable, growing, declining or seasonal) with the changes you made three months earlier and check whether they are still helping. [L1468.015]
  2. If growth is still climbing steeply, consider delaying the listing until it levels off. [L1468.015]
  3. Identify strategic buyers, get to know competitors and check whether they would be open to acquiring your business. [L1468.015, L1468.013]
  4. Check that supplier, affiliate and special pricing terms are clean and can be transferred to a new owner. [L1468.015]
  5. Verify that you are visibly a real person online, such as with real social profiles and connections. [L1468.015]

Start looking for strategic buyers and talking to competitors about three months before the sale. Strategic buyers are the route most likely to produce the highest multiple. [L1468.013]

One month before the sale

  1. Document all processes as standard operating procedures (SOPs). [L1468.016]
  2. Replace yourself in day-to-day work with VAs, so the business runs without you. [L1468.016]
  3. Review the financials, cut useless expenses, and confirm revenue and profit are clean and tracked per store. [L1468.016]
  4. Set up a training and site-turnover plan for the new owner. [L1468.016]
  5. Add at least two alternate suppliers, even if you do not use them. [L1468.016]
  6. Shop for a broker or platform that fits your site and price target. [L1468.016]
  7. Turn off WHOIS privacy so your name shows as the domain owner. [L1468.016]
  8. Prepare the listing materials: earnings records, traffic data, and any video or screencast evidence. [L1468.016]
  9. Make sure all financial figures in the listing match each other exactly and that date ranges and metric definitions (sessions or page views) are stated. [L1468.016]

Sale timeline

  • Prepare a sale plan about six months before the sale, because the site needs time to recover from changes. [L1359.002, L1468.014]
  • Vetting usually takes 2 to 4 weeks. [L0554.013]
  • Typical time on market is about 2 to 3 months. Average time on market in the last 12 months was 66 days. [L0553.027]
  • Store listed with a broker: the full timeline is in the broker process below. [L0554.013]

Documentation and handover preparation

  • When selling an online store, do not leave the handover to the end. Assemble the store's documentation, supplier and account details, and process notes before listing it, because reconstructing them after the sale is slow and frustrating. [L0826.023]
  • Keep documentation as a separate asset. A brand built around proven sales with clean records can be transferred to another owner. [L1439.056]
  • A transition can be handled by informing each supplier that a new owner is taking over, asking them to remove your card from future payments, and putting the new owner's card on file. The same step applies when closing a store to stop future charges. (2023) [L0970.017]

Broker process

Steps for selling a dropshipping store through a broker, in order:

  1. Get an initial online valuation on the broker website. [L0554.013]
  2. Submit the business for vetting, which usually takes 2 to 4 weeks and requires a large amount of information, including checks of the Shopify numbers. [L0554.013]
  3. The broker markets the business to its buyer list and may run seller interviews for YouTube. [L0554.013]
  4. The seller answers buyer questions and enters negotiations. [L0554.013]
  5. The business is transferred to the buyer and the seller receives payout. [L0554.013]

Broker fees and incentives

  • Brokers charge 10 to 20% of the sale price as their fee. They reject 30 to 40% of the sites submitted, and rejected sellers get their listing fee refunded. [L1468.008]
  • Broker fees are a percentage of the final sale price, so the broker earns more only when the seller earns more. This aligns incentives, but the seller should still check the broker's listing standards and fee terms before signing. [L1468.050]
  • The platform's brokers represent the seller and owe the seller a fiduciary duty. They earn fees on the sale price, so sellers must decide their own minimum acceptable price rather than rely on brokers to push for the highest possible offer. [L0553.038]
  • Broker fees create a weaker incentive than the seller's own interest in price. On a store that sells for $270,000 instead of a $300,000 list price, the seller loses $30,000 while the broker's fee difference is only about $4,500, roughly 15 percent of that gap. Sellers should therefore decide their own minimum acceptable price before reviewing offers. [L0553.043]
  • Brokers that vet listings typically have staff collect the site's information and then have a principal approve or deny each listing. Expect a curated marketplace with rejections rather than automatic acceptance. [L1468.051]

Channel eligibility

  • Only online businesses are eligible for sale on this platform. Physical-location, retail, offline and real estate-related businesses are not accepted. [L0553.035]
  • The sale platform accepts almost any online business model, including non-dropshipping types such as Kindle e-book businesses. It excludes offline businesses, businesses with a physical or local component, and real estate-related businesses. [L0553.045]
  • A Shopify store is hard to sell unless it has at least one year of revenue history and roughly 500,000 USD in total revenue. Buyers for such stores were mostly US-based companies, according to the speaker's experience. [L1469.014]
  • A store with no real revenue has almost no resale value, and buyers typically want about 12 months of consistent revenue. If a store has no sales, it is usually better to drop it and focus on a new product or store than to try to sell it. [L0660.006]

Vetting and due diligence

  • Brokers' vetting takes 2 to 4 weeks and requires a large amount of information, including checks of the Shopify numbers. [L0554.013]
  • Buyers ask for the full financial history, commonly four years of financials. A business without accurate, complete numbers cannot be sold. (older, 2021-22) [L0599.007]
  • Basic due diligence reports from third-party services such as Centurica tend to be conservative. Fixing the issues they flag before listing reduces the chance that a buyer uses the report to negotiate the price down. [L1468.052]
  • A business with a sustained sharp decline should be considered for sale while it still has value. A buyer pays for something, while a business at zero yields nothing. (older, 2021-22) [L0449.010]
  • If a business is declining toward zero, selling it earlier can still recover some value, which is better than selling nothing later. (older, 2021-22) [L0599.008]
  • The critical question buyers ask is whether the business will thrive and operate successfully without your direct involvement and daily management. This assessment determines whether they acquire an independent, scalable asset or commit themselves to a full-time job. [L0231.032]
  • Buyers reject a store if it lacks a defensible moat, has poor customer experience from China shipping, depends entirely on finding winning Facebook ads, or has no established brand. [L0231.004]
  • Store with unexplained dips: each dip should be explained, as stated above. [L0554.006]
  • Use the broker's industry report and buyers' numbers to test the store's trend before vetting. [L0554.008]

Verifying the numbers before a sale

  • Make sure all financial figures in the listing match each other exactly, and that date ranges and metric definitions (sessions or page views) are stated. [L1468.016]
  • Prepare the listing materials: earnings records, traffic data, and any video or screencast evidence. [L1468.016]
  • Track revenue, costs and owner time separately for each store. [L1468.014]
  • Pets niche case: financial data covered from the start of 2016 and earlier, showing a growing business with some dips. [L0554.052]
  • Beauty niche case: the accounting was cash-based, and some monthly spikes were caused by when supplier bills were paid. [L0554.053]
  • If 30% of your traffic comes from one forum account, buyers will expect that account to be included in the sale. [L1468.009]

Vetting of competitors and buyers

  • Competitors often request due diligence only to copy the business, so vet any competitor carefully before sharing your books. [L0554.014]
  • Buyers who pay high multiples value an online store on trailing 12-month figures. [L0966.001]
  • Only 100% online businesses can be sold through the marketplace. [L0554.011]

Buyer profiles

  • Dropshipping store buyers comprise several profiles: cash-rich, time-poor investors seeking passive income; operators who prefer hands-on management; creators focused on building and innovation; cash flow investors prioritizing profitability; and portfolio or fund buyers acquiring multiple businesses for diversification. [L0231.029]
  • Typical buyer profiles include a salaried person seeking a lifestyle change with $200,000 to spend on a store earning $8,000 to $9,000 monthly profit; a hands-on buyer looking for a dropshipping store around $40,000 to tweak; and an experienced e-commerce operator who adds stores to existing staff. [L0553.037]
  • Buyers of online stores are often people with money but no time, people who want to skip the trial-and-error stage, and institutional or fund buyers. Online assets are compared with index funds and real estate as investments, with higher risk but potentially higher returns. [L0554.010]
  • Strategic buyers, usually competitors in the same niche, generally pay the highest price. [L1468.004]
  • Some buyers want a passive dividend and do not want to run the site. A broker can place investor capital into a site and have a managing team run it, though this was described as an early test. [L1468.011]
  • Private equity typically buys deals of $10 million or more, and the platform handled deals up to about $5 million. Newer micro-funds pooling $10 million to $100 million for small online assets started appearing in the last 12 months. [L0553.036]
  • Buyers want to see a business that will thrive without the owner. A store that depends on the owner is a job, not an asset. [L0231.032]
  • Dropshipping stores remain attractive to buyers and retain strong valuations despite market concerns that dropshipping is declining. Buyers actively seek dropshipping businesses, particularly those seeking small business capital, and valuations have not fallen drastically. [L0231.031]
  • The market outlook for dropshipping is positive, with valuations rising over the previous two years. Recessions may push buyers toward models that need less upfront capital, which can increase demand. [L0554.016]
  • Buyers pay for proven monthly net profit. [L0553.001]
  • Brokers and strategic buyers as channels for buyer types: [L1468.004, L0553.036]

Deal terms and payment

  • Buyers may pay part of the price at closing and hold back the rest until a contractual obligation is met, such as finishing staff training after the handover. For example, a $300,000 sale could be split into $200,000 paid at closing and $100,000 paid once training is complete. Sellers who train their team before listing avoid this deferred-payment risk. [L0553.041]
  • Buyers may hold back part of the payment, for example 50% upfront and 50% over six months, or 70% upfront and 30% after training and handover. This happens when the business depends on the owner's knowledge. [L1468.012]
  • The post-sale support a seller offers should match the buyer's experience. Buyers of a small store around $40,000 are often newcomers who need more help, while buyers of stores in the $400,000 to $500,000 range usually have more experience and need less. The support scope is agreed and written down before the deal closes, and extra support can be used as a negotiating point. [L0553.042]
  • A non-compete usually applies in the same niche for a set period after the sale. (2024) [L0836.047]
  • Check that supplier, affiliate and special pricing terms are clean and can be transferred to a new owner. [L1468.015]
  • Sell only once the business is clean: confirm the financials, supplier terms and documentation before terms are negotiated. [L1468.016, L0970.017]

Handover and SOPs

  • Document all processes as standard operating procedures (SOPs). [L1468.016]
  • Replace yourself in day-to-day work with VAs, so the business runs without you. [L1468.016]
  • A pets store used a customer support virtual assistant for chat, phone calls, emails and Zendesk tickets, and the assistant was willing to continue with the new owner. [L0554.052]
  • A beauty store had a full-time virtual assistant handling daily operations, and it had full SOPs, training documents and documentation. [L0554.053]
  • Set up a training and site-turnover plan for the new owner. [L1468.016]
  • Add at least two alternate suppliers, even if you do not use them. [L1468.016]
  • Turn off WHOIS privacy so your name shows as the domain owner. [L1468.016]
  • Make sure you control or can grant admin access to analytics. [L1468.014]
  • When selling a store, a transition can be handled by informing each supplier that a new owner is taking over, asking them to remove your card from future payments, and putting the new owner's card on file. (2023) [L0970.017]
  • A business with documented, transferable systems is worth substantially more than one that depends on the owner. [L1473.035]
  • Brands with systems and records are easier to hand over. [L1439.056]
  • When a business with several sites is sold, the usual method is an asset sale: the sites, customer lists, historical data and SOPs are sold. The entity itself is then dissolved rather than sold. (older, 2022) [L1092.027]
  • Sites in the same niche with overlapping suppliers are valued together, and a buyer will often purchase related sites as a bundle. (older, 2022) [L1092.023]

Market data and case listings

Case listings (sold through a broker)

  • Pets niche case: a Shopify store created in August 2014 sold a variety of pet products for several needs of pet owners. It sold for $367K, listed at 33x, and was supported by a Facebook group presence and an email list of about 5,000 emails. Financial data covered from the start of 2016 and earlier, showing a growing business with some dips. Traffic came from 55% paid, 30% organic, 10% direct and 5% other sources. The store worked with several US suppliers, and a customer support virtual assistant handled chat, phone calls, emails and Zendesk tickets, and was willing to continue with the new owner. The store also used paid search. [L0554.052]
  • Beauty niche case: a dropshipping store focused on products for salons and massage businesses was listed at $167K at 30x. It had an email list of over 2,000 subscribers that was not monetized but received informational messages and links to blogs. The financial history was long with a strong general trend, though some monthly spikes were caused by when supplier bills were paid, and the accounting was cash-based. It had 18 suppliers, some with exclusive relationships, and about half of traffic came from paid search with some from organic sources. A full-time virtual assistant handled daily operations, and the store had full SOPs, training documents and documentation. [L0554.053]
  • AliExpress/apparel case: a store with $8,700 monthly profit, Facebook-only traffic, Chinese shipping and decreasing monthly earnings was listed at $183,000 (21x), took 126 days to sell, and sold for $120,000 (13x). The store was 12 months old, and buyers had the same negative view of AliExpress models. [L0553.029]
  • Dropshipping lifestyle case: $5,000 monthly net profit, 18 months old, Google Ads, organic traffic and Facebook retargeting, steady earnings. Listed at $142,000 (28x), sold for $133,000 (26x) in 10 days from listing. [L0553.030]
  • Twitter-background website case: see the neglect example above. [L1468.002]
  • Home lighting store case: a single home lighting store grew to multi-seven-figure annual revenue and was then sold for a six-figure sum, which freed capital for new stores. After the sale, the operator opened further sites and took partner stakes in other owners' stores. Selling a proven store and reinvesting the proceeds is a route to a portfolio of stores. (older, 2021-22) [L1014.008]
  • Early dropshipping store exit: a dropshipping store sold after about one year was priced at roughly one year's salary from a regular job. The first such exit was about 39,500 USD, showing that early store sales can be in the tens of thousands. (older, 2021-22) [L1502.007]
  • Building for exit: dropshipping stores can be built with the intent of selling them as an exit. Later stores reached exits in the high six figures, so building, scaling and selling a store is a legitimate business model in its own right. (older, 2021-22) [L1502.008]
  • Starting point case: the owner had about 1,000 USD left, no flight ticket home, and was building a store on a borrowed laptop while living in Thailand. The first sale came two months after starting. The store later reached over 1 million USD in total sales with close to 200,000 USD profit. Its web-only theme would have lost sales as shopping moved to phones, so the owner sold the store while it was still healthy and built another store using the same process. [L0826.013]
  • The first brand the course group sold achieved a 37 times multiple on monthly net profit. [L1153.013]
  • Early AdSense micro-site model: sites earned about 8 USD per month on average, and sites of this kind were sold at about 20 times their net profit. The money from each sale was reinvested into building the next, larger site. [L1359.003]
  • Private sale or broker example: sale prices in examples of this kind ranged from about $44,000 to $60,000 per store. [L1126.042]
  • Lesson sale of an early dropshipping site netted nearly $200,000 after two years of running it. (older, 2021-22) [L0458.007]

Reinvesting the proceeds

  • Treat the proceeds from selling a store as investment capital, not spending money. The lesson invested the full proceeds of each sale and kept living costs low so that no sale money was needed for daily life. [L1126.034]
  • Selling a profitable store, investing the proceeds into income-producing assets and then starting another store can be a time-saving cycle. Brokers such as Empire Flippers handle buying and selling stores. [L1515.025]
  • Selling a proven store and reinvesting the proceeds is a route to a portfolio of stores. After the sale, the operator opened further sites and took partner stakes in other owners' stores. (older, 2021-22) [L1014.008]
  • The early AdSense micro-site model reinvested the money from each sale into building the next, larger site. [L1359.003]
  • Building a brand that can later be sold makes it easier to build several brands one after another. [L1439.056, L1212.046]
  • Some buyers want a passive dividend; investors can place capital into a site run by a managing team. [L1468.011]
  • A seller can sell only some of the stores to build a cash reserve and keep the rest. (older, 2020) [L0468.012]

Market context

  • Brokers' sales data shows dropshipping valuations rising over the two years before the talk. [L0554.016, L0554.008]
  • Average store listing multiple in the last 12 months was 27x and the average sold multiple was 24x. [L0553.027]
  • The market outlook for dropshipping is positive, and recessions may increase demand for models that need less upfront capital. [L0554.016]

Further details

  • A store without consistent, established profit or revenue is valued mainly on its assets. Its price is then whatever a buyer is willing to pay. [L1092.025]

To estimate the sale price of a small store with no or low revenue:

  1. Open flippa.com.
  2. Filter for e-commerce stores with no revenue or low revenue.
  3. Note what similar stores sell for and use those prices as a reference for your own store. [L1092.026]

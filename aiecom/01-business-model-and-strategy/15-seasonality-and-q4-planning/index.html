1.15 Seasonality and Q4 planning

Planning the Year Around Q4

  • Q4 has been the largest quarter for US retail e-commerce sales in almost every year, the exception being 2020, when lockdowns pulled extra demand into another quarter. The US Census quarterly figures used here are unadjusted, and the data runs from 2016 to Q2 2025 in one source and from 2016 to 2025 in another. Plan promotions and inventory around Q4 as the highest-volume season of the year. [L1223.001, L1224.001, L1285.084, L1287.001]
  • Sales rise in Q4 even without action, because total buying increases, shoppers buy gifts for themselves and others, and they compare prices online and find smaller stores through competitors' promotions. A live store with no promotions gets only a small Q4 lift. Planned promotions turn that lift into a large spike, and the spike requires coordinated promotions across the website, search ads, social ads and email at the same time. The promotion effort covers five channels: content and SEO, the website, Google Ads, Meta Ads and email. [L1222.001, L1223.001, L1224.002, L1287.002]
  • A well-prepared Q4 can bring in roughly the revenue of two other quarters combined, which justifies the extra work of planned promotions. [L1224.003]
  • For one brand, about 89 percent of sales from October to mid-December were Christmas gifts, so ad spend should be raised in this period. [L1431.004]
  • Q4 order volume for winning products began picking up from September, and the surge arrived in the weeks before the peak season. [L0780.010]
  • Q1 is the weakest period for e-commerce, while Q2 to Q4 is the growth season. Test products in Q1 to find winners that can be scaled through Q4. [L1171.037]

Peak Revenue Months and Peak Count

  • Count revenue peaks across the year, such as Black Friday, Mother's Day, Father's Day, Memorial Day weekend or back to school. Aim for at least three peaks per year. If only one or two appear, add promotions or product drops to reach three. [L1235.020]
  • Older advice (2018): For established dropshipping stores, November with Black Friday and Cyber Monday is usually the highest-revenue month of the year, while October typically runs at normal levels. [L0466.001]
  • Older advice (2018): In December, the first two weeks usually bring most of the month's sales. The week before Christmas is the only real slump, because late orders cannot arrive in time as gifts. Sales soften again around New Year. [L0466.002]
  • Older advice (2018): Promotions often start between December 28 and December 31 and lead into a strong January, so plan inventory and ad budgets for that window. [L0466.003]
  • Older advice (2018): A store that is set up correctly should not stop selling in December. Treat the holiday period as a normal month, not a reason to pause. [L0466.004]

Launch Readiness and Lead Times

  • Seven weeks is sufficient to build and launch a complete store ready for Q4 traffic if working efficiently. Older advice (2021). [L0270.010]
  • Build the store around a sustainable business that can grow over the next decade, rather than chasing a short trend such as a holiday season. A late-starting holiday store can still be launched in a few weeks, but it is a last-minute effort and should not be the main reason to build it. Older advice (2022). [L0429.012]
  • Start planning Q4 promotions many weeks ahead, not in the final week before a holiday. Planning five promotions across about three months takes time, and a store launched a few weeks before Halloween still has time to build a list and run them. Planned promotions capture more of the holiday spending, so plan them several weeks ahead rather than days before the holiday. [L1223.002, L1285.085]
  • Setting up the store before a Q4 promotion still leaves time to build an email list through abandoned carts and exit overlays before the first promotion goes out. [L1285.099]
  • Black Friday and Cyber Monday planning should begin in September, so campaigns, promotions and messaging are ready before the October and November peaks. [L0278.022]
  • Winter products need to be ready for the next peak season in advance. Plan a store relaunch in October or early November for Black Friday. Preparing new creatives and an improved store before the season matters more than reacting during it. [L1439.003]
  • Plan seasonal products for the whole late-year period, not only for Black Friday, because buyer demand can arrive later in December. One seller made almost nothing on Black Friday but earned most of the season's results from the Christmas period. [L1444.064]

Supplier Approvals in December

  • Older advice (2018): If you already have approved suppliers, start Google Ads in December rather than waiting until after Christmas. [L0466.031]
  • Older advice (2018): If you are still waiting on supplier approvals in December, expect slow responses because staff are on holiday. Use the time to improve the website, build the supplier list, set up social pages and optimize conversions. [L0466.032]

Black Friday, Cyber Monday and Late-Year Timing

  • About 50 percent of e-commerce brands launched their Black Friday sale around November 17. Launching in that window is roughly mid-pack. A dip in sales before Black Friday is normal for almost all stores. [L1225.012]
  • Ride the holiday momentum up to the last order date for Christmas shipping cutoffs, and keep the messaging aligned with that end date. [L1225.011]
  • In early November, shift storefront and evergreen campaigns toward gift guides and gifting messaging instead of discounting. This keeps demand going before a formal offer. [L1225.009]
  • Holiday promotions such as Black Friday can add sales even for new stores, not only for stores with a loyal customer base. Traffic costs rise during these periods, so check margins before running them. [L1097.006]

Holiday Messaging and Email

  • Plan holiday messaging as one calendar with a separate wording set for each event, in this order: Halloween, Black Friday, Cyber Monday, Christmas and New Year. Each event needs its own angle so subscribers can tell the sales apart. [L0547.001]

  • Sequence matters across the season. After Black Friday, the Cyber Monday message should give a new reason to buy instead of repeating the same deal. New Year messages work best after Christmas, framed as a fresh start. [L0547.003]
  • Do not run a sales-driven push in a new store without a plan. Send at least one promotional email to the list with a discount code for the holidays. [L0836.056]

Audiences and Remarketing

  • Promote to warm audiences only: email subscribers and people who visited the site in the last 180 days, excluding recent customers. Cold audiences that do not know the brand are not the target of holiday promotions, because warm audiences are far more likely to buy and cost almost nothing extra to reach. [L1223.004]
  • Older advice (2019): Most products and businesses, including B2B, pick up around the holiday season, because consumers buy gifts and companies spend before year-end. Build your email list and audiences before the holidays, not only in December. [L1110.036]

Q5: The Post-Black Friday Window

  • Older advice (2020): Q5 is an informal label for the roughly three weeks between Black Friday/Cyber Monday and New Year, when many competitors pull back on ads and activity. Competition for ad space drops, and suppliers have more capacity to take on new accounts. [L0468.001]
  • Older advice (2020): During the low-competition window after Black Friday, keep and increase what already works, but do not launch new ad platforms or untested experiments. [L0468.002]

Ad Costs and Competition

The sources describe ad costs at different times and do not agree on one trend. Use each value for its own period and re-check against current platform costs.

  • Older advice (2022): CPMs (cost per thousand impressions) and CPC rates fell on Google and Facebook Ads as larger advertisers reduced budgets because of economic uncertainty. This created better-priced inventory, resulting in more traffic at the same ROAS (return on ad spend). [L0272.056]
  • 2023 expectation: When ad costs fall during a downturn, the plan is to hold revenue steady, which means more visitors buy for the same or lower ad cost. This was the expectation for 2023 and should be re-checked against current platform costs. [L0804.004]
  • 2023 observed level: Ad costs had returned to roughly pre-pandemic levels after being unusually cheap during COVID, when many local businesses cut their ad spending. Competition in dropshipping stayed about as intense as during the pandemic, while product availability had recovered. [L0970.164]

Economic Downturns and Recessions

  • During an economic slowdown, buyers with high incomes and products that solve a clear, important problem tend to keep buying. Favor these two groups when the economy is uncertain. [L0363.080]
  • During a recession, e-commerce growth dipped by about 13 percent compared to the previous year. Stores that remain profitable can still grow at a lower rate. [L0788.053]
  • Products that solve a real problem or have clear use cases keep selling during a downturn regardless of price. Novelty products need higher perceived value to sell well. [L0788.054]
  • A recession significantly affects only stores with very high volume, typically above $1 million over two or three months. Low-priced products of $24 to $29 with no clear use case are the most affected. [L0788.055]
  • A store with low fixed costs can lower ad spend in a downturn without paying bills to stay open. It keeps making money at lower volume and grows again when spending recovers. Older advice (2019). [L0467.053]
  • Launch the store now instead of waiting for supply chain problems or inflation to ease, because people keep buying and suppliers still have products to sell. The business adapts to market conditions as they change. [L0941.001]
  • When sales fall at the same time across many stores in the same market, first check whether the drop is market-wide by comparing your revenue trend with other stores and niches. This happened with many US e-commerce stores from June to July 2021. Longer supplier lead times and shipping problems can add to a slowdown, but any problem specific to your own store still needs its own fix. Older advice (2021 to 2022). [L0721.036]

Procedure for downturns and supplier closures (older advice, 2020)

  1. Maintain current ad spend during economic downturns if ROAS is stable.
  2. Accept orders with clear customer notification about delays.
  3. Follow up with customers manually via phone and email.
  4. Concentrate on building remarketing email lists and audiences.
  5. When the economy recovers, execute aggressive remarketing campaigns to this built-up audience.

[L0245.061]

  • Do not pause ads during supplier closures or economic uncertainty if return on ad spend remains stable. Instead, clearly notify customers of shipping delays before purchase and build remarketing audiences for future campaigns. Older advice (2020). [L0245.060]
  • L0467.053 and L0245.061 apply to different conditions. Lowering ad spend in a downturn fits a store with low fixed costs. Keeping spend fits a store where ROAS holds.

Seasonal Products and Seasonal Niches

  • For a first store, sell products that can be sold year-round rather than mixing seasonal ranges. Running two seasonal product sets doubles the work on suppliers, traffic analysis, conversion checks and page optimization. Seasonal pairs such as sun and snow products can work in principle but are not recommended for a beginner. [L0964.001]
  • Seasonal niches are not recommended as a first store because they lack year-round profitability. They can be added to a portfolio of stores after the core business is established, but should not be the starting point. Older advice (2021). [L0270.015]
  • Seasonal products lose most of their sales for roughly three months a year, so test other products to cover the shortfall. Products that are too expensive for a new brand to sell at scale are a poor choice, even if they are profitable per unit. Expect several test products to fail before one scales. [L1077.002]
  • For a seasonal product, run an aggressive offer during the off-season to cover the sales gap, since a deep discount can be sustained without large losses. Also target a region currently in its season, such as the Southern Hemisphere when the Northern Hemisphere is off-season. [L1077.003]
  • Use a pre-season sale before a market's season starts to extend sales across most of the year. Check shipping times before targeting a distant region. Shipping of 10 to 15 days lowered results when the supplier had no warehouse in that region. [L1077.004]

Case: UK seasonal dropshipping brand

A UK seasonal dropshipping brand started with about £300 of personal capital and no savings. The first product sold well immediately, but scaling it outside its selling season lost a lot of money. A later good season allowed scaling, and the business later took on a business partner. Current figures cited: about £30k monthly revenue, about £5k monthly profit, with a next target of £10k monthly profit. The owner learned web design, graphic design and advertising himself. [L1077.001]

Holiday Niche Stores

  • A holiday niche store can be broad within its theme, selling anything connected to the occasion rather than one narrow product. Open it quickly, reach out to influencers, and run ads to test before expanding the range. [L1214.017]
  • Ads sold holiday products, but their profit margins were lower than those from influencer marketing. Compare margin per channel before deciding where to spend. [L1214.018]
  • For a holiday such as Valentine's Day, about three weeks before the date was considered enough time to open a themed store, send influencer outreach and start ads. If creators do not reply, run paid ads to test the offer instead of waiting. [L1214.061]
  • Summer products should be items such as sandals, women's summer bags and accessories rather than swimwear like bikinis, which carries a risk of platform bans. [L1214.021]
  • A sandal product sold in 2020 was advertised with Facebook ads in Australia and New Zealand instead of the US, and it performed well. Before reusing an older strategy, check what has changed in the market and adjust it. [L1214.022]

Chinese New Year and Supplier Closures

  • Launching products just before or during Chinese New Year, which ends on February 10, risks delayed orders, complaints and disputes. Use the time before the holiday to research products, build product pages and ads, and launch several products after February 10. [L1127.011]
  • Before a holiday such as Chinese New Year, suppliers may close, so stock preparation has to start early. If stock runs out during the holiday, a pre-order model keeps sales going until restocking. [L1214.019]
  • If no winning product is available during a period such as Chinese New Year, stop running ads and use the time to learn more about dropshipping. Digital products are an alternative during that time and can be tested with ads. [L1161.017]
  • For ad handling during supplier closures, see [L0245.060] in Economic Downturns and Recessions.

Business-to-Business Year-End

  • Older advice (2019): Business-to-business sales also tend to rise before year-end, because profitable companies spend remaining cash on purchases to lower the tax they would owe. Plan B2B offers and outreach for the final months of the year, not only for consumer holidays. [L1110.051]

Case Studies: Launch and Black Friday Results

  • A store launched in April with a launch event and reached over $50,000 in total revenue and over 500 orders by Black Friday. About two-thirds of its month's revenue came from Thursday to Monday. Its VIP email day was its best day, and returning launch customers bought again six months later. The owner kept the ad budget conservative and used email heavily. [L1225.082]
  • A store ran a four-day Black Friday sale this year, against 14 days the year before, and still grew about 20 percent year over year with lower ad spend and better profit. It planned to keep its working campaign running and build cash flow before adopting a new structure. [L1225.083]

  • The 20 to 30% gross figure and the 30% store target are not in conflict. The first describes the typical market, the second describes what a new store should aim for. [L0511.002, L1179.002]
  • The 30% goal is an average across the whole store, not a rule that every product must meet. [L1179.003]
  • Calculate margin before running ads. Aim for at least 3x margin on the product price to scale profitably, because thin margins are a common reason for sales without profit. [L1260.056]
  • Margin depends on where the product sits in the supply chain (older, 2021 to 2022):
    • Keystoning: a retailer buys from a supplier at half the retail price, which gives the retailer a 50% margin. Retailers following manufacturer map pricing often work this way. [L0511.004]
    • Example: a retailer sells a 1,000 USD product bought from the manufacturer for 700 USD, keeping about 300 USD (around 30% margin) after its own costs of about 350 USD to make the product. With private labeling, the same 1,000 USD retail price against 350 USD cost gives a 65% margin. [L0511.005]
    • Private label markup: roughly 4x to 5x the landed product cost. For example, a product costing 10 USD should sell for 40 to 50 USD. [L0511.003]

Gross margin versus net

  • Dropshipping margins are thinner than in most other e-commerce models, so every sale has to be run deliberately to become profit. Revenue growth alone does not show whether the business makes money. [L0414.047]
  • The first task is finding products that can be sold above their full landed cost. Every product and supplier should be checked against this before it is listed. [L1148.001]
  • Free shipping is the common standard in dropshipping. The customer sees free shipping at checkout, and the shipping cost is paid out of the merchant's profit margin. [L1179.001]
  • Set the planning figure as net profit, not revenue. [L1147.006]
  • Revenue targets mean little without profit. A store should be proven profitable before it is scaled up. A store making 10,000 USD per month at a loss is not a success. [L1487.004]
  • Growth goals should be set in profit and cash flow terms, so each decision is judged by what it does to money kept. [L1458.012]
  • A product whose cost of goods is too high to be profitable at a realistic price should be dropped rather than forced. Spend the time on products with better margins. [L0640.036]

Profit per order and break-even

Margin per order determines how far ad spend can go.

  • Higher margins allow more advertising spend before break-even. With dropshipping, as paid advertising costs rise over time, low margins make the business unsustainable. Owning the brand and pricing accordingly keeps unit economics healthy longer. [L0227.003]
  • Higher margins free cash for acquiring new customers. When customer lifetime value is the goal, profit per order can matter more than order value. [L0511.007]
  • A business that earns more profit per sale can afford to spend more on ads and keep them running longer, which gives it an advantage over competitors with less margin per order. [L0740.015]
  • A store with only paid ads needs upsells, cross-sells, customer win-backs and a focus on customer lifetime value to become profitable. [L1215.026]
  • Use paid traffic only on products with real margin. Products with thin margins, such as those with heavy discounts, should rely on organic traffic. [L0551.053]
  • Use example prices and costs to learn how the expense structure works, but do not copy them. Run your own product price, supplier cost, shipping fee and ad budget through the same profit calculation before launch. [L0759.035]

Worked margin check for large items (older, 2021)

  1. Wholesale at 50% of MAP: a 1,500 USD sofa costs 750 USD, a 500 USD sofa costs 250 USD. [L0834.002]
  2. Subtract shipping (here 150 USD). [L0834.002]
  3. Reserve ad spend at a minimum return of 10x ROAS: 50 USD for the cheap sofa, 150 USD for the expensive one. [L0834.002]
  4. Subtract the merchant fee (2.9% + 0.30 USD). [L0834.002]
  5. Result as stated in the source: the cheap item is left with about 50 USD profit and the expensive one is strongly profitable. The same shipping cost can make one product break even and the other pay. [L0834.002]

Ad budget limits and stop-losses

Limits on ad spend, by product and by day.

  • Maximum ad spend to acquire a customer is up to 10% of the product's MAP price. For example, a 1,000 USD product allows up to 100 USD per customer acquisition. [L0251.013]
  • Set the target for every product at a return on ad spend of at least 10x, meaning each dollar of ads brings back at least 10 USD in revenue. Use this ROAS target as the main gate for whether a product keeps getting budget. [L1115.001]
  • A business with a target of 10 or more ROAS is considered profitable at the Google Shopping level, but profit per sale must still clear the dollar threshold set for the business. [L0856.114]
  • Start with a small daily ad spend, roughly 30 to 50 USD per day. Keep what produces results, cut what does not, and grow from there. [L0970.015]
  • Scale gradually instead of jumping. For example, raise a 1,000 USD monthly budget to 1,500 USD and check how return on ad spend changes. Scaling means balancing how much you can spend while return on ad spend stays strong. [L0970.016]
  • Ad spend is fully controllable. When ads stop converting well, lower the budget and optimize campaigns for profit at lower spend. [L0966.008]
  • Knowing the return per ad dollar gives the confidence to raise the budget step by step. Example: 30 USD back per dollar spent in the last month. A growth limit for many owners is reluctance to spend more on traffic. [L0359.003]

Stop-loss procedure when a store is not profitable and money is tight (older, 2020)

  1. Keep the Shopify subscription (about 29 USD per month) so the store stays open. [L0359.001]
  2. Cancel every paid app that does not lead to sales. [L0359.001]
  3. Stop paid traffic that does not convert. [L0359.001]
  4. Cut all other unnecessary expenses. [L0359.001]
  5. Consider a short contract or job with a small digital marketing business (SEO firm, pay-per-click agency or local marketing company) to earn income and keep skills and motivation up. [L0359.001]
  • Closing a store during a pause is a mistake. The store keeps its setup, and the fixed cost is small compared with rebuilding it. Stop only costs that do not produce sales. [L0359.002]
  • Monthly profitability should be consistent. If a store has some profitable months, find what separates good months from bad ones, such as ad return or product mix, rather than abandoning the niche. [L0664.050] (older, 2020)

Review before shutting down a niche with unprofitable months (older, 2020)

  1. Compare the figures for good months and bad months. [L0664.051]
  2. Check whether the difference comes from return on ad spend or another cause. [L0664.051]
  3. Have someone review the store and its numbers to find the fix. [L0664.051]

Cost structure

  • Apart from traffic, a dropship store has very low fixed expenses. Ad spend is fully controllable. [L0966.008]
  • Costs outside a brand's control: rising acquisition costs, shipping costs, software costs, and new tariffs on goods made outside the US. [L0955.001]
  • Costs a brand can control: returns handling, support time, lost parcels, refunds and fraud. Reducing them protects margin. [L0955.001]
  • Customer acquisition is usually one of the largest expenses, whether paid through Google, Bing, Facebook, Snapchat or TikTok ads or invested in SEO content. Ad prices tend to rise over time, so a brand must acquire customers more cheaply than competitors. (older, 2021 to 2022) [L0511.008]
  • Standard drop-shipping operators often keep under 10% net profit once shipping, card processing fees and capital expenditure are paid, so a 20 to 30% gross margin leaves little room for error. (older, 2021 to 2022) [L0511.021]
  • Review all paid software and cloud services. Switching from monthly to annual billing is a saving in itself, where cash flow allows. [L0575.010]

Cash reserves and cash flow

  • Sales cash arrives within days, so a dropshipping business does not need a credit card to carry a balance for months. The card works best as a payment tool with the balance cleared each cycle, not as financing. [L0699.008]
  • Do not go into negative cash flow or debt to pay for growth projects such as content marketing. Invest in these only as the business grows. [L0970.002]
  • Keep the business as the first priority. Do not inject money you may need for living costs or the emergency fund. [L1126.032]
  • Reserve target from the investor timeline: the savings target was 30,000 USD, but the real need was six months of expenses, under 6,000 USD. [L1126.033]
  • Pre-sales let a new business start with minimal cash flow. Cash can be conserved during product refinement and manufacturing by collecting money before production is committed. [L1016.006]

Funding and capital

  • Launch and ad spend should not be financed by a business loan or grant. Borrowed money tends to lead to overspending. Start lean with small ad budgets and increase spending only as cash flow allows. [L0966.009]
  • A founder raised up to about 200K USD specifically to fund aggressive scaling, but only after knowing the store's margin and cash position. Outside funding should follow proven numbers. [L1213.027]
  • Putting new money into the business is worthwhile when it can plausibly return about 10 times its cost. Dropshipping needs little startup capital. [L1126.032]
  • Card rewards (older, 2021 to 2022):
    • Rewards on expenses you already plan to pay work as cost-free arbitrage. The spending happens anyway, and the card issuer shares part of its fee with you. [L0903.003]
    • Early-stage owners with high personal debt and poor credit often lose access to additional cards, so card-based reward programmes are easier to access before credit deteriorates. [L0903.027]
    • Classic arbitrage example: buy an item for 50 USD at a thrift store or auction and sell it for 500 USD to a buyer who values it. Card rewards on spending you would make anyway add a similar return with no extra cost. [L0903.040]

Reinvestment and profit distribution

  • First-time profit distributions do not need to wait a set period. After weeks of sales generating net profit, you can take a distribution (for example 3,000 USD from 5,000 USD net profit) and reinvest the rest into the business. [L0268.063]
  • Reinvestment timeline from the lesson:
    • 2013: the presenter started with about 1,000 USD and launched a dropshipping store. [L1126.033]
    • 2014: the store produced about 3,000 to 7,000 USD per month in profit. [L1126.033]
    • 2016: a store built alone was sold for about 60,000 USD. A second store was sold in 2017, and a later store sold for 44,000 USD through the broker Empire Flippers. [L1126.033]
    • All proceeds were invested, starting with about 3,000 USD per month from profits. Within three years the index fund holdings reached over 200,000 USD, with about 37,800 USD of that from growth alone. [L1126.033]

Tax-efficient growth spending

  • Spending part of the profit on growth before taxes reduces taxable profit. Spending qualifies when it covers ads, new ad platforms, team members, contractors or coaching. It only makes sense when the spending is expected to bring more profit next year and the owner's lifestyle goals and salary are still covered. [L1344.006]
  • Example: out of a 200,000 USD profit, 50,000 USD can be reinvested in ads, testing new ad platforms, hiring or contractors, and coaching. If that spending is expected to lift profit in the following years, it can be worth it. [L1344.007]

Profit goals and income floor

  • Target monthly profit (net profit, not revenue) is the starting planning figure for a first store. Derive the required unit count backwards from it. In the lesson's example, the planning target was 3,000 USD net profit per month. [L1147.006]
  • Keep the day job until the new business produces enough cash flow to cover your own costs. Quitting early removes the income that lets you absorb early mistakes and keep working toward the goal. [L1438.002]
  • Fund the brand from part of a regular salary. Cover living costs first, then invest the remainder in inventory, start-up costs and monthly growth. The founder put about 60% of his salary into the brand. This works best with few fixed commitments such as rent or dependants. [L1438.001]
  • A store that is not yet profitable should first keep its Shopify subscription and cut costs that do not produce sales, while looking for income to protect the owner's finances. [L0359.001]

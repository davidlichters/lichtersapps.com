20.5 Choosing a hiring model and sequencing hires

Start from the problem, not the provider

  • Asking someone who profits from your purchase whether you need their service creates inherent bias, because the vendor gains from a yes regardless of your actual needs. Set your requirements independently before talking to any provider. [L0217.001]
  • Define the exact problem before choosing a solution. Document which capabilities are missing, what jobs need doing, and what success looks like before engaging any service provider. [L0217.003]
  • Five hiring models exist to solve business problems: freelancer, agency, full-time employee, part-time employee, and contractor. Choose the model that fits your specific problem and business stage, not the reverse. [L0217.002]
  • Most outsourcing problems come from weak hiring and weak ongoing management, not from outsourcing itself. A bad outcome usually means the wrong person was selected or was not managed effectively. [L1055.001]

Outsourcing preparation (Source B, 2021-22, older)

Procedure:

  1. Identify which tasks from your 180-day plan could be outsourced. [L0223.012]
  2. Create a Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) for each task to be outsourced, documenting exactly how it should be done. [L0223.012]
  3. Write job postings or advertisements for those positions. [L0223.012]
  4. Conduct interviews with candidates using provided template questions. [L0223.012]
  5. Test by conducting interviews via message format for efficiency. [L0223.012]
  6. Hire team members as positions are filled and bring them into your management system. [L0223.012]

Hiring process

Procedure (Source A), repeat for every new role to keep consistency and speed:

  1. Know who to hire: job roles and organizational structure. [L0263.008]
  2. Create and post the job, and design the filtering process. [L0263.008]
  3. Run interviews: a short call and a final call. [L0263.008]
  4. Onboard systematically. [L0263.008]

Four hiring stages, in this order (Source B, 2021-22, older):

  1. Post the job so candidates can find it. [L1055.002]
  2. Shortlist the applicants. [L1055.002]
  3. Interview the shortlisted applicants. [L1055.002]
  4. Trial the successful candidates. The trial overlaps with onboarding and is the last point where you decide whether to keep someone long term. [L1055.002]
  • Hire for one clear job at a time rather than a generalist, because one person rarely does sales, uploads, writing and support well. Test each candidate with a paid trial task and check how quickly they finish it. Hire part-time first and convert to full time when results are good (Source B, 2021-22, older). [L1396.048]
  • Breakdown example (Source B, 2021-22, older): a one-year goal of growing a customer service team by three people is split into three rocks, one new hire per quarter. Each hiring rock is broken into tasks: write the job, create the job advertisement, post it, shortlist, interview, make the hiring decision, and provide training if needed. These tasks are spread across weekly to-do lists over the 90 days. [L0679.015]

Economics of each hiring model

Agency versus in-house

  • For businesses under $10 million annual revenue, hiring a specialized agency is usually mathematically cheaper than hiring full-time specialists in-house. [L0217.013] (For reference, $10 million a year is about $833,000 per month on average. This figure is derived, not from the source.)
  • In-house senior positions in media buying, growth strategy or paid ads cost $30,000 to $40,000 per month in additional fixed expenses. Equivalent agency services cost $12,000 to $15,000 per month. [L0217.014]
  • Example: a creative agency charging $3,000 per month for 5 to 7 pieces of creative, or 10 creatives per month, is often more economical than a junior in-house designer at $5,000 per month (equal to a $60,000 annual salary). [L0217.015]
  • Once annual revenue exceeds $10 million, hiring specialized in-house roles becomes economically viable because the workload justifies full-time dedication and better efficiency. [L0217.016]
  • Where the sources differ: Source E sets the in-house break-even for specialist roles at $10 million annual revenue. Source C uses monthly revenue phases instead (see the revenue stages below) and moves to agencies for social platforms the store does not advertise on at $500,000 to $1 million+ per month. Use the Source E test for specialist roles and the Source C phases for sequencing. [L0217.013, L0217.016, L1129.002]
  • Hire an agency or outside help for ongoing ad management before building an in-house team, unless the threshold above is met. [L0217.013, L0217.014]

Hourly rates

  • A VA for product research or customer service can be hired on Upwork, which was preferred over Fiverr. A VA paid around $3 to $5 per hour was described as a reasonable rate. [L1127.032]
  • Hourly rates by phase (Source C): overseas customer service $2 to $6 per hour; project manager $8 to $12 per hour; supply chain manager $6 to $15 per hour; payment processing agent $8 to $12 per hour. [L1129.002]
  • Hire only when the new person's hourly cost is lower than your own hourly value, so the difference is margin. Example: if you are worth $20 per hour, look for help at $10 per hour. [L1359.011]
  • Pay the best person you can afford. Low pay tends to produce low-quality results, and paying a higher rate to skilled people is usually cheaper than fixing poor work. [L1000.023]
  • Cheap assistants can be expensive. Assistants paid around $100 per month delivered poor quality work, while freelancers on platforms such as Upwork gave better results. [L1431.018]

Efficiency-adjusted cost

Formula:

Efficiency-adjusted hourly cost = hourly wage ÷ share of output the worker actually achieves [L1359.020]

Example (derived from the lesson's figures, for illustration): $5 per hour at 20 percent efficiency costs $25 per hour of full-output work, which is more than $15 per hour at 100 percent efficiency. [L1359.020]

Offshore versus US hiring

  • A Filipino VA working full-time at about AUD 26,000 per year, all costs included, was compared with an Australian hire of the same qualifications at about AUD 75,000 per year with on-costs. Overseas staffing is the cost-effective option for small businesses. [L1122.016]
  • Five US staff costing about $20,000 per month can be replaced by Philippine staff costing about $5,000 to $6,000 per month, but only with the right processes and people in place. [L1359.019]
  • Hire all team members internationally, not from the US. Build from the Philippines, Bangladesh and South America via onlinejobs.ph, because of the lower cost base, strong customer service experience in those regions (BPO industry), cultural fit, and alignment on work commitment. [L0263.047]
  • Filipino VAs are a common choice for email, live chat and order processing because their written English is strong and they have experience with outsourced support work. Their accent makes them a poor fit for phone calls where it could hurt conversion. [L1430.008]
  • If the task needs knowledge of a specific culture, market or sport, such as a regional audience or a local sport, hire someone from that market. An offshore assistant can only handle basic tasks that need no deep cultural knowledge. [L1359.016]

  • Call team members "team members" or "employees", never "virtual assistants" or "VAs". This framing determines whether you build a scalable team or stay limited to two or three assistants. [L0263.002]
  • Hire remote full-time employees, not freelancers. Freelancers juggle multiple clients and will not invest in your systems, training and growth. Define this in job postings and screening to filter for people seeking stable employment who will commit fully to your business. [L0263.003]
  • Use freelancers for task-based work, and virtual assistants for ongoing work. A VA can be a freelancer, but a freelancer is not always a VA. [L1122.013]
  • Virtual Assistant Finder runs the full recruitment process for a fee: you describe the need and the agency finds candidates. 123 Employee is a managed VA provider for US businesses that offers Filipino VAs at 10, 30 or 40 hours per week, which suits owners not ready for a full-time hire. [L1122.015]
  • For an ongoing VA, you can hire a freelancer directly or use a managed VA service. A managed service usually gives more consistent results. With a freelancer, the whole selection process, from vetting to testing, is your responsibility. [L1122.029]
  • Choose a VA for work with objective, process-based decisions. Choose an apprentice for work that needs judgment, adaptation when the roadmap runs out, or subjective calls. VAs can handle more adapting work, but it is harder. [L1359.015]
  • Hire for one skill set per role, and keep the core skill of the worker matched to the task. A worker hired as a developer should not also be asked to write blog content. [L1359.018]
  • Hiring for advertising and content roles is harder than for customer service and product listing. Fewer qualified candidates exist on onlinejobs.ph for these skills. Expect a longer process and fewer applicants. Build your own email marketer internally and train them rather than relying on agencies. [L0263.040]

Specialists and generalists

Strengths and weaknesses

  • Specialists in specific skills (for example video editors) deliver high-quality output quickly, because they understand their domain deeply and have practiced it extensively. [L0260.005]
  • Avoid staffing entirely with specialists. They work in silos, lack versatility outside their skill set, may require larger teams to scale, and typically cannot also manage other team members. [L0260.006]
  • Generalists handle multiple varied tasks with agility, adapt to changing roles, are cost-effective for smaller teams, tend to have stronger leadership potential, and bring fresh ideas from outside a single domain. [L0260.007]
  • A team of only generalists lacks depth in specialized outputs. Their energy and priorities can disperse across many deliverables, making them vulnerable to conflicting demands unless priorities are explicitly aligned. [L0260.008]
  • Maintain a healthy balance of specialists and generalists to gain speed from deep expertise, fresh thinking from diverse perspectives, and leadership capacity for growth. [L0260.009]
  • Hire hybrid roles where specialists extend beyond their core skill set, for example a video editor who also handles animation and graphic design. These rare talents give the team valuable flexibility. [L0260.010]

Avoid the Super VA myth

  • Expecting one low-cost offshore worker to cover unrelated skill sets, such as coding, customer calls, writing and podcast editing, is a mistake. A $100,000 USD job cannot realistically be pushed onto one worker paid $400 per month. [L1359.017]

Generalists trained on your method

  • Training a generalist assistant on your own tested ad method worked better than hiring a specialist who only claimed Facebook ads experience. Teach the assistant your tested method first, then let them run their own variations. [L1431.065]

Virtual assistant scope and first tasks

What a VA takes over

Typical tasks a virtual assistant can take over:

Area Tasks
Order fulfillment Make sure orders are fulfilled every day and tracking numbers are sent to customers
Customer support Answer emails, DMs, social media comments and live chat questions from customers
Store management Create the store, do product research, manage PayPal and Stripe cases, stay in touch with the supplier, set up social media pages, run ads
Random tasks Any task the assistant is qualified for, e.g. managing finances

[L0765.006]

  • Build a VA team through a VA agency. Hand off store building, customer support and ordering to the team while the owner supervises. Keep order fulfillment in-house as long as it is manageable. [L0492.059]
  • A first VA works best on a set of closely related tasks, such as customer service across phone, live chat, email, social comments and messages, rather than one all-purpose person. Starting at about four hours per day and growing toward eight as the business scales is a practical pattern. [L0360.079]
  • Hire a VA to fill skill gaps such as supplier negotiation, customer support quality or store graphics. The assistant can cover areas where the owner lacks experience. [L0765.004]
  • A new store's team can be built from a VA who learns the SEO guide and makes small improvements to product pages each week (2023). [L0855.170]
  • One-off tasks such as product uploads are better handled as fixed gigs on Upwork or StoreTasker, paid per job, than by a dedicated VA on set hours. [L0360.080]
  • A VA is especially useful for beginners in dropshipping who feel overwhelmed. They can help build the store, perform product research, and answer questions when the owner is unsure. [L1418.010]
  • Beginners who feel overwhelmed can benefit from a VA for store building, product research, and answering questions when they are unsure. Support of this kind can speed up early progress. [L1433.010]
  • Customer service and product research: a VA for either can be hired on Upwork. [L1127.032]

Order-volume and margin gates

  • Hire a VA only once sales are consistent, meaning at least 5 to 10 orders per day. Before that point, the workload is usually too small to justify the cost. [L0765.001]
  • Hire a VA once you consistently get about 5 to 10 orders per day for at least three weeks and can no longer keep up with emails and comments. Record a Loom video of the process so the VA can start work quickly. [L1218.023]
  • Hire a VA when your profit margin is high enough to pay for the assistant and still leave extra income. If margin cannot cover the cost, hiring will drain the business. [L0765.002]
  • Hire a VA for order processing, email, live chat and turning visitors into shoppers only when business profits can pay for outsourcing, and only after you know each process yourself. [L0941.096]
  • Do not hire virtual assistants until the store is profitable and you have run it yourself for about a month. Document each process as a playbook so a new hire can take over tasks with clear instructions. [L0941.095]
  • Handle customer questions yourself first so you learn what people ask and how to answer them. Do not hire staff or outsource work until the business's own profit pays for it. [L1287.008]
  • Before hiring a VA, check that the business can pay their monthly wage. Do not hire someone and let them go a month later, because they depend on that income. [L1437.020]
  • Hire a VA once the business can pay for them from profit. A typical VA salary is roughly $300 to $800 per month, which should come from store profit before you hire. [L1090.134]
  • Outsource to a team of VAs, based in the Philippines and hired through Online Jobs PH, once the store is making sales and getting traffic. Hire to offload day-to-day tasks and keep yourself in an oversight role (2021). [L1090.136]
  • Start outsourcing when the store is profitable and the owner feels overwhelmed by daily work. The first hire is usually a VA (2019, older). [L1275.051]

Revenue and profit stages

Monthly net profit thresholds (Source A)

  • The first hire should be customer service, made once the store nets $2,000 of profit per month. Hiring is tied to actual monthly net profit. [L1446.024]
  • The second hire should be a person to upload and optimize products and fix existing pages, made once the store nets $3,500 of profit per month. [L1446.025]
  • Marketing should be outsourced once the store nets $5,000 of profit per month. This is the third stage, after customer service and product upload. [L1446.026]
  • Hiring should follow profit stages rather than the hope of a successful launch that makes many hires possible. Tying each role to monthly net profit keeps the team funded by real earnings. [L1446.028]

Procedure:

  1. Draw the long-term organization of the company as a flowchart or on paper. [L1446.027]
  2. List the roles you want in the future. [L1446.027]
  3. Assign each role a monthly net profit threshold. [L1446.027]
  4. Hire each role only when its threshold is reached, in order. [L1446.027]

Monthly revenue phases (Source C)

Phase Monthly revenue Roles to add Hourly rate mentioned
1 $0-20k Customer service agent(s); fulfillment agent(s) if dropshipping; influencer outreach agent Overseas customer service $2-6/hr; other roles not priced
2 $20-100k Project manager; email marketing (optional); media buyer (optional, first hire recommended in this phase) Project manager $8-12/hr; email via referral or Upwork
3 $100k-500k Supply chain manager; payment processing agent Supply chain manager $6-15/hr; payment processing agent $8-12/hr
4 $500k-1M+ Agencies for social platforms the store does not advertise on Not priced

[L1129.002]

  • Below $20k per month, the only priority is traction. Make only one or two essential hires to free the owner's time for growth. Those hires should take over work that does not need the owner. [L1129.003]
  • The first media buyer should be hired in phase 2 ($20-100k per month), once customer service, influencer outreach and project management are covered. At that point the owner is mainly left with paid ads. [L1129.011]
  • Media buyers usually match their price, so a cheaply hired entry-level buyer will need training time before performing well. Budget that training time or pay for a more experienced media buyer, with Upwork named as a source. [L1129.012]
  • Above $100k per month, new problems appear that the owner did not expect earlier. More specialist roles are needed to keep the store running at that scale. [L1129.013]

Three stages by revenue or profit (Source C)

Build a VA team in three stages matched to revenue:

  • Beginners (roughly $5,000 to $50,000 monthly revenue) keep store building and product descriptions partly in their own hands. [L1432.001]

  • Advanced sellers (roughly $5,000 to $20,000 monthly profit) delegate order fulfillment and customer support first. [L1432.001]

  • Business owners (around $20,000 plus monthly profit) run the business almost fully on autopilot, with a team handling fulfillment, support, live chat, finances and ad management. [L1432.001]

  • Where the thresholds differ: the Source C phases are set by monthly revenue ($20,000 and $100,000 breakpoints), while Source A's thresholds are set by monthly net profit ($2,000, $3,500 and $5,000). Use profit thresholds to decide whether a hire is affordable, and revenue phases to anticipate which roles the business will need. [L1446.024, L1446.025, L1446.026, L1129.002, L1432.001]

Build the team when the need appears

  • Build a team when you realise you need other people. Identify the skills the business needs and search for people who have them. This is one of the first signs of real business growth. [L1438.051]
  • As the business grows, you can build a team by hiring employees or VAs to take over work. In the beginning, the owner does most of the work themselves. [L1480.030]
  • Low-ticket dropshipping at scale requires a large team of VAs and freelancers to handle orders, shipping follow-up and customer service. High-ticket dropshipping needs far fewer orders, and so far fewer staff. [L1481.023]

Recommended hire order

Sequence from the sources

Procedure (Source A), each function scaled before adding complexity:

  1. Start with product listing specialists. Why: they enable unlimited brands, because you manage suppliers. [L0263.006]
  2. Add a supplier relations specialist, who manages supplier outreach and partnerships. [L0263.006]
  3. Add a customer service team, with pre-purchase and post-purchase roles. [L0263.006]
  4. Add an operations manager, who oversees fulfillment and is the biggest team, in the middle of the organization. [L0263.006]
  5. Add a bookkeeper, who tracks costs, reconciles, and finds unprofitable orders. [L0263.006]
  6. Add advertising, content and email specialists. [L0263.006]
  7. Build each store with the same structure. [L0263.006]

Procedure (Source A, 2021, older):

  1. Hire one person to handle support (live chat, phone and email) and order processing. [L0834.102]
  2. Add a second person when inbound volume grows. [L0834.102]
  3. Hire part-time (around 20 hours a month) for social media and website content once repetitive tasks are offloaded. [L0834.102]
  4. Test content yourself first, and hand it off once you know what works. [L0834.102]

Procedure (Source B, 2021-22, older), typical hiring order for a drop shipping operation (costs per month are estimates):

  1. Hire a customer service VA first, at about $1,000, since customer communication usually takes most of the owner's time. [L1334.028]
  2. When one VA is overloaded, split the role into customer service and order processing, the latter at about $600 for order management and supplier coordination. [L1334.028]
  3. Hire a catalog manager at about $1,200 to keep product information, listings and stock current. [L1334.028]
  4. Hire a customer service manager at about $1,500 so the owner no longer answers the team's questions and only hears about resolved problems. [L1334.028]
  5. Once the operation is stable, hire growth roles one at a time: an SEO person at about $800, a PPC person for paid ads, and a content writer for blog posts and emails, all under a marketing manager. [L1334.028, L1334.024]
  6. When the business gets busy, hire a sales rep at about $1,000 and split sales off from customer service. [L1334.028]
  7. Add a product uploader at about $500 for data entry when product volume requires it. [L1334.028]
  8. Add a finance and admin role for bookkeeping, Profit First planning and financial reporting, and outsource tax work to an accountant. [L1334.028]

Procedure (Source C):

  1. Hire first for order fulfillment and customer support. [L1431.016]
  2. Next hire someone to build stores. [L1431.016]
  3. Then hire someone to run ads, because each role needs different skills. [L1431.016]
  • Outsource customer service first, then order fulfillment (if no agent or supplier already handles it), and then agencies that run ads. VAs for fulfillment are not needed when an agent or supplier already does the work. [L0780.020]
  • For a product business, hire a dedicated operations or supply-chain lead before the founders are overloaded. A hire with prior operations experience at a large company can take over supply-chain work the founders were handling themselves. [L0334.061]
  • Hire the first helper quickly, even part-time and for a single task, instead of waiting until the full team is planned. Each task moved off the owner's plate frees time for growth work. [L0337.004]
  • Hire one full-time employee responsible for both customer service (email and chat) and order management (status updates and supplier coordination). [L0230.010]
  • Build an in-house team through a VA agency, and keep order fulfillment in-house as long as it is manageable. [L0492.059]

Case: team build-up timeline

Store team timeline (Source A):

  • 2014: A dropshipping store started on a different model. The owner learned on their own and saw it could not reach financial independence. [L0337.018]
  • 2016: The owner switched to the Source A method, still working a corporate job and handling orders and customer calls in early mornings, nights and breaks. [L0337.018]
  • 2017: A part-time phone helper was hired and paid per answered call. The owner still worked the full-time job and got burnt out. [L0337.018]
  • 2018: A full-time virtual assistant was hired for operations. The owner left the corporate job. [L0337.018]
  • 2020: A team of three, then four (including a US hire with experience and a second US hire). Revenue reached $1.4 million. The team handled operations, customer service, and an outsourced social media, ads and accounting function. [L0337.018]
  • 2021: A fourth virtual assistant was hired. The team handled all day-to-day operations and escalations, so the owner only did sales, some marketing and accounting, from anywhere. [L0337.018]

Affordability check before each hire

  • Do not hire virtual assistants until the store is profitable and you have run it yourself for about a month. [L0941.095]
  • Tie each hire to the profit it can be paid from, and do not hire until the business's own profit pays for it. [L1287.008, L1437.020]

What to hire first

  • Start with the job that takes the most of your time and needs no judgment. In most sources that is customer communication. [L1334.028, L1431.016, L0780.020]
  • When the business needs a skill you do not have, hire for that gap, for example supplier negotiation, customer support quality or store graphics. [L0765.004]
  • Before the first hire, document each process as a playbook, so a new hire can take over tasks with clear instructions. [L0941.095]
  • Hire a VA for order fulfillment and support first, then a person to build stores, then one to run ads, because each role needs different skills. [L1431.016]
  • Test content yourself first and hand it off once you know what works. [L0834.102]
  • Hire the first helper quickly, even part-time, and for a single task. [L0337.004]
  • Hiring for advertising and content roles takes longer. Build your own email marketer internally and train them. [L0263.040]
  • When a media buyer is needed, the first one should come in phase 2 of the revenue stages, once customer service, influencer outreach and project management are covered. [L1129.011]

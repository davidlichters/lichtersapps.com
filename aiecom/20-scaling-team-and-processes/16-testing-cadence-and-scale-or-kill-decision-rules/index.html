20.16 Testing cadence and scale-or-kill decision rules

Launch speed and testing rhythm

  • Speed beats perfection in early product testing. Taking too long to pick a product, build a store or start ads is a common cause of failure, so set a time limit and start running ads quickly. [L0577.060]
  • Testing with more money does not automatically bring success. Wealthy students who test products too fast and spend heavily without a valid strategy become impatient and see no results, so patience and a sound strategy matter more than budget size. [L0577.061]
  • The most common scaling blocker for beginners is launching too slowly, because sales and results only become visible after a product, ad and store are live. Shortening the time from sourcing to launch is the first priority. [L1454.001]
  • An experienced operator can take a product from sourcing through store and ads to a live launch in about one hour. A beginner should aim for no more than one day for the full cycle. [L1454.002]
  • A beginner should be able to launch a new product at least every three days, and the target cadence is about one new launch per day. If cash flow is limited, the target is adjusted to about two launches per day. [L1454.003]
  • The product, the ad campaign and the store should all go live on the same day. Launching them separately delays the first data and slows down testing. [L1454.004]
  • Run a fixed testing system with two to three new product tests per week. Keeping this cadence consistent is what produces winners over time. [L1454.016]
  • Running the full cycle from sourcing a product to a live store and ads, even once, builds a habit that makes the next launches faster. Once the habit is in place, launching one product a day becomes a routine rather than a project. [L1454.024]
  • Speed comes from routine and preparation: a store template, products sourced in advance, and a product page kept ready let you switch products immediately. [L1171.046]
  • Running three to five product campaigns per week at this spend level, and cutting quickly when no winner appears, is a workable test rhythm. Testing more products faster increases the chance of finding a winner. [L1194.019]
  • Testing several products at once means separate campaigns, each with ad groups that hold different videos. Costs add up quickly because each asset needs a minimum of about $20 in spend. [L1194.018]
  • Testing several products at once only makes sense if you have the capacity to maintain quality control. Testing too many products too fast tends to lower the quality of ads and product checks unless you have a team with experience. [L1215.016]
  • Post every finished landing page change in the shared team channel so the mentor and other members can review it. Copy a page template to other products only after it has been reviewed and the add-to-cart ratio has been checked. [L1194.059]
  • Set decision rules in advance. Many beginners launch ads without knowing what to do after launch, such as when to quit and when to scale. [L0363.071]
  • Build a testing routine, because tests give proof for decisions instead of opinions. With a limited budget, put more money into scenarios that already make sales instead of spreading it across untested ideas. [L0644.026]
  • Keep testing when a product is new and when it is proven. Testing is part of scaling, and some ideas will fail, so quickly adjust the product or offer when a test does not work. [L1153.057]
  • Do not reject a hypothesis unless someone writes a full thesis explaining why it should not be tested. Test everything, because unexpected wins happen and the reasoning is obvious only after the fact. [L0067.043]
  • Top-performing brands test and rotate offer structures monthly, switching between formats such as "buy one get one free", "buy two get one free", "buy any two get the third 50% off" and "bundle and save", to identify which drives the highest ROAS and CVR. [L0232.028]
  • Winning audiences and campaigns typically fatigue after two weeks to two months, even when they were consistently profitable, because the creative wears out. Keep a separate testing campaign that tests new creatives and hooks on a weekly or biweekly rhythm, so fresh creative is ready when a winner dies. [L0398.005]
  • If your testing window is short, such as three months, spending most of it learning a new skill leaves little time for testing. Use ready-made creative for early tests and build skills alongside them. [L0341.025]

Testing order and what to test

  • Follow this testing order for each new store:
    1. Build a creative that gets a high CTR. [L0388.041]
    2. Build an offer that turns clicks into sales. [L0388.041]
    3. Find the interest or audience that buys with a high conversion rate. [L0388.041]
    4. Turn on ad spend and scale the winning audience by spending more on it. [L0388.041]
  • Scale by spending more on the audience that converts, since more spend brings more visitors and early sales. Scale an audience only after its data shows it converts at a break-even or better cost per purchase. [L0388.042]
  • Testing should cover audiences, creatives and marketing angles, not only audiences. Ongoing angle testing keeps a winning product from dying out. [L1213.031]
  • Change one variable at a time: creative, audience and offer are separate and each should be judged on its own data. While testing offers, keep running ads unchanged unless they are clearly failing. [L1345.080]
  • Store conversion and ad creative are separate problems and can be optimised in parallel. Fix the offer when the store has about 200 visitors and weak add-to-cart rates, and replace creatives when CTR and CPC are poor. [L1345.081]
  • Funnel data and tracking must be set up and checked before any price or offer test is started, so test results can be trusted. [L1288.013]
  • Marketing and testing checklist order after the store is built:
    1. Define the target audience. [L1288.011]
    2. Produce creatives. [L1288.011]
    3. Set up Facebook ads. [L1288.011]
    4. Set location targeting. [L1288.011]
    5. Set up TikTok ads. [L1288.011]
    6. Set up Google Ads. [L1288.011]
    7. Set up Pinterest ads. [L1288.011]
    8. Set up the funnel data. [L1288.011]
    9. Check tracking. [L1288.011]
    10. Run the price A/B test in UK, Australia and US. [L1288.011]
    11. Run the offer test. [L1288.011]
    12. Set up bundles and upsells. [L1288.011]
    13. Set up email marketing funnels. [L1288.011]
    14. Build customer audiences. [L1288.011]
    15. Build lookalike audiences. [L1288.011]
  • Use a process of elimination for performance drops: change one variable at a time and record the outcome before the next change. [L0950.075]
  • Optimise by data thresholds, not by days alone: check ad spend, CTR and CPC against averages, and check store visitors and conversion rates against benchmarks before deciding. [L1345.097]
  • Test one product at a time before moving to the next. This focuses energy and allows detailed analysis. Once profitability is proven, duplicate the page and product structure for other items, but do not split attention across multiple products during the initial testing phase. [L0321.030]
  • Focus on one dropshipping product at a time per store, so that traffic, ad spend and data all relate to that product. This makes it clearer where to scale and where to stop. [L1347.027]
  • Test one product at a time instead of running several simultaneously, because overlapping tests make results hard to read. Finish one test with clear data before starting the next product. [L1172.036]
  • Testing many products at once with little data gives weak results. Focusing on one product per week lets you learn faster and make clearer decisions. [L1176.042]
  • Under one domain, test products in sequence:
    1. Launch one product on its own product page with ads on the same day. [L1412.050]
    2. Check its performance the next day. [L1412.050]
    3. If it underperforms, create a new product page for another product, which the lesson says takes about 2 hours, and launch it. [L1412.050]
    4. Repeat until a winner is found. [L1412.050]
  • After finding a winning product, either keep testing under the same store and dedicate it to the winner later, or create a one-product store for the winner. Use the approach that keeps the pixel data and ad setup working for the proven product. [L1412.051]
  • The choice between one shared store and separate stores matters little compared with the product itself. Optimise the funnel and the time per test rather than the store structure. [L1412.052]
  • Split products across more campaigns does not speed up learning when the total budget stays the same. A new store should start with one Alpha and one Beta campaign containing all products. Create additional campaigns later to scale proven best sellers with the highest margins, using separate budgets to control spend. [L0837.052]
  • Existing campaigns that work should stay untouched while a new attribution setting runs next to them in a separate campaign. Shift spend only after the new campaign performs at a similar level to the working one. [L1022.019]
  • If a store is already profitable on its current campaign structure, do not switch it off just to adopt a new one. Change structure gradually when results are not where you want them. [L0854.107]
  • Spend a large share of the available budget on testing new ideas only if the core campaigns are proven. For a $1,000 budget, about 10% can go to a test. [L0854.106]
  • Improvements come from continuous testing of plans on top of a campaign that already works, without interrupting the proven plan. Over several years, a series of tested changes can cut cost of sale dramatically. [L1261.027]
  • Testing can answer questions such as product form, pricing, a claim, or whether a rival or agency will perform better, for a small amount of money. Let consumer response decide, instead of internal debates or taste. [L1261.025]

Testing budgets and break-even spend

  • Minimum testing budget: spend at least your break-even cost per purchase (your profit margin). Example: if your product has $10 profit, spend at least $10 per ad set before concluding it does not work. Recommended: 2-3 rounds of testing iterations (for example, $50/day for 3 days, twice = $300 total). [L0067.041]
  • Budget a testing phase of about 2,000-2,500 USD to test 8-10 products. After a winner is found, add about 500-2,000 USD per month to scale slowly. [L0363.068]
  • Start new ad tests with a small budget (for example about 100 to 200 dollars per product) and move on if the product does not work. Scale after the product has proven itself. [L0577.062]
  • Launch new paid campaigns at a low starting daily budget, around $10 to $20/day, and judge them against a clear ROAS target of more than 10x before scaling spend. [L1509.013]
  • Start with a small budget, collect data, and then scale up or down based on results. Never begin with a very large budget before the data shows what works. [L0551.059] (older, 2020)
  • Accept a deliberate budget for experimentation that burns cash on tests, and manage it at a monthly or product-group level rather than by day. This keeps daily swings from driving decisions. [L0719.041] (older, 2021-2022)
  • Test a new product on Facebook with about $200 to $300 of spend across 10 to 15 audiences, usually $200 if signs are very poor. Review the data every five to six hours during the test. [L1213.037]
  • On TikTok, test seven to ten creatives for a product before switching to the next product if it does not work. [L1213.037]
  • A small test campaign of this kind often costs about $3,000 to $5,000, and much of its spend is recovered through sales even when the product proves unpopular. Such a test is a far smaller risk than launching a full campaign on opinion. [L1261.024]
  • Consumer test campaigns of about $1,000 each helped one advertiser avoid a costly product change and find a new form that consumers preferred by a large margin. Testing before launch can prevent expensive mistakes and reveal winners. [L1261.026]
  • Over five years, one advertiser tried more than fifty plans and eventually found one that cut the cost of selling by 75 percent, making it four times more effective than the previous best. Systematic testing turned small improvements into a large cost advantage. [L1261.028]

Minimum data before scaling

  • Do not scale an ad until it has produced consistent results: at least about six sales at a break-even or full ROAS. Do not raise the budget, duplicate or change the ad set after one or two sales, because early results may be luck. [L1315.020]
  • Before scaling aggressively, have at least three to four weeks of consistent sales, data and profitability. Scaling without this base risks running out of cash. [L1213.028]
  • Check the store's profitability and ad data for the last four days, then the same data for the last seven days. If either period shows inconsistency, do not scale; keep testing and try new creatives. If both the last four days and the last seven days are consistently profitable, launch the new campaign or ad set at a much higher budget. [L1213.029]
  • Scale a campaign when the test over 5 to 7 days shows all metrics within target: good CPM, cost per click and cost per purchase, ROAS well above break-even, and a healthy website funnel. Do not scale on incomplete data. [L1354.021]
  • When analysing ad performance for decisions, take a window of the last 4 days rather than the last day only, or 7 days if not confident, to avoid reacting to daily noise. [L1335.038]
  • Scale a campaign only after it has shown a CPA below break-even over several days, and let the trend confirm the result first. The example scaled a campaign that had turned profitable, with daily revenue around $1,000. [L1124.015]
  • Only increase budget after profit and loss results are consistent over several days. If metrics swing, cut or pause the test rather than scaling. [L1175.034]
  • Analyse the data together before scaling, check the break-even point, and start the scaling process only once you have a product that clearly wins. [L1176.041]
  • After a product gets good ad metrics, follow this decision procedure:
    1. Confirm the Meta ads show a CTR above 2% and a CPC below $1. [L1172.035]
    2. If clicks do not turn into sales, improve the product page: test prices, bundles, store layout, copy and a different marketing angle. [L1172.035]
    3. Once sales start and the conversion rate is good, test price changes to raise margin while keeping the conversion rate above 1.5%. [L1172.035]
    4. Keep testing different interests until one interest produces more than six sales. [L1172.035]
    5. After reaching this threshold, contact support for help with scaling each week. [L1172.035]
  • Once a winning offer and product are found, scaling is fast, often within days. Spend the effort on finding what works rather than on the scaling step. [L1469.022]
  • Do not scale ads aggressively at first. Identify the audiences and creatives that work, combine them, and only then scale from those proven assets. [L1469.010]
  • Scaling a product follows a sales ladder: first $1,000, $2,000 and $3,000 in sales to confirm potential, then $10,000 and $100,000. Invest more only once each level shows the product has potential. [L1171.045]
  • Do not kill a product based on spend without sales before the landing page is checked, because the page, offer, creative or product can each be the cause. Fix the controllable parts first. [L1436.052]

Profit targets and ROAS thresholds

  • Start with a modest profit target, such as 10%, when a product is new, then raise it toward 20% once the product is proven. Each profit target produces its own desired ROAS, so the threshold for aggressive scaling moves with it. [L0445.026]
  • Start with a profit target of about 10% when the first ads are tested. Once the store is consistently profitable, raise the target step by step, for example to 15% and later to 20%, and recalculate the desired ROAS each time. [L0875.031]
  • Scale aggressively only when the measured ROAS exceeds the desired ROAS for the profit target. If the campaign is below that value, keep the spend at the same level and test new things instead of increasing budget. [L0875.010]
  • Example: if the break-even ROAS is 1.6 and the goal is 20% profit, a ROAS of about 2.0 is needed. A campaign reaching ROAS above 2.0 may be scaled up, while a campaign that only earns around 5% profit should not be scaled aggressively. [L0875.011]
  • Minimum ROAS before scaling is around 2.5 (one operator's threshold is 2.53). Set this threshold against your own break-even ROAS. Scaling has not been successful for the speaker unless the ad had at least the full ROAS. [L1315.021]
  • Increase ad spend only while the return on ad spend stays at least 10x. If performance drops below that, first try on-site optimisation, and if that fails, keep the budget at the highest level where 10x still holds. [L1287.007]
  • A standard rule for scaling ads: keep raising ad spend only while return on ad spend stays at or above 10x. Once it drops below that, optimise the store first and, if that fails, hold budget at the highest level where 10x still holds. [L1287.068]
  • Products that justify large ad budgets have a strong 2X MAP to wholesale spread and good conversion. Once they are profitable, ad spend can be increased on them as far as profit allows. [L0966.088]
  • Note on thresholds: the units give different ROAS levels: about 2.0 to 2.5 for a 20% profit target (Source C, [L0875.011, L1315.021]) and more than or at least 10x (Source A, [L1509.013, L1287.007, L1287.068]). The units do not state the margin behind the 10x figure, so apply each threshold only to the business its source describes and recalculate break-even ROAS from your own margin. [L0875.011, L1315.021, L1509.013, L1287.007, L1287.068]

Scaling step sizes and budget increases

  • Increase ad spend by no more than 2x per step and never above 3x, because larger jumps can break the campaign. Moving from 100 to 300 to 900 USD in consecutive days is a failing example. [L0363.066]
  • Increase ad spend gradually rather than jumping several times over in a single day. For example, a $30 daily budget should not become $150 overnight. [L0934.084, L0934.116]
  • Required spend to reach a target revenue = current daily cost × (target revenue / current revenue), where daily cost is ad spend plus fulfilment cost per day. Example: 100 USD ad spend plus 100 USD fulfilment gives 200 USD cost and 400 USD revenue, so 800 USD revenue requires 400 USD cost. [L0363.067]
  • Scale ad spend gradually: start with a small budget, spend more the next month only if sales were profitable, and keep increasing while the ROAS minimum holds. Avoid suddenly quadrupling the budget after a few good months. [L0808.036]
  • Scaling ad spend once a store is profitable:
    1. Increase the budget gradually, e.g. from $2,500 to $3,000 per month, then $3,500, then $5,000. [L0854.103] (older, 2022)
    2. Keep each new spend level only if the return stays profitable. [L0854.103] (older, 2022)
    3. Work toward larger budgets step by step rather than doubling at once. [L0854.103] (older, 2022)
  • A common growth barrier is reluctance to spend large amounts on ads. Profitable stores should keep raising spend in steps to match their growth. [L0854.104] (older, 2022)
  • Growing to large monthly revenue usually requires substantial ad budgets, which feel frightening at first. Scale spending on campaigns that the data confirms works rather than spending blindly. [L0469.108] (older, 2021)
  • The most sensible scaling approach is to start small, find what works, scale what works, cut what does not, and keep repeating this cycle. [L0970.152]
  • Scaling traffic is not linear. A small lift in what you can pay per subscriber can unlock a much larger increase in volume, because competitors stop bidding at their limits while you can continue. [L0378.093]
  • Example: a business getting 1,000 subscribers per day at $1 each could spend $1.25 per subscriber if they were worth that much by day 14. The 25% increase in spend might add far more than 25% in volume, and at a $2 spend limit volume could rise to about 5,000 subscribers per day. Revenue scaled from about $1,000 to $10,000 per day with only a doubling of value per subscriber. [L0378.094]
  • The traffic cliff is the point where you can outspend competitors because your economics support higher acquisition costs. Reaching it requires knowing your customer value precisely. [L0378.095]
  • A higher AOV lets you spend less on ads per customer while keeping the same revenue per order, which makes scaling more profitable. Post-purchase upsells raise AOV without buying more traffic. [L1141.025]
  • A single visitor can buy three to six units in one session when the funnel stacks add-ons, which multiplies order value without extra traffic. Stacked bundle offers are the main way to reach this. [L1141.026]

Learning phase: keep hands off

  • After launching traffic campaigns, do not constantly adjust settings, do not panic or overreact to early performance data, and do not restart or duplicate campaigns, because this disrupts Google's optimisation. [L0168.006]
  • After launch, monitor for three specific issues only: campaign disapprovals, account warnings and technical errors. Escalate these to support, but do not adjust other metrics during the learning phase. [L0168.007]
  • When a store has a long ad-time budget, leave it running. Sales from paid traffic should appear within 1 to 3 days of the click, so a long wait without sales signals a problem to fix. [L0964.148]
  • If an ad is making sales but is not yet profitable, let it run until it either becomes consistently profitable or falls below break-even ROAS, then switch it off. Do not switch it off while it still performs well, and do not change the budget during the learning phase. [L1315.023]
  • Do not customise a product whose original profit fluctuates a lot. Keep the original running at the same budget and treat the early phase as a learning phase. [L1030.009]

Optimisation rules for scaling campaigns

  • Optimisation rules, applied to yesterday's ROAS and the last 4 days (use 7 days if not confident):
    1. If yesterday's ROAS is above benchmark ROAS, increase the budget by 20%. [L1335.036]
    2. If yesterday's ROAS is roughly equal to break-even ROAS, make no change and monitor closely. [L1335.036]
    3. If the ROAS over the last 3 days plus today equals break-even ROAS, keep the same budget; if it is slightly below break-even, reduce the budget by 20%. [L1335.036]
    4. If the ROAS over the last 3 days plus today is below break-even ROAS, turn the ad set off (kill the loser). [L1335.036]
    5. If a campaign is unprofitable, check both ad-set and ad level; if one ad takes most of the spend (for example 90%) without a sale, remove that ad. [L1335.036]
    6. Consistently underperforming ad sets or creatives should be removed. [L1335.036]
  • Budget example: an ad set at $50 with a 4-day ROAS of 1.4 and break-even ROAS of 1.5 is slightly below break-even, so its budget is reduced by 20% to $40. [L1335.037]
  • Test one ad group at a time: Scale only from proven assets, not from unproven combinations. [L1469.010]

Kill criteria

  • Product kill decision: if you have spent your break-even cost per purchase and metrics are not promising (poor attention, poor interest, poor conversion rate despite good attention), stop testing and move to the next product. Do not burn budget on products with no signal. [L0067.042]
  • Kill the product if results remain poor after the spend and audience test on Facebook. [L1213.037]
  • Cutting a product after roughly one month without clear data, because results seem insufficient, makes it hard to know whether the product was really working. Decide on a product using its data rather than short-term impressions. [L1347.036]
  • Decide when to cut on day 2 or day 3 by judgement: when soft metrics are weak early in the test and the product cannot realistically become a brand, cut it. The choice depends on the product and the operator's conviction. [L1030.014]
  • Cut a product that shows thin margins and repeated red days without a consistent signal. When margins are very lean, one more red day can be enough to stop it. [L1030.015]
  • Keep a product only while it has a clear chance to become a brand. Holding a mediocre product ties up budget that could test another product, so move on when opportunity cost outweighs the upside. [L1030.013]
  • A product built for a quick pump-and-dump with no brand potential should be dropped rather than customised. [L1030.016]
  • Reallocate ad budget across a supplier list with uneven margins:
    1. Cut ad spend on products and suppliers where the margin is too thin or no sales happen. [L1069.073] (older, 2019)
    2. Move the budget to the products and suppliers where margins are healthy. [L1069.073] (older, 2019)
    3. Return to the low-margin suppliers and negotiate better pricing before adding more ad spend to them. [L1069.073] (older, 2019)
  • Decide with the data for each product and do not kill a product on a single weak signal. [L1213.037]

Product customisation gates

  • Test a book product first with an unchanged or minimally changed cover and content, then look for proof of validation, meaning real customers buying the book, before investing in full branding. Only build the full custom version once that proof exists. [L0333.015]
  • Customising a book's cover and contents before knowing that it sells wastes time and possibly money if the product does not work. Test the product first. [L0333.014]
  • A "pump and dump" approach uses fast tests with minimal customisation to check many products quickly. It suits speed-focused testing but skips the brand-building that comes after validation. [L0333.016]
  • Once a brand consistently makes over $1k per day in profit, the owner can consider custom ads and custom covers as the next stage. Before that, focus on validating the concept. [L0351.047]
  • Once a product is profitable, refine it rather than changing products. Mentors suggested focusing on one profitable product before testing more products. [L0577.063]
  • Proof-of-concept gate before custom production: the product must hold a profit margin of US$75-100 per day consistently for 3-5 days. [L1019.046]
  • Do not build the full testing, scaling and graveyard structure before the proof-of-concept gate is passed; save the structure for later. [L1019.048]
  • The minimum signal to consider customising is consistent profit of about $100 or more per day over three days. Below that, customising wastes time and money on video credits. [L1030.010]
  • A product that makes about $200 profit per day on the same budget for three to four consecutive days is a candidate for customisation. In the example, customisation started on day 8, after the profit held on the following days. [L1030.011]
  • Customisation workflow for a proven product, in order:
    1. Keep the original product running in its CBO campaign. [L1030.012]
    2. Around day 5 or 6 (or day 8 if the $200 pattern applies), duplicate the CBO campaign. [L1030.012]
    3. Upload custom UGC ads to the duplicate. [L1030.012]
    4. Create a second product on the same store landing page with a custom cover photo, a different title and its own link. [L1030.012]
    5. Duplicate the campaign again and point it at the custom-cover product, with its own custom UGC ad. [L1030.012]
    6. Run the custom version together with the original until the custom version shows consistent profit. [L1030.012]
    7. Turn off the original product's ads once the custom version is clearly profitable. [L1030.012]

Diagnosing bottlenecks

  • Identify the bottleneck by checking the funnel in order and reading the colour of each metric. If the Meta side is red, fix ads; if the website side is red while Meta is green, fix the website; if both are red, fix both; a healthy Meta side with a healthy website means scale. [L1354.026]
  • Troubleshooting a store with lots of traffic and few conversions:
    1. Cut outsourced traffic if it produces no sales. [L0664.065] (older, 2020)
    2. Reduce Facebook and Google budgets to about $5 per day. [L0664.065] (older, 2020)
    3. Pause Bing ads until Google Shopping is profitable. [L0664.065] (older, 2020)
    4. Focus on making Google Shopping profitable. [L0664.065] (older, 2020)
    5. Check the store look, traffic source, product pages and checkout process. [L0664.065] (older, 2020)
  • When CPA is higher than target while the campaign is still profitable, the funnel has room to improve. The levers are the ads, the average order value and the landing page, not only the budget. [L0875.012]
  • Optimise in this order once products are live: images, discount display, ETA, reviews, and tabbed pages. Feature-to-benefit rewriting and extra images follow as soon as possible. [L1130.032]
  • If revenue stays flat, first check how much of the existing audience is used. Look at email database revenue per contact and remarketing audiences before adding new traffic. [L0854.105] (older, 2022)
  • Use a test-and-measure approach for performance drops: change one variable at a time and record the outcome before the next change. [L0950.075]

Further details

  1. Pick four or five representative towns or market segments for the test.
  2. Start users quickly with a sample offer or a free package that carries your full claims.
  3. Track whether the people who get the sample buy, how often they reorder, and how much they spend.
  4. Calculate how long it takes for the profit from those buyers to repay the cost of selling to them.
  5. Only expand to national or wider campaigns after the figures confirm the cost, sale and payback are acceptable. [L1261.023]

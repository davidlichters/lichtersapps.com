20.3 Documenting processes: SOPs, checklists and training videos

Why document before you delegate

  • Build documented business systems and processes before you need to delegate. This lets you hire without disrupting the business and gives you a repeatable roadmap for onboarding [L0230.002].
  • A documented business system provides three benefits: a smooth transition when delegating, less stress for the owner, and higher business value [L0230.019].
  • Create a video of every process from the very beginning, even before hiring anyone, because someone else will eventually need the knowledge. A business where all knowledge sits in the owner's head ends when the owner is away, sick or unavailable [L0337.007].
  • Running a business with all processes stored in the owner's head makes it impossible to hand over work. Write down processes and build a system before hiring anyone [L1000.046].
  • Do not wait for a major life event to force you to build systems. Start documenting while you still run the operation yourself, so a hire can take over when revenue allows [L1473.032].
  • When an ecommerce brand grows quickly, operations tend to become unstable, data ends up spread across disconnected systems, and priorities lose clarity. Build standardized systems and processes during growth so that scale stays sustainable [L0828.001].
  • Outsourcing requires standard operating procedures and training. This feels like a step backwards at first but frees time later; expect several months of stress before the benefit appears. (Older advice, 2021.) [L0834.103]
  • Staff who are still learning need documented processes more than experienced staff do. Without them they miss steps that are routine for the owner. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0949.038]

What to document first

  • Document only processes that already make money. Scaling an unprofitable task adds inefficiency and bloats a small business [L1359.004].
  • Confirm that the process is profitable and document it before you hire anyone for it. Hiring for an unproven process means paying someone to learn something you have not yet proven works [L1359.010].
  • Start with the repetitive, time-consuming tasks that eat your hours [L1359.006].
  • Do customer service and daily operations yourself at the start, even if it means very long weeks, so you can write down how the store works. Outsource only after you have documented processes to hand over. (Older advice, 2019.) [L0467.008]
  • Learn every task yourself before delegating it, including customer reply templates, comment moderation and assigning a page moderator. Then record your screen with a tool like Loom and give the videos to new hires for training [L1431.022].
  • Answer customer questions yourself for the first period of the business [L1287.009].
  • Systemize repeatable tasks so they can be done efficiently and consistently, reverse engineered, outsourced and automated. Systemizing is the basis for scaling the business to the next level [L1043.008].
  • Each supplier should have its own project and documentation, because a team member working on one supplier's uploads will not know the workflow of another supplier [L0359.102].
  • Pair every launch step with a downloadable checklist and a ready-to-use template. A defined output format lets the operator complete the step by following the list instead of working out the method each time, which reduces delays [L0555.009].
  • Build a standard operating procedure for repeatable tasks that are not business specific, such as SEO, Instagram or Facebook work. Share and store these procedures so the team can reuse them. (Older advice, 2021.) [L0801.157]
  • Write standard operating procedures for every recurring task you hand over, so team members know exactly how to do the work. Templates and SOPs can be reused, because most businesses repeat the same tasks [L0413.013].

SOP definition and principles

  • A standard operating procedure (SOP) is a written workflow or manual that tells a person how to handle a specific situation. Its purpose is to make sure tasks always run the same way and as efficiently as possible [L1340.001].
  • A SOP is a written step-by-step list for a recurring task. Create SOPs for everyday tasks as the business grows, so the work can be handed to a team member without one-on-one training [L0359.100].
  • Written SOPs let any new person do the same job the same way, whether a long-term employee, a newly hired stranger or a first-time virtual assistant. A new person can read the SOP and manage situations without prior training from the owner [L1340.002].
  • Write SOPs simpler than seems necessary. What is obvious to the owner after many repetitions can confuse a new person. Visual steps help people who read the language less fluently [L1359.009].
  • Break long processes into short chunks so a team member can finish one part in a sitting and know exactly where to resume. A process that must be finished in one go is harder to delegate [L1359.005].
  • Keep SOPs light on words and heavy on screenshots or video. A five-minute task should not need a ten-page document. (Older advice, 2020.) [L1088.071]
  • Writing an SOP for a task you have never performed yourself leads to wrong time estimates and missing steps. Someone else then fails at the task or takes far longer than expected [L1359.007].

Writing an SOP: the procedures

Steps to build an SOP:

  1. Confirm that the task makes money before you document it [L1359.006].
  2. Do the task yourself at least a couple of times, and time it, so you know how long it really takes and that the steps work [L1359.006].
  3. Start with the repetitive, time-consuming tasks that eat your hours [L1359.006].
  4. Write the steps at an entry-level, almost basic level, as if a fast-food employee had to follow them [L1359.006].
  5. Split complex tasks into small chunks, and when a sub-task is complicated, link it to its own separate process [L1359.006].
  6. Add screenshots or short videos to each step, especially for team members who are not native speakers [L1359.006].
  7. Test the SOP with a peer or another entrepreneur who does not know your business, and fix every step they cannot follow [L1359.006].

Systemizing a repeatable task:

  1. Look for inefficiency, especially in tasks that repeat often [L1043.009].
  2. List what currently works for each task and what wastes time or effort [L1043.009].
  3. Combine the best elements into one written procedure for the task, which can then be handed to others or automated [L1043.009].

Writing a simple SOP with Loom (older advice, 2020):

  1. Perform the task once while recording it with Loom [L1088.070].
  2. Write a short bullet list of the steps, using few words [L1088.070].
  3. Embed the recording next to each step, so the video shows exactly how each step is done [L1088.070].
  4. Keep all SOPs in a shared Google Drive so the team can access and edit them in real time [L1088.070].

Documenting a repeatable process before handing it over:

  1. Write down how the task is done and the rules that apply [L1122.022].
  2. Prefer a screen recording of you completing the task, for example from order collection in Shopify through the supplier order and fulfilment [L1122.022].
  3. Store the documentation so new hires can use it, and it will also ease a future business handover to a buyer [L1122.022].

SOP format: text, video and checklist (older advice, 2021-22, unless stated)

  • Video walkthroughs of the task performed on screen, with narration, are often the easiest SOP format because the person hears and sees each step. Not every task suits video, though. Many operators provide both a video and a text document for the same SOP, since people learn in different ways [L1339.004].
  • Text SOPs become easier to follow when screenshots of the relevant screen are added, so the person can confirm they are in the right place [L1339.005].
  • Record the SOP as a screen share, with voice and screen but no need for your face on camera. Add screenshots or video to each step [L0263.019, L1340.005].
  • Structure every SOP in this order [L1339.006]:
    1. Use text or video as required.
    2. Always start by explaining the purpose of the task.
    3. Break the task into a step-by-step sequence.
    4. Finish the SOP with a section on what NOT to do (no-nos or reminders).
    5. Create a single-page checklist for the SOP.
  • The purpose section should explain what the task helps the business achieve and how it supports the wider business. For example, uploading products to a Shopify store widens the product range, which engages more customers and increases revenue and profit. Knowing their role fosters a sense of inclusion, which improves engagement and the quality of work [L1339.007].
  • Break the task into as many specific numbered steps as possible (Step 1, Step 2, etc.). In a video, state each step and demonstrate it. In a text document, outline each step with its details and add screenshots where a particular page or screen needs to be recognised [L1339.008].
  • Example: for a Shopify product upload SOP, Step 1 is to copy the product title into the title field, Step 2 is to paste the product description into the description field, and further steps cover every other product data field. Each step is one concrete action, so the person can follow it without guessing [L1339.018].
  • A no-nos section lists what the person must not do, such as copying content from other sites. Specifying prohibited actions is as important as the steps, because it removes doubt about expectations and clarifies whether a contractor should be kept if they repeat a forbidden action. If someone repeats an action after being told not to do it, that is grounds for immediate dismissal [L1339.009].
  • Example: for a task where a contractor writes unique product descriptions with research, copied content from another site is a clear no-no, and product descriptions must contain no duplicated content [L1339.010].
  • Every SOP must end with a checklist that fits on a single page. The checklist is a quick reference the person ticks off after completing the task, while the full SOP is used for learning rather than for memory checks [L1339.011].
  • Checklists reduce errors in industries that use them, as shown in aviation, where accidents fell after pilots began running through pre-flight checklists. Checklists reduce time spent fixing and checking errors, and lower the need for later performance management of staff [L1339.012].
  • The book The Checklist Manifesto (a short book of a few hundred pages, available from major bookstores and Amazon) covers why checklists support business processes, safety and quality [L1339.013].
  • Keep every SOP simple by starting from one minimal template that already contains all required parts: purpose, numbered steps, a no-nos section and a single-page checklist. Adapt the template for each new task instead of designing a new layout each time [L1339.017].
  • Record screencasts of recurring tasks without using the employee's name, so the same video can train every future hire, including replacements [L1515.028].

Templates

  • SOP templates are Word documents that can be opened in Microsoft Word, Apple Pages or Google Drive. They are meant to be edited so they fit your own business [L1340.003].
  • SOP templates are a starting point, not a final document. Adapt each template to your own products, suppliers and policies before your team uses it [L1340.004].
  • The seven SOP templates available in the set [L1340.007]:
    1. Customer Service SOP
    2. Order Management SOP
    3. Virtual Assistant Interview Template
    4. Hiring Questions
    5. Supplier Procedures SOP
    6. Website Style Guide SOP
    7. Shipping and Return Policy SOP
  • Procedure for adopting the SOP templates [L1340.005]:
    1. Download all SOP templates.
    2. Open them on your computer and read through them.
    3. Look at the processes described and identify what you actually use.
    4. Modify each template so it fits your business better.
    5. Save all finished SOPs in your own Google Drive account.
    6. When you hire someone, share a Google Drive link with them instead of sending a file.
  • A user guide, meaning a written instruction manual for the business, is the core document for outsourcing. It should describe each recurring process in enough detail that a new person can follow it without the owner present [L0592.009].
  • Pre-built templates for the user guide let the owner plug in their own business information instead of writing each document from scratch. Using them speeds up documenting processes before delegation [L0592.010].
  • Write quotes and emails from fixed parts. Keep the same opening and closing sentences in every template and only edit the middle for each customer. This removes the need to write each message from scratch and keeps all messages consistent even when different people send them. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L1396.010]
  • Create a quote template in Excel or Google Sheets with your logo, company colours, the date, the company email, the customer name and contact details, and fields for item number and price. Store the file in the PDF or templates section so every team member uses the same version. Staff then only replace the item and price fields instead of building the quote from scratch. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L1396.011]
  • Build a pre-built template for each new product page and reuse it. Once one product page works, reuse the same template and checklist for the next product [L1188.030].

Storage and sharing

  • Store all SOPs in Google Drive, using separate folders per work area (for example Shopify product uploads with attachments, website design, content creation and posting). Share a folder with a freelancer or VA to give access; updates reach them automatically without resending files. Dropbox or another online file storage works equally well, and originals should also be kept on a hard drive or backup storage. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L1339.003]
  • Store your SOPs in your own Google Drive and share the link with each new team member. Do not send downloaded files, because the team then works from one central, consistent version [L1340.006].
  • A common mistake is to send SOP files directly to new team members, which creates several copies that drift apart over time. Sharing one Google Drive link keeps every team member on the same current version [L1340.008].
  • Creating and storing SOPs:
    1. Write each SOP down or record it as a screen share [L1000.060].
    2. Store the SOP in Google Drive or on YouTube [L1000.060].
    3. Add a link to each SOP in the shared business spreadsheet, so staff can find it by clicking [L1000.060].
  • Storage location: Google Drive is the default in the Source A sources. YouTube is the other option named for stored SOP videos [L1000.048, L1000.060, L1340.006]. Dropbox is named as an equal alternative in the Source B source (older advice, 2021-22) [L1339.003].
  • Create a shared resource board for guidelines, logos, photography and key information in SharePoint or Google Drive. Set it up as a self-service repository so team members find materials without requesting them from the creative team [L0260.017].
  • Share all strategy documents (brand, marketing, content, influencer and so on) in a central accessible location so any team member can reference company direction at any time [L0260.021].
  • Keep one master folder per product with subfolders for thumbnails, videos, review screenshots and brand assets, and use a consistent naming pattern. This makes it fast for a team member to rebuild a product page or ad without searching the whole computer [L1188.029].
  • Store important information in more than one place, such as the price list appearing in both the company information and the quotes section, because people search for things in different ways. Attach documents like price lists directly to the relevant card in Trello. Update every copy whenever a manufacturer sends a new price list. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L1396.006]

Data standards

  • Standardize controlled lists in SOPs, for example approved product tags. Create an official bucket of tags that all team members must use, with no ad-hoc variations. The manager approves additions only. This ensures data consistency, enabling collections, filters and reporting across products [L0263.020].
  • Create a live SOP file that team members can contribute to with comments and suggestions. If someone discovers a faster method, they add it to the SOP and the manager approves. SOPs then improve over time and everyone invests in them [L0263.019].

Process repository and team questions

Answering recurring team questions through a process repository:

  1. Whenever a team member asks how to do something, record the answer as a short screen video (or article) instead of explaining it again [L0337.009].
  2. Save it in the shared repository and send the link to the team member [L0337.009].
  3. When a team member asks about a process that is not in the repository, check first whether it exists [L0337.009].
  4. If it does not exist, the team member records the process and adds it to the repository, then reports that the new entry is there [L0337.009].

Staff who handle customer service need the same knowledge base. Written standard procedures for returns, support, uploads and ads reduce errors as the team grows. (Older advice, 2020.) [L1111.164]

Customer service playbook

Building a playbook from customer questions:

  1. Answer customer questions yourself for the first period of the business [L1287.009].
  2. Record every recurring question and its approved answer in one document [L1287.009].
  3. Turn that document into a playbook that a future hire or assistant follows when responding to customers [L1287.009].
  4. Update the playbook as new question types appear [L1287.009].

Onboarding and training

  • Onboard new team members through a video library that covers company goals, the vision and all core processes, so newcomers reach working speed without the owner. The library also makes it easier to sell the business later, since documented processes are expected by buyers [L0337.010].

  • Create a training video for a complex recurring task such as product uploading [L1441.020]:

    1. Record a narrated screencast of about 30 minutes that shows exactly how to upload the product, including the tags, description format, removal of files and linking [L1441.020].
    2. Have the virtual assistant watch the video at half speed [L1441.020].
    3. Have the assistant repeat the task for each product [L1441.020].

    This approach saves hundreds of hours over time [L1441.020].

  • Before a new hire starts, record a sample upload of about 5 to 10 products and send it to them, so they can see the correct process [L0971.008].

  • Give a VA or freelancer both a written SOP and a video walkthrough of each task. Record the video after running through the task once. The written SOP is the quick reference for later use, because it is faster to navigate than a long video. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0945.007]

  • A virtual assistant can book shipping by following written step-by-step instructions for the booking process [L1291.015].

  • Assign the product page SEO checklist to whoever creates or uploads product pages, and have them run through it during the product data upload. Team members can also work through it as a learning tool when onboarding someone new. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L1191.024]

Hiring and outsourcing

Hiring a Business Process Outsourcing freelancer to build documentation when the owner is overloaded (older advice, 2019) [L0467.007]:

  1. Post a job on Upwork for a BPO specialist, not a day-to-day virtual assistant [L0467.007].
  2. Choose a candidate whose Upwork profile shows experience writing SOPs (standard operating procedures) [L0467.007].
  3. Have them do the tasks and document each process as they go, so the owner spends only a few hours a week with them [L0467.007].
  4. Once the documentation exists, bring on a customer service VA and hand over the documents [L0467.007].

A BPO freelancer costs more than a customer service VA, but the Philippines offers strong workers at good prices (older advice, 2019) [L0467.008].

  • Write standard operating procedures before hiring a virtual assistant so they know how to run the business. Hire someone who has held jobs at other companies for long periods, since short tenures often signal instability (older advice, 2021) [L0853.068].
  • Write the SOP before posting a job advertisement, not after hiring someone. Documenting the task first clarifies what to ask candidates in the application and interview stages and avoids pressure during onboarding (older advice, 2021-22) [L1339.002].
  • Create a standard operating procedure for every task you delegate, regardless of how small or simple it seems. For large tasks, several SOPs covering individual components can be useful, but every outsourced task needs at least one (older advice, 2021-22) [L1339.001].
  • Every SOP should be adapted to the specific business before it is handed to a VA. Add the business's own preferred methods, storage locations for files and any other specifics, and update the SOP whenever the business changes (older advice, 2021-22) [L0945.006].
  • Link building outsourcing SOPs should be written first for the Skyscraper method, since it is the most time-consuming and most complicated link building approach. Its tasks are the priority for documentation (older advice, 2021-22) [L0945.008].
  • Learn as you go and document each step, so that whoever runs the operation later can understand how every supplier works without asking the owner (older advice, 2020) [L0468.172].
  • Build the business so that it can be handed over. Create a video of every process from the start, and keep the team's SOPs current [L0337.007, L1122.022].

Systemizing and continuous improvement

  • Build repeatable systems for every recurring task, because systems maximize efficiency and produce consistent results. A good system can be handed to an employee or virtual assistant with predictable output [L1039.016].
  • Use the same proven selection criteria every time you choose a product, and the same repeatable process every time you build traffic, instead of starting from scratch each time. This makes results predictable across stores [L1039.017].
  • Study competitors who already succeed in your market and reverse-engineer their systems to see what works. Then replicate it and improve on it rather than guessing [L1039.018].
  • A system should work like a recipe: anyone who follows it exactly should reach the same result. This allows you to outsource or automate tasks without wondering whether they are being done correctly [L1039.019].
  • If you cannot describe a task as a clear step-by-step process, you do not understand it well enough to delegate or repeat it. Document every routine as you go, including tasks like uploading a product or researching suppliers [L1039.020].
  • Look for inefficiencies in repetitive tasks and improve them, since a system should be the most direct route to the result. Keep what works and build it into the process for your future employees [L1039.022].
  • Build systems as an ongoing habit rather than a one-time to-do list, and keep refining them as the business changes. Treating process improvement as part of your normal way of working makes it continuous [L1039.035].
  • Build a standard operating procedure for repeatable tasks that are not business specific, and share and store them so the team can reuse them (older advice, 2021) [L0801.157].

Mistakes to avoid

  • Running a business with all processes stored in the owner's head makes it impossible to hand over work. Write down processes and build a system before hiring anyone [L1000.046].
  • Do not wait for a major life event to force you to build systems. Start documenting and building the operation while you still run it yourself [L1473.032].
  • Writing an SOP for a task you have never performed yourself leads to wrong time estimates and missing steps [L1359.007].
  • Sending SOP files directly to new team members creates copies that drift apart. Use one shared Google Drive link [L1340.008].
  • Hiring for an unproven process means paying someone to learn something you have not yet proven works [L1359.010].

20.2 Owner time, task audit and what to delegate

Why owner time is the scarce resource

  • A business that depends on the owner's daily involvement stops when the owner is unavailable. The goal is a business that keeps running without the owner in day-to-day operations, which is the main reason to document and delegate early. [L0337.001]
  • Build systems so the business keeps running when the owner is not working in it. A business that depends on constant personal effort does not scale. [L0631.007]
  • Scale by building backend systems for customer retention, so the business keeps running without the owner doing daily work. [L0914.016]
  • Older advice (2021-22): Scaling is easier and more fulfilling than remaining solo, because the owner can focus on work they enjoy rather than handling every task, including those they dislike. [L0107.006]
  • Older advice (2021-22): To perform higher-value work, you must build a team. Scaling emerges naturally when you focus on meaningful work rather than pursuing it as a direct goal. [L0107.004]
  • Running the business alone is possible for years. Outsourcing repetitive daily tasks is a lifestyle choice rather than a requirement. [L0968.102]
  • Build the business as a lifestyle business. A single store can run part-time with about 15 to 20 minutes of work per day, and full-time work on one store suggests tasks should be outsourced. [L1111.162]
  • Recurring daily tasks are what consume the owner's time, so they are the first candidates for automation or delegation. The owner's time is better spent on growth, finding new opportunities and other priorities. [L0592.003]
  • Busy work that does not contribute to results should be removed from the business, not just performed more efficiently. Eliminating it is one of the fastest ways to stand out from people who have struggled for years. [L1039.021]
  • Growth stalls when the owner keeps senior-level tasks that are not worth their time or energy. Sustainable growth needs a team of people who outperform the owner in specific areas of the business. [L1129.001]
  • Income grows through leverage, which comes from working less in the business, not working longer hours. Leverage is built by hiring staff and learning to manage them. A business earning around 250,000 dollars a year cannot grow if the owner does every task, so delegation frees time for new ideas and new businesses. [L1513.010]
  • Parkinson's law says work expands to fill the time available for it. Owners who work 40 to 60 hours a week can test this by deliberately cutting their working time, for example to 10 hours in one week, and often find the same output gets done. Use the test to find unnecessary work and tighten deadlines. [L1513.011]
  • Older advice (2021-22): Outsourcing and personal effectiveness are closely linked for an owner-operator, because delegating tasks frees time and focus for the work only the owner can do. [L0802.009]
  • Older advice (2021-22): Owner-level preparation, such as time management, prioritization and focus, comes before scaling tactics, because a business cannot grow faster than its leader's capacity to execute. [L0802.010]
  • The owner's role shifts from running the business to building it. [L0337.041]

Owner time and hourly value

  • Calculate your hourly time value and multiply it by the weekly hours you spend in your current role. If an agency costs less than this weekly opportunity cost and frees you to work on higher-impact activities, the financial math improves. [L0217.011]
  • Example: if you spend 10 hours weekly managing ads at five hundred dollars per hour, your time costs five thousand dollars weekly. Outsourcing frees that value to redirect toward higher-impact work such as supplier negotiations or product development. [L0217.012]
  • Estimate how long a task should take by doing it yourself and then allowing for the staff member's experience. A product upload may take five to ten minutes, and experienced staff can be faster than the owner. Use a reasonable average and reject unreasonable estimates, such as a 500-word article in half an hour. [L1396.053]
  • Outsourcing a task costs money, but the freed time usually lets the business do more growth work, so the extra activity can repay the cost over time. Refusing to outsource to protect every dollar of profit can limit growth and reduce total profit in the long run. [L1247.024]
  • Outsourcing early can speed up progress considerably, even when the outsourcing cost is hard to carry and leaves little profit in the short term, because the business reaches a better position sooner than it would by doing everything itself. [L0499.002]

Task audit: list, measure and test

Step 1: map the work

  • Decide what you actually need before anything else. The four-step method to free up working time is applied in this order: [L0827.001]
    1. Definition: decide what you actually need.
    2. Elimination: remove everything you can get rid of.
    3. Automation: automate what can run without you.
    4. Liberation: outsource what remains and cannot be automated.
  • Work time is drained by three groups of tasks: minimize expenses, material items, responsibilities, accounting and daily routine tasks. Automate lead generation, ad management, FAQs, email responders, call tracking numbers, order emails and passive income. Liberate by handing work to virtual assistants, phone support, a bookkeeper, partners, or by selling the store and living on investment income. [L0827.003]
  • Build a task chart before hiring: [L0337.002]
    1. List the main business areas (in the example: operations, customer service, sales, product support, sales and marketing, accounting).
    2. Break each area into small, specific tasks, e.g. "answer the phone" instead of "customer support".
    3. Color-code who does each task: tasks the owner does are blue; tasks the owner does 100% alone get a heavy dark red outline.
    4. When a task is handed to someone else, recolor it green so the owner can see what has left their plate.
  • To choose which tasks to outsource, work through these steps in order: [L1122.028]
    1. List every task you perform to run the business day to day.
    2. Record how many hours per week each task takes.
    3. Focus on the pain points, which in dropshipping are usually repetitive tasks such as product uploads during setup, or order placement and payment processing when sales are high.
    4. Outsource the simplest repetitive tasks first.
  • Review the task lists of each priority in the 180-day plan (up to five lists). Ask how much of the work the owner should really be doing, and which tasks could be handed to someone else. [L1247.019] (Older advice, 2021-22.)
  • Plan a delegation: [L1514.016] (Older advice, 2021-22.)
    1. Identify the task and ask whether only you can do it, not whether you are the best person for it.
    2. If not, plan when it can be handed off.
    3. Identify where and how to find someone to do it and the options available.
    4. Create SOPs and training materials.
    5. Hand the task over when the timing is right, or set a date if it is not possible immediately.

Step 2: test each task against the criteria

  • Test each task on the task list against seven criteria: [L1247.008] (Older advice, 2021-22.)

    1. The task is simple and repeated.
    2. The task is urgent but not important.
    3. The task is neither urgent nor important.
    4. The task is time consuming for small returns.
    5. The owner is not good at or does not enjoy the task.
    6. The task is unpredictable and disruptive.
    7. The task is affordable to outsource.

    A task only needs to meet ONE of these criteria to be a candidate for outsourcing.

  • Delegate any recurring task that takes more than one hour per day of the owner's time and does not need the owner specifically. The owner's top priority as a business leader is delegation, not doing everything. [L1514.014] (Older advice, 2021-22.)

  • Never delegate a task you would not do yourself. Only hand off work whose results you can judge. [L1515.030]

  • Outsource tasks as early as possible when you lack natural strengths for them or do not enjoy them, and create space for others to contribute. Keep the work that fits your strengths for yourself. [L0701.033]

  • Work that does not come naturally to you or that drains you should be handed to someone else. Every time work must be converted into a form that another person has to handle, value is lost, so fewer conversions give a better return. [L0701.031]

  • A task the owner keeps postponing or dreads is a sign it should be outsourced, even when it is important to the business. [L1247.012] (Older advice, 2021-22.)

  • Tasks the owner dislikes or is weak at tend to be avoided and never completed, so they should be outsourced even if they are important, such as content writing for SEO or managing ads. [L1247.011] (Older advice, 2021-22.)

  • Do not outsource a task only because you dislike it. Some tasks the owner must personally keep, especially those that build key relationships. [L1122.010]

  • Unpredictable tasks that pop up and need quick handling, such as phone calls and online chats, should be outsourced to protect the owner's focus. [L1247.013] (Older advice, 2021-22.)

  • Small optimizations such as product data, product descriptions and SEO metadata help but are not big growth levers, so they are good outsourcing candidates for time-consuming, low-return work. [L1247.010] (Older advice, 2021-22.)

  • Customer service is urgent but usually not important, and it does not grow a store significantly on its own. The owner should keep focus on tasks that drive large gains, such as SEO, email marketing and ads management. [L1247.003] (Older advice, 2021-22.)

  • Growth increases customer service volume. An owner who handles it personally loses time for growth work, creating a vicious cycle that threatens the business in the long run. [L1247.004] (Older advice, 2021-22.)

  • Tasks that are neither urgent nor important should not be done by the owner at all. If they are still wanted, someone else should do them, and the owner's focus should stay only on important tasks. [L1247.009] (Older advice, 2021-22.)

  • When a task needs research, the owner should define exactly what must be found and then hand the research to someone else. Doing the research personally keeps the owner stuck in manual work. [L1247.023] (Older advice, 2021-22.)

  • Consider a virtual assistant when work-life balance suffers because the business takes more screen time than personal time. Delegation can restore free time for family and friends. [L0765.005]

  • Routine work that keeps getting too little time can be protected: move it into the first two work hours after the daily business and community tasks. Ad creation then gets protected focus time instead of leftover slots. [L1181.027]

Priority and sequencing

  • Outsource the most outsourceable tasks first, build up gradually as the business grows, and keep the tasks that only the owner can do or that carry the highest return and complexity. [L1247.018] (Older advice, 2021-22.)
  • Outsource the repetitive, low-skill tasks that eat owner time first, such as loading products, order placement and payment processing. Keep in-house the tasks you do best, and any task that depends on supplier relationships or on understanding paid traffic. [L1122.002]
  • Outsource customer service once order volume exceeds what the owner can answer alone. Keep supplier relationships in-house, since those are central to the business and hard to hand over. [L1279.101]
  • Prioritize outsourcing customer service within the next six months as a primary candidate for delegation, if you have not already done so. [L0223.011] (Older advice, 2021-22.)
  • Owners who already outsource should review how they do it, not only look for new tasks to hand off, because the working process around outsourcing determines much of its value. [L0499.003] (Older advice, 2021-22.)

Before you delegate: do the task yourself first

The rule

  • Do a task yourself before you outsource it, so you know what good results look like. [L0413.012]
  • Learn each task yourself before you delegate it, so you can judge the finished work. Only outsource fully once you have the skills and can check the quality. [L0519.013]
  • Before delegating any task, the owner should first build the skill personally and find out what works. A team member can only follow a method the owner already knows, otherwise they will produce generic output without a clear target. [L1360.002]
  • Only delegate a task after you have learned to do it yourself. Knowing the method lets you judge the output, teach the next person, and cover the work if a team member leaves or raises prices. [L1358.002]
  • Learning a skill yourself first protects the business if a team member leaves or suddenly raises rates. Once you know the method, you can run the task yourself for a short time or teach the next hire. [L1360.003]
  • Perform each day-to-day task yourself until you fully understand it, and only then outsource it. This applies to all routine business tasks, such as customer contact and order handling. [L1447.003]
  • Outsourcing only works when the owner already knows the correct way to do a task. Without first doing the process yourself, you cannot write clear instructions or judge whether the outside person is doing it right. [L0592.007]
  • Delegating a process you have never run yourself is a mistake, because you cannot explain it properly or verify the results. Start with hands-on work and only outsource after the process is proven. [L0592.008]
  • Never outsource paid ads, web design, content, SEO or social posts to outside providers too early. Learn each process first, document it for each supplier, then share the documents with a future team. [L0856.099] (Dated 2024, not older advice.)

The steps

  • Three steps to working less and delegating: [L0230.008]
    1. Build a documented business system with processes and tools.
    2. Find and hire the right person.
    3. Train thoroughly and let go of day-to-day control.
  • Order for scaling a business with a team: [L1359.040]
    1. Do the task yourself until you know the steps work and how long it really takes.
    2. Confirm that the task makes money before you scale it.
    3. Document it as an SOP with short, simple steps.
    4. Decide whether the role needs a contractor or an employee, and whether a virtual assistant or an apprentice fits the work.
    5. Train the new person with the skill transfer process so that efficiency is not lost.
    6. Repeat the cycle for each new process as the business grows.
  • Outsourcing sequence: [L0827.004]
    1. Do the task yourself first.
    2. Eliminate tasks that are not necessary.
    3. Automate what you can, then hire for the rest.
    4. Record your workflow as a screencast so someone else can follow it.
    5. After hiring, follow up with the hire every week.
  • Before outsourcing a task: [L1515.027]
    1. Do the task yourself until you know exactly how it works.
    2. Remove every unnecessary step, spreadsheet column or report.
    3. Automate or buy software for what remains.
    4. Hire a person only for tasks that cannot be removed or automated, and train them with a screencast of you doing the task.
  • Perform each task yourself inside the business until you know how it works and what the correct result looks like. Then: [L0592.006]
    1. Document every step you take, creating a user guide that works as the instruction manual for the business.
    2. Fill the ready-made templates with your own business information to complete the user guide.
    3. Hand the completed user guide to the person you bring in, so they can run the task based on what already works.
    4. Once the task is delegated, focus your own time on growth or other priorities.

What the owner must understand

  • You cannot train someone for a task you have not done yourself. Before handing off customer support or order processing, you must be able to explain the likely customer questions, how to respond to them in different situations, and the supplier-specific steps, including who to contact and what to do when an order goes to a different supplier. [L1447.005]
  • Without a basic understanding of what an outsourced person does, you cannot tell whether they are succeeding or doing good work. This applies equally to outsourcing store building or traffic setup, so a minimum base-level understanding of every area is needed before hiring. [L1447.006]
  • A business does not need to be profitable before you start, but the owner must understand what is happening in it. For example, the owner should be able to explain CPM and conversion results when the assistant reports them. [L0519.017]
  • Handing over the whole dropshipping business to a virtual assistant without understanding it means you cannot tell whether store design, emails or ad results are good. Always keep enough knowledge to evaluate the work. [L0519.016]
  • A beginner should not outsource store creation entirely, because learning to build the store yourself lets you check and teach the assistant later. [L1432.002]
  • Early in a business one person may need to wear all roles, including supplier calls and uploads. Once a process is working, hand specific tasks to others. [L0832.037]

What to delegate first: the order

General order

  • Recommended delegation order for a running dropshipping business: [L1358.003]
    1. Product research
    2. Store build
    3. Traffic and ads
    4. Order fulfillment
    5. Customer support
  • Delegate in this order as the business grows: [L1360.024]
    1. Product research: build your own skill first, then hand over the search with your criteria and tools.
    2. Store development: learn store building yourself, then have someone build the store to your structure once you have a product to test.
    3. Traffic and ads: learn the Facebook or TikTok ad strategy yourself, then let a team member prepare creatives and set up campaigns under your monitoring.
    4. Order fulfillment: outsource early, since it is time-consuming and not highly skilled work.
    5. Customer support: outsource early as well, covering email, live chat and social media messages.
  • Virtual assistants can take over store setup, Facebook ad management, customer emails and order fulfilment, while the owner checks the finished results. Outsourcing is recommended when there is not enough time to finish tasks on schedule. [L0519.014]
  • As a store grows, outsource tasks such as customer service, email marketing and Google Ads so the founder can focus on the highest-impact work, such as Facebook ads and creatives. Founders need to leverage their time once the business reaches a size where doing everything personally no longer scales. [L0639.040]
  • Outsourcing ads, email and creator scripts to an agency and hiring a virtual assistant can free up the founder's time for other work. This makes sense when customer service and operations are taking many hours per day. [L0694.035]
  • Outsource repetitive roles such as customer service once they consume daily time. When one person covers customer service, ad creation, finances and fulfillment at the same time, a structured calendar becomes essential to get anything finished. [L1181.034]
  • Early on, when the owner has limited hours, delegate the most time-consuming tasks first. [L1358.001]

Beginner sequence (as described in the course)

  • Typical beginner delegation sequence as described by the speaker: [L1432.005]

    1. Do the product research yourself and pick the product to test.
    2. Let the assistant build the store and write product descriptions while you learn the store setup.
    3. Have the assistant install apps such as Klaviyo and SMS Bump and implement them, while you understand what each does.
    4. Let the assistant search for product images and videos on Instagram and Facebook.
    5. Once orders arrive, have the assistant learn CJ Dropshipping and handle order fulfillment.
    6. Create a separate support email address and give the assistant direct access to it. For tricky emails, the assistant sends you a screenshot and you dictate the reply, while the assistant sends it.

  • Note on sequence: this beginner order keeps product research with the owner, while the general order above delegates research once the owner has built the skill. Both apply at different stages.

Early-stage and repetitive-first sequence

  • Early-stage sequence for when to outsource: [L1447.007]
    1. Follow the blueprint.
    2. Start processing orders yourself.
    3. Once you are comfortable with the business and feel qualified to operate it, begin outsourcing.
  • Start outsourcing early and in small steps, but only when the business can afford the cost. Build up gradually instead of handing over everything at once. [L1122.003]
  • Assistants can also take over the owner's personal admin, such as drafting emails or researching documents for a task. Handing these tasks off frees time for the business. [L1431.067]
  • At the business-owner stage, delegate personal and university or study tasks as well as business tasks, and hire missing skills such as Google Ads or email marketing into the company rather than relying on outside help. [L1432.035]

The first hire and the first assistant

Timing

  • Hire help once a store is live, running ads and making sales, and you need time for product research, ads and offers. Early on, when the owner has limited hours, delegate the most time-consuming tasks first. [L1358.001]
  • There are two entry points for building a team: once a store is live, sales are coming in and the owner needs help to free time; or early on, when the founder lacks the time to run everything and needs help with the most time-consuming tasks. Both lead to the same goal of delegating work so the owner can focus on product research, running ads and creating offers. [L1360.001]
  • Delegation frees time to focus on the highest-value tasks, such as product research, ads and offers. Outsourcing tasks like order fulfillment or customer support early is a common way to achieve this. [L1360.004]
  • Outsource as early as possible once the business is up, running and profitable, meaning it makes money. Hire help, adjust your schedule and free time from day-to-day tasks at that point. [L1447.008]
  • Waiting until you can no longer cope with the workload before outsourcing is a mistake. Once the business is profitable, delegating routine tasks should begin rather than being postponed until you are overwhelmed. [L1447.009]
  • Hiring the first VA earlier rather than later is recommended, since the sales gap after hiring was large. [L1437.022]
  • At a low volume of high-ticket sales, around 20 sales a month, the owner can handle calls and emails personally, so a virtual assistant is not yet necessary. Add support once revenue and workload clearly justify it. [L1472.057]
  • Many owners delay hiring a virtual assistant because they feel the business depends on them personally. Delegating only works once the owner accepts that the assistant will be the face of the business and must be trained to represent it. [L1000.022]
  • Many owners delay hiring because they believe nobody else can run the business and because the project feels overwhelming with no clear starting point. Breaking the work into a system, a hire and training steps makes it manageable. [L1473.033]
  • Once the early hustle phase is over (about six to twelve months at most), hand off tasks that fall outside your strengths and values. Keep only the work that sits in your sweet spot. [L0701.010]
  • Founders who personally run websites, supplier contact, ads, email and phone for years end up with slow response times and neglected ad optimization. Build a team and systems within the first few months once sales start to take off. [L0852.003] (Older advice, 2020.)
  • Outsourcing is useful for bringing in experience you lack. For example, a hired customer service specialist with 14 years of experience improved service processes and customer engagement. [L1122.011]

Hiring a virtual assistant

  • To hire a virtual assistant: [L0671.085, L0671.222] (Older advice, 2021; the two units are identical and merged here.)
    1. Run the store yourself when it goes live and let orders and emails come in.
    2. Learn how to handle each supplier's orders and the most frequently asked customer questions.
    3. Write these steps in a Google Doc as an instruction manual for your business.
    4. Hire the assistant and give them that document.
  • Outsourcing means hiring a person to take over tasks that still require human interaction, while tasks that repeat identically are automated. Early on, the typical outsourced worker is a virtual assistant hired remotely from anywhere in the world, not an employee on payroll in an office or warehouse. [L1447.002]
  • A virtual assistant makes sense when you want to spend your time on scaling and growth rather than repetitive operational tasks. Delegating routine work frees the owner for decisions that move the business forward. [L0765.003]
  • A virtual assistant buys back time, which is the scarcest resource for a business owner, by taking over day-to-day tasks such as answering phones and managing email. The owner can then focus on growth, travel or rest. [L1000.021]
  • Delegating daily tasks to a virtual assistant freed the owner to focus on the big picture. Hiring the first VA earlier rather than later is recommended, since the sales gap after hiring was large. [L1437.022]
  • Handing over daily operations, including customer service, to the team let the owner stop working day to day and focus on the bigger picture. A team that treats the business as its own can run it while the owner is away. [L1437.033]
  • When an employee is given responsibility and trust, their performance improves noticeably and they ask fewer questions. Letting go of tasks early is the main leverage for the owner. [L1473.030]
  • Training and letting go: Train thoroughly and let go of day-to-day control. [L0230.008]
  • Hire and train with the screencast and SOP: after hiring, follow up with the hire every week. [L0827.004]

Accountability

  • Without someone to hold you accountable, tasks get postponed indefinitely. Hiring help is an investment that also trains leadership and team management skills. [L0519.018]

Owner role as the business scales

Core role

  • The business owner should own strategy: find where the valuable traffic is, design the funnel and ladder, and then hire specialists to run ads and optimize tactics. Trying to do every task personally slows the business down. Division of labor makes growth possible. [L0523.091]
  • At scale the owner should act as the strategic leader who sets the vision, makes sure work gets done and puts the right people in the right roles. Daily tasks such as supplier calls, uploading product pages and optimizing the site should be handed to others. [L0413.008]
  • Partnerships, branding and the X factor are the three most effective ways to raise revenue, so the owner's time should go there. Optimization, product uploads and research can be outsourced to a team. [L0413.015]
  • Once the team handles day-to-day operations and escalations, the owner should keep only sales, some marketing and accounting, which can be done from anywhere. The owner's role shifts from running the business to building it. [L0337.041]
  • Hand over any task that someone else can do, including code-level changes you are not able to make yourself. A strong business leader delegates tasks rather than doing everything personally. [L1300.031] (Older advice, 2021-22.)
  • Outsourcing frees time for growth work, such as finding new businesses, new ideas and new opportunities. Using time on repetitive order handling leaves no room for this. [L1122.012]
  • The purpose of outsourcing routine tasks is to free time for growth, because a business cannot grow while the owner only handles day-to-day operations. Delegating these tasks is the step that allows the business to expand. [L1447.010]
  • Outsourcing is a major lever for e-commerce owners across marketing, growth and day-to-day operations, and it is worth starting early rather than waiting until the business is large. [L0499.001] (Older advice, 2021-22.)

Future role

  • The owner's future role can be to step back from the day-to-day, for example by managing a team or a network of businesses. Decide in the vision whether the owner stays in the weeds or moves to an advisory or management position. [L0439.012] (Older advice, 2021-22.)
  • An owner moves up the ownership ladder as early revenue produces free cash flow to pay the first hire. Each new hire takes one more task off the owner, from doing the work, to managing those who do it, to managing those who work on the business. [L1334.044] (Older advice, 2021-22.)
  • Scaling works best when the owner only reviews what changed: a supplier that stopped responding, a backordered product, or a stock report with low inventory. Routine work is delegated. [L0468.175] (Older advice, 2020.)
  • Before leaving the country or taking a long break, set up the store and hire someone to handle phone calls and customer messages. [L0468.174] (Older advice, 2020.)
  • Early in a business one person may need to wear all roles, including supplier calls and uploads. Once a process is working, hand specific tasks to others. [L0832.037]

Owner-level example

  • A dropshipping owner with a niche store at about 40,000 USD monthly sales had plateaued. Customer feedback showed the range was too narrow, so he planned to add about 700 products. He hired freelancers for image editing, product descriptions and uploads to Shopify, and finished in about three weeks. Within two months monthly sales rose from 40,000 USD to 80,000 USD. Afterwards his own time grew to 20 to 30 hours per week until he moved the tasks to a team, and he cut his weekly time from over 30 hours to under 5 hours per week while keeping pay-per-click management himself. [L1122.006]
  • Starting point: a single-owner store that had run since 2016, with the owner still in a corporate job and working around 40 hours per week on the business in January. The owner set a goal of $2 million revenue for 2021 and $1 million by June 30; 2020 revenue was $1.4 million with a team of three. The owner then joined a state COVID vaccination response team in early January 2021 and worked about seven days a week, 12-hour days, for six months, coordinating 121 nurses and 70 National Guard members who administered 250,000 vaccinations. [L0337.017]
    • What was done: the business was run with help from a team and was reduced to about 10 owner hours per week by June while revenue kept growing.
    • Result: $1 million was reached in May, one month ahead of the June goal, and about $1.29 to $1.3 million was reached over the six months. [L0337.017]
  • A store owner in one case ran a store from 2014 and handled all operations for four years while struggling with trust issues and overwhelm. In 2018, after having a child, she hired a virtual assistant who saved the business and her well-being. Key insight: building systems before a crisis forces you to delegate is better than waiting until life events demand it. [L0230.001]

What must stay in-house

  • Keep supplier contact in-house, because supplier relationships drive bulk rates and discounts that compound as the business grows. Do not hand this task to someone else, even when it is unpleasant. [L1122.009]
  • Keep supplier relationships in-house, since those are central to the business and hard to hand over. [L1279.101]
  • Keep the tasks you do best, and any task that depends on supplier relationships or on understanding paid traffic, in-house. [L1122.002]
  • The owner should keep only sales, some marketing and accounting, which can be done from anywhere. [L0337.041]
  • Owner keeps strategy and key relationships: Partnerships, branding and the X factor are the three most effective ways to raise revenue, so the owner's time should go there. [L0413.015]
  • Focus yourself on ads, supplier relationships, offers and big-picture decisions. Outsource your weaknesses and keep your strengths. Give tasks like web design and customer service to others. [L0852.004] (Older advice, 2020.)

Mistakes to avoid

  • Delegating a process you have never run yourself is a mistake. [L0592.008]
  • Handing over the whole business to a virtual assistant without understanding it is a mistake. [L0519.016]
  • Outsourcing a task costs money, but the freed time usually lets the business do more growth work. [L1247.024]
  • Never outsource a task at a loss: if outsourcing would push the business into the red, wait until the business can afford it, though owners may push closer to break-even because outsourcing enables faster growth. [L1247.014] (Older advice, 2021-22.)
  • Busy work that does not contribute to results should be removed from the business, not just performed more efficiently. [L1039.021]
  • Many owners delay hiring a virtual assistant because they feel the business depends on them personally. [L1000.022]
  • Many owners delay hiring because they believe nobody else can run the business. [L1473.033]
  • Waiting until you can no longer cope with the workload before outsourcing is a mistake. [L1447.009]
  • Refusing to outsource to protect every dollar of profit can limit growth. [L1247.024]
  • Do not outsource paid ads, web design, content, SEO or social posts to outside providers too early. [L0856.099]
  • Outsourcing a task only because you dislike it is a mistake. Some tasks the owner must personally keep. [L1122.010]

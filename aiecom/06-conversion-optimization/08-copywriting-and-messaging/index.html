6.8 Copywriting and messaging

Copy foundations and the job of copy

  • Copywriting is persuasive writing that aims at one specific action, such as a click, an opt-in or an add to cart. In ecommerce most of these actions are add to cart and purchase, so any product page text that pushes toward them is copywriting. (older, 2021-22) [L0396.001]

  • Content writing informs and educates, but a product description has to sell the product. Describing what the product is does not persuade on its own. (older, 2021-22) [L0396.002]

  • The marketing message can be delivered as text, video, images or any combination, and no single format is required. What matters is that every element of the marketing message is present, whatever the format. (older, 2021-22) [L0396.003]

  • Copy has to answer three questions: | Question | Where the answer comes from | |---|---| | Why buy this product? | Mainly the marketing message | | Why buy it from you? | Can be covered by a unique selling proposition of up to five points | | Why buy it now? | Mainly the marketing message |

    (older, 2021-22) [L0396.005]

  • Copy does not need to be the best copy in the world. It only needs to be more developed than the marketing messages of direct competitors to persuade customers to buy from you. (older, 2021-22) [L0396.016]

  • Marketing messages for the product (copy, videos and images) should be better than competitors' messages. This element is separate from the offer structure and gets its own treatment later. (older, 2021-22) [L0411.022]

  • Write website copy from the customer's perspective. Answer their questions before they ask them, such as shipping time, return policy and product materials, so they feel comfortable buying without uncertainty. (older, 2020) [L0268.033]

  • Education builds trust by giving useful information before asking for money. Explaining the problem and its causes gives the reader value up front and makes the product feel like the logical answer. [L0516.035]

  • Consistency also means people want their actions to match the identity they show publicly. Copy that appeals to an identity, such as "of course you want the best for your pet", makes the reader more likely to act in line with it. [L0516.018]

  • A complete landing page must surface the customer's problems, accentuate benefits, demonstrate the features that prove those benefits, show the desired outcome, and handle objections, usually in an FAQ section. [L0917.003]

  • Use an AI chat tool such as ChatGPT to rewrite benefits in a more striking or conversational style, and to produce problem-solution copy and feature-to-benefit lists from the product description. If the copy is weak, use such a tool to draft it. [L0917.008]

  • Specific, consumer-addressed wording in guarantees and product copy outperforms generic phrasing, because generic text reads as template text that buyers have seen many times. [L0577.003]

  • Copy quality rarely decides a page alone. A poor headline or unclear page is a common reason for a 1 to 2 percent conversion rate, and small changes to landing page structure often produce larger gains than split testing. [L0387.026]

Bottom-of-funnel and psychological framing

  • The path to conversion runs through negative emotions, not around them. Bottom-of-funnel ads must address the psychological state of prospects by first acknowledging the problem they face, before presenting how your solution transforms their situation. [L0221.008]
  • To generate bottom-of-funnel ads, ask Claude for ad copy and concepts built from all gathered market research, the emotional framework analysis and the target evidence metrics. Claude creates the ad copy and concepts from the customer psychology and language patterns you provide. [L0221.011]
  • Competitor headlines pair a specific benefit with a time frame, which makes the promise measurable and sets clear expectations. Use the same formula of benefit plus time window, for example Fall Asleep Faster in 14 Days or Feel Less Stiff by Week Three. Only use time frames you can back with real customer results. [L0600.031]

Mapping the product before writing

  • Product copy is built from separate elements such as headline, benefits, features and proof, not written in one pass from top to bottom. Gathering these elements in a structured document before writing reduces the stall that comes from facing an empty product description box, and it shortens total writing time even though it adds a step. (older, 2021-22) [L1160.001]

  • Answer these questions about the product before writing any copy, in this order:

    1. Product name (used as the tab name and header, so the record can be tracked)
    2. Primary problem: the one main problem the product solves for most customers
    3. Secondary problems: any additional problems the product solves
    4. Unique mechanism(s): the attribute that solves the primary problem in a way competing products do not
    5. How the unique mechanism works: the process that produces the result
    6. Features: the attributes of the product you would tell a customer to justify buying it
    7. Benefit of each feature: a list that mirrors the feature list point for point
    8. How the product improves the ideal customer's life: a picture of what the customer experiences once they have used it
    9. Proof points: reviews, demonstration videos, third-party tests

    (older, 2021-22) [L1160.002]

  • Use a Google Sheets product mapping template for the exercise. Make a copy and save it to Google Drive, or convert it to Excel if you prefer to work offline. (older, 2021-22) [L1160.003]

  • Keep a master template tab in the mapping sheet. For each new product, right-click the template tab and choose Duplicate, then right-click the copy and choose Rename to set the product name. Each product then gets its own tab in one file, so products can be flicked between and compared. (older, 2021-22) [L1160.004]

  • The primary problem should be the biggest and most common problem the product solves for most customers, because people buy a product to solve a problem they have. Pick that one for the main slot and record any other problems as secondary problems. (older, 2021-22) [L1160.005]

  • Secondary problems can be recorded in addition to the primary problem. A product may solve several, and that is not a problem. Only the biggest one goes into the main slot when writing copy. (older, 2021-22) [L1160.006]

  • A unique mechanism is an attribute of the product that solves the primary problem in a way competing products do not. Ideally it is built into the product, such as a technology or a specially designed feature, and a product can have more than one. (older, 2021-22) [L1160.007]

  • Claiming a unique feature without explaining how it works is a mistake. After naming a unique mechanism, always state how it produces the result, otherwise the claim is not convincing. (older, 2021-22) [L1160.008]

  • Five product features is the recommended number to list. Three is acceptable if you want to focus, fewer than five is fine, and beyond five the value of each added feature drops. (older, 2021-22) [L1160.009]

  • Each feature must be paired with a benefit that answers why it matters to the customer. Write the benefit list as a matching list to the feature list, so every feature has its own benefit line. (older, 2021-22) [L1160.010]

  • The customer transformation describes what the ideal customer experiences in real use after owning the product, in concrete terms. It should show how daily life changes, not just restate the product's features. (older, 2021-22) [L1160.011]

  • Proof points are evidence that backs your claims and should be recorded in the mapping for use in copy and on the product page. Typical proof points are customer reviews, demonstration videos showing use and effect, and third-party tests that verify the results. (older, 2021-22) [L1160.012]

  • Worked example, a compact two-person infrared sauna for home use. This was technical and expensive, sold outside the field the mapper knew well.

    • Primary problem: the range of health benefits people seek from heat and infrared light, including better sleep, weight loss, relaxation, improved circulation, relief from joint and muscle pain, and improved skin.
    • Secondary problem: convenience, since users no longer have to travel to a gym or spa, pay repeatedly for sessions, or lose travel time.
    • Unique mechanism: a trademarked technology called True Wave II Full Spectrum Heating Technology. Its heaters produce near, mid and far infrared together at a full 1000W, which delivers the benefits of infrared saunas in less time.
    • Features listed: True Wave Technology, low EMF, 360 degree heating, non-toxic materials, easy assembly, lifetime warranty, and chromotherapy lighting.
    • Matching benefits:
      • full-spectrum heat in a shorter session
      • electromagnetic output of only 1mg against a recommended healthy limit of 3mg, which makes everyday use considered safe based on testing to date
      • consistent infrared access across the whole body from 360 degree heating
      • no toxic chemicals escaping from the materials
      • assembly by two people in about an hour with no professional installation needed
      • a lifetime warranty so the customer never pays again for a defective unit
      • full-spectrum light therapy linked to reduced swelling, pain relief and faster recovery
    • Customer transformation: the customer can practise heat therapy at home, which makes them more consistent and helps them feel better in both physical and emotional life.
    • Proof points: product reviews, a video demonstration of the product, and third-party testing of the heat sources.

    (older, 2021-22) [L1160.014]

  • An infrared sauna example listed 1000W of infrared energy as the full output, stated as 25x more than the closest competitor, and an electromagnetic field output of 1mg against a recommended healthy maximum of 3mg. These figures were used as concrete, checkable benefit claims in the copy. (older, 2021-22) [L1160.015]

  • Fill in a marketing message sheet for each product before writing its description:

    1. Write the primary promise or promises.
    2. Write the unique mechanism.
    3. List the features and their benefits.
    4. List the dimensionalized benefits to include on the page.
    5. Give the sheet to the copywriter together with the product data, or use it yourself as the first step before writing.

    (older, 2021-22) [L0396.033]

Problems, pain points and desire

  • Problems are not always consciously recognized by the buyer. Status-related problems in particular are usually felt subconsciously, so copy has to name them even when the buyer has not put them into words. (older, 2021-22) [L1157.002]
  • A problem, also called a pain point, creates a desire for either a solution or a pleasant outcome. Copy should connect the problem to the result the buyer wants. (older, 2021-22) [L1157.003]
  • Buyers are motivated either by moving away from pain or by moving toward pleasure. Copy can address only the pain, only the pleasure, or both, depending on what fits the product and audience. (older, 2021-22) [L1157.004]
  • People buy for only two reasons: to escape pain or to pursue pleasure. Copy that triggers these feelings more strongly than competitor pages tends to sell more. (older, 2021-22) [L0396.015]
  • People buy a product to solve a problem that creates a desire, so the copy must first name the problem the product solves. Match it to the problem the target customer is actually experiencing. It is fine to address more than one problem the product solves. (older, 2021-22) [L0396.006]
  • After naming the problem, the copy must explain how the product solves it. Then it must describe how the customer's life changes in a way they can picture, which is what is meant by benefits. (older, 2021-22) [L0396.007]
  • Visitors on product pages already have a strong interest in solving a specific pain point. A headline that names that pain point or its direct outcome gets the most attention. (older, 2021-22) [L0416.005]
  • Copywriting should surface the customer's problem first, then present the solution as a benefit. Features are shown to prove the benefit, and the copy should ask what each feature means for the customer. [L0919.009]
  • For products that solve a health problem, name the specific conditions and the pain the customer wants to escape. When the problem is clear, the customer does not need false urgency to buy, only a direct call to fix it. (older, 2021-22) [L0396.026]

Emotion before logic

  • Primary promise and unique mechanism trigger emotion, while proof, dimensionalized benefits and comparisons supply the logic that confirms the feeling is right. Specifications and features written clearly also serve as logic. (older, 2021-22) [L0396.014]
  • People buy an emotion and then justify it with logic, so each section of the page should add rational reasons that match the pain point the visitor is feeling. [L1016.032]
  • When buyers are asked why they bought, they recall the logical reasons easily, but they often cannot name the emotion they felt. This effect is strongest for expensive purchases, so high-ticket copy must trigger the emotion before it offers the reasons to justify the purchase. (older, 2021-22) [L0611.009]
  • Once the headline and primary promise have hooked the visitor, the copy should trigger emotion before the product is pitched. People buy for emotional reasons that logic then supports, so an emotional response built early raises the chance of a sale considerably. (older, 2021-22) [L0580.001]
  • The first one or two paragraphs under the headline should name the main problem the primary promise addresses and show what life looks like once that problem is solved. Describe the feeling the customer will have after solving it, such as relief, happiness, excitement or satisfaction. (older, 2021-22) [L0580.002]
  • Getting the reader to picture the end result makes them start to experience the emotions tied to that result. If product images or video already created this picture, the copy reinforces and multiplies the effect, so it is still worth doing. (older, 2021-22) [L0580.003]
  • Two proven ways to create this emotional visualization are telling a story about the product and using thought commands. Story is the stronger choice when a real story exists; thought commands work for any product. (older, 2021-22) [L0580.004]
  • Before writing the imagined scene, build a short product map that lists the primary problem, the primary promise and the secondary problems. Take the scene's angle and vocabulary from these entries so the picture follows directly from the headline and keeps the copy consistent. (older, 2021-22) [L0580.019]
  • If the product page already has sales copy, the story or thought-command section can be added as a few extra paragraphs without rewriting the rest. Highlight the new text while editing so it can be reviewed and compared against the original version. (older, 2021-22) [L0580.020]
  • Concrete everyday details, such as a specific time of day, a short distance from the bed and one simple action, make a scene easier for the reader to picture than general benefit statements. Show a routine the reader can imagine happening in their own home. (older, 2021-22) [L0580.021]
  • Copy that opens with a thought or story and then moves into the offer is a structure to test. Add the copy elements one at a time (opening story or thought command, primary promise, unique mechanism, then benefits) and save each version. Comparing the versions shows whether each added element makes the text easier to read and more convincing. (older, 2021-22) [L0465.029]
  • A mechanism placed right after the primary promise keeps readers reading toward the call to action and at the same time builds the logical case for the product. Every section of the copy should keep the reader moving down the page. (older, 2021-22) [L0465.016]

Primary promise headlines

  • Good copy has no effect if nobody reads it, so the headline's first job is to earn attention before any selling happens. A product page or sales page headline should make visitors read the next sentence, not decide whether to buy. (older, 2021-22) [L0416.001]

  • Headlines carry most of the copywriting weight, followed by short bullet points. Keep bullets short and benefit-driven, and add a testimonial section because shoppers look for reviews before they trust a site. [L0335.043]

  • In a video sales presentation, the headline is the first one or two sentences spoken at the start of the video. Its purpose is the same as in text: to make viewers decide to keep watching. (older, 2021-22) [L0416.003]

  • Headline must include searchable keywords customers use. Example: Spiralizer Envy Spiral Vegetable Slicer includes both the brand name and the product keywords customers search for. (undated) [L0227.020]

  • Place a unique selling proposition with the product name at the top or directly below it, and include a money-back guarantee in it. Keep the line short and benefit-led. [L0871.024]

  • Hero value-proposition formula: a short headline that states the outcome the customer wants, paired with a qualifier that makes it specific to the product, then a trust line directly beneath. Example pattern for a grooming product: Remove all pet hair from your home easily and effortlessly, followed by Rated Excellent on Trustpilot. Second example for a fitness recovery product: Boost recovery and blood flow after every session, followed by Trusted by 10,000+ athletes. [L0918.004]

  • Procedure for writing a primary promise headline:

    1. Read the primary problem the product solves from the product mapping worksheet.
    2. Check whether the product sits in the health or finance space. If it does, do not put health or financial result claims in the headline.
    3. If it is health or finance, pick a secondary problem (such as convenience, time saved or money saved) or the biggest feature or unique mechanism as the basis of the headline. If it is not health or finance, start from the primary problem.
    4. Write the headline as a direct reflection of that problem, making the promise specific and including the how.
    5. If you use a number, make sure you have evidence for it and can explain where it comes from when asked.
    6. Make the headline bigger and bolder than the body copy, and include keywords if organic search matters.
    7. Split test the headline against at least one alternative and keep the version with the better time on page and conversion rate.

    (older, 2021-22) [L0416.006]

  • Case: infrared sauna headline. The product is in the health space and its primary problem is health benefits, so a health-based headline such as weight loss within a timeframe would put ads at risk and could not be proven. The copywriter chose the unique mechanism (full-spectrum infrared heating) as the basis, because the manufacturer provided heating-power and efficiency figures claimed to be backed by evidence, and avoided naming any health benefit. The headline promised 25 times more heating power and efficiency from the IS-2 sauna, with the product title and image kept above it and the headline placed as the first text element visitors read. No sales outcome is reported. The stated goal was to create enough curiosity that readers continue to the body copy, and the copywriter noted that the figure must be explainable if a customer asks where it comes from. (older, 2021-22) [L0416.023]

  • Outcome-led headline with a proof stack: the headline names the result the buyer gets, a short support line makes the product specific, and three perks or guarantees follow, such as free shipping, a free gift or a risk-free trial. The buyer reads the benefit first and sees low-risk reasons to buy right away, instead of a temporary discount.

    • Example 1: Keep Your Knives Sharp Through Every Dinner Rush, followed by a forged steel blade, free resharpening for a year and a 60-day return window.
    • Example 2: Get Clean Sound Without the Cable Mess, followed by a 30-hour battery case, free shipping and a 30-day money-back guarantee.

    (Source E 2025-26) [L0886.032]

  • Headlines that name the specific risk a buyer fears and then offer one simple action to avoid it convert well. The formula is risk first, then the low-effort fix.

    • Example 1: Don't book the trip before you check the seat map.
    • Example 2: Don't buy the sofa before you measure the doorway.

    (Source E 2025-26) [L1016.041]

  • Formula: compare the abstract benefit to a familiar professional service the buyer would normally pay for, so the product feels like a personal solution. This makes the benefit tangible and memorable in headlines and copy.

    • Example 1: A masseuse for your back, in your pocket.
    • Example 2: A personal trainer on your wrist, without the monthly gym fee.

    (Source C) [L0919.044]

Unique mechanism and features-to-benefits

  • Introduce the unique mechanism with a subheading to break up the copy and make it easier to read. The subheading can be a question, a statement or just the technology name. A subheading is optional, not required. (older, 2021-22) [L0465.025]

  • Pattern: a landing page that explains why earlier solutions failed. First state that most treatments only act on the surface, then that the real problem sits deeper in the layer where the damage is, then present the mechanism of your product as the deeper fix. This works because it explains past failure and makes the new mechanism credible.

    • Example 1: Most hair serums only coat the strands, but thinning starts in the follicle, which is why nothing you tried worked. Our formula reaches the follicle, so the result comes from the root.
    • Example 2: Most eye creams sit on the skin and only hide puffiness for an hour. The swelling is caused by fluid under the surface, and our gel-patch works there.

    [L0374.023]

  • A feature should never be stated on its own, because it does not tell the customer why it matters. Always follow each feature with the benefit it delivers in practice. (older, 2021-22) [L0396.021]

  • Writing a feature list without the benefit each feature delivers leaves the customer to work out why it matters, and many will not. Every feature in the description should have a benefit statement attached. (older, 2021-22) [L0396.022]

  • Focus product page copy on benefits (what the customer will feel or achieve) rather than features (technical specifications). Show the end experience: instead of multiple different features, write transform your skin appearance and confidence. Include stories and feelings, not plain technical details. [L0321.007]

  • Feature-to-benefit pattern: name a concrete feature or ingredient, then state what it does for the customer in outcome terms.

    • Example 1: Our joint chews contain fatty acids; this supports everyday mobility and strong bones.
    • Example 2: Our bottle is made with a fast-dissolving powder; you have a smooth coffee in ten seconds, no blender needed.

    Features prove the benefit and make it believable. [L0919.010]

  • Copy should also show the desired outcome, meaning what the customer will be able to do or feel after buying, and future-pace them into it. Example pattern: Glowing skin: get selfie ready with a radiant complexion. [L0919.011]

  • Feature-to-benefit pairing for a two-person infrared sauna:

    • A full-spectrum heater with 1000 W output gives full benefit in a shorter session.
    • 360-degree heating gives consistent heat across the whole body.
    • Non-toxic materials mean no harmful chemicals can escape during heating.
    • Assembly by two people in about one hour means buyers need no professional installation or prior installation knowledge.

    Each feature is followed by the practical result for the buyer and why it matters. (older, 2021-22) [L0416.026]

  • Feature-to-benefit explanations, with examples:

    • For a fleece product, the fleece traps air to insulate, which means the wearer stays warm for longer. Writing it this way links the material directly to a customer result on the landing page.
    • An open toe on a warm sock or legwear feature can be explained as letting the toes breathe, so the customer understands why the design matters.
    • A full-length stocking that covers the whole leg is explained as keeping the entire leg warm rather than only part of it. The benefit statement should name the body area the customer gets covered.
    • A static microfiber brush head with a hair collection lid can be turned into two benefits on a pet-hair cleaning page: it keeps furniture and surfaces intact, and it lets the user clean without the brush getting cluttered.

    Pair each feature with the comfort or practical result it delivers rather than listing it alone. Each benefit should answer what the customer avoids or gains. [L0917.016, L0917.017, L0917.018, L0917.019]

  • A credible statistic for a cold-legs problem is that legs account for about 25% of the body's heat loss, which makes the problem concrete in problem-agitation-solution copy. Confirm any such figure against a published source before putting it on a landing page. [L0917.020]

  • Pattern for the feature block on a product page: a short lead line in the form [number] reasons to [action] today, followed by bullets where each bullet pairs one feature with one dimensionalized benefit instead of listing a bare spec. It works because every line answers what the feature does for the reader, and the time-bound lead adds a reason to act now without inventing scarcity. (older, 2021-22) [L0396.036]

    • Example 1: Four reasons to order the kettle today: followed by Double-walled steel keeps tea hot for two hours so that you can sit down for a proper break.
    • Example 2: Five reasons to book your bike service today: followed by Fresh brake pads and a true wheel so that every ride feels smooth and you can enjoy the hills again.

  • Compare the product against a familiar alternative using a concrete figure. Example: a 30 minute sauna session claimed to burn up to 600 calories, against about 300 calories from 30 minutes of jogging. Every such figure needs a cited source, otherwise it counts as an unsupported claim. (older, 2021-22) [L0396.023]

  • Reframe a physical effort in a familiar unit to make it feel easier, for example stating that a short workout equals a walk of a specific distance. The familiar comparison turns an abstract effort into something the buyer can picture, and it also answers the objection that the activity is too hard. [L0919.042]

  • A product page for a connected home infrared sauna can list the concrete features buyers compare: wood species (for example Western Red Canadian Cedar or Basswood), glass front with glass skylight roof, recessed accent lights, digital keypad with intensity control, medical-grade chromotherapy, Bluetooth and AUX audio, optional control by tablet or smartphone app, and a reversible bench with an ergonomic side and a flat side. Write each feature with its exact specification instead of general words such as premium. (older, 2021-22) [L0465.030]

Proof, claims and credibility

  • Every claim in a description needs proof, and more proof points make the copy more convincing. A claim that cannot be backed by evidence should be removed. For example, a health claim needs links to the relevant studies, and a claim of highest quality in a class needs demonstrable support. (older, 2021-22) [L0396.008]
  • Beyond the product itself, buyers want answers to three doubts: how the product is better than similar products, how the buyer is protected, and why this is the best deal. Address these with comparison points, a risk reversal such as a guarantee, and perceived value bonuses. (older, 2021-22) [L0396.009]
  • Scientific or research claims can counter a doubt about effectiveness, for example by citing studies that show a method works better than a common alternative. Claims should be backed by a verifiable source. [L0919.033]
  • Show scientifically or quantitatively backed benefits with exact numbers below the fold. Example: a 15-minute routine delivers the benefit in 2 minutes and reduces pain by 9% and soreness by 5%, because numbers back up the emotional buying decision. [L1016.031]
  • When a product is positioned as the most cost-effective option, the copy attracts price-sensitive buyers, so that claim is a conscious targeting decision. Premium products should be aimed at buyers who want the best rather than the cheapest, and a comparison document should back the claim. (older, 2021-22) [L0396.025]
  • Selling can be harder when the product is too perfect, because buyers suspect exaggeration. Acknowledged flaws, explained honestly, increase credibility. [L0378.080]
  • Concrete figures in a headline or description need a source behind them. Used in the infrared sauna example, 1000W, 25x and 1mg against 3mg were presented as checkable claims. (older, 2021-22) [L1160.015]

Full product description structure

  • Build the product description in this order:

    1. Primary promise: a headline-style promise that is the opposite of the main problem that brought the customer to the page.
    2. Unique mechanism: the feature or attribute that delivers the promise, ideally unique to this product, such as patented technology or a special shape.
    3. Features followed by benefits: state what each feature does in practice for the customer.
    4. Dimensionalized benefits: turn each benefit into an outcome that triggers an emotion and can be pictured.
    5. Proof of claims and social proof: back every claim with evidence and show existing customer reviews.
    6. Comparison: show how the product is better than similar products, which attributes differ and who it suits best, using a comparison document or an on-page comparison tool.
    7. Urgency hook: connect the problem to an emotional response and state what happens if the customer does not buy now.
    8. Call to action: tell the reader exactly what to do, such as buy now.

    (older, 2021-22) [L0396.013]

  • A well-structured product description for a more expensive or technical product runs to about 500 words or a little more. Simple products such as t-shirts can use a much shorter version, as long as all elements are still present. (older, 2021-22) [L0396.031]

  • The length of the description is not what matters. What matters is that each element of the marketing message appears on the page. Simpler products can carry the same elements visually with little text. (older, 2021-22) [L0396.032]

  • Case: worked product description for a low-EMF far infrared sauna. The writer did not sell this product and knew little about it, and wrote the description to demonstrate the method.

    • Starting point: a 4-person unit priced at $4,999, positioned as the most cost-effective infrared sauna on the market.
    • The copy opens with a promise for buyers who want better health with convenience and a lower price, then contrasts the usual alternatives. A typical installed sauna costs over $8,000, and a trip to a gym or outdoor run takes about 60 minutes in total for a 30 minute workout.
    • The mechanism is far infrared rays that raise blood circulation and oxygen supply at a lower temperature than steam saunas.
    • Health claims about inflammation, pain, skin and conditions such as arthritis, hemorrhoids and psoriasis are listed, with a note that links to peer-reviewed studies must be inserted.
    • The cardio comparison says a 30 minute session can burn up to 600 calories, against about 300 calories for 30 minutes of jogging. That figure needs a cited source.
    • Features include eight colour settings of medical-grade chromotherapy lighting, a Canadian hemlock wood build, automatic thermostat, timer and buzzer, built-in audio, and a flat pack that one person can assemble.
    • Each feature is turned into a benefit. The lighting is tied to restoring the skin and looking and feeling younger. The wood is tied to saving money and easing worry about the environment.
    • The description closes with a call to action to buy today with delivery within 72 hours, a link to a model comparison document, and a separate specification list on the page.
    • The finished text runs a little over 500 words. The lesson reports no sales results.

    (older, 2021-22) [L0396.035]

  • Lead the product description with the problem the customer wants to avoid, then state the product's unique ingredients or mechanism. Visitors on a product page are already looking for a solution, so describing what they can see does not help the decision. (older, 2021-22) [L0409.020]

  • A product description that only lists specifications and what is visible in the image does not sell the product. Specifications are needed, but the description must also state the primary promise, the benefits and why to buy this product from this store rather than others. (older, 2021-22) [L0409.036]

  • Procedure for a complete sales page, in this order:

    1. Immediate problem.
    2. Consequence of action or inaction.
    3. Reframing why the old way fails.
    4. New mechanism.
    5. Who it is for.
    6. Who it is not for.
    7. What you get.
    8. Proof that it works.
    9. Social proof.
    10. Risk reversal.
    11. Comparison against alternatives.
    12. FAQ.
    13. Call to action.

    (Source E 2025-26) [L1016.040]

  • Procedure for a complete landing page of product copy:

    1. Build the hero and value proposition.
    2. Surface the problem, accentuate the benefits, prove them with features, show the outcome, and handle objections in an FAQ.

    For the hero, see the homepage procedure below. [L0917.003]

  • Process for the product description format: format the description and features on the page for readability. Description comes first, then features, which also matches what Google Merchant Center and search ranking expect. [L0964.132]

  • Optimize bullet points and product description with compelling sales copy. Format the description with paragraphs, bold text and colours to improve readability. Test and iterate: tweaks to copy can increase conversion by 25%. (undated) [L0227.022]

Objection handling

  • Objection handling means identifying the barriers that stop a purchase, such as doubts about ingredients, quality, price, whether the product works, or whether the customer believes it will work for them. Each barrier should be answered on the page with proof, a solution or a reframing. [L0919.017]

  • Process for objection handling:

    1. Research the common objections customers have to the product, covering price, quality, ingredients, safety and believability.
    2. Address each objection on the page, for example with a full ingredient list or an all natural statement, or with proof.
    3. For price objections, reframe in terms of savings or spread the benefit over time so it compounds.
    4. Use FAQs to cover common questions such as how the product works, how many uses one bottle gives, and the size of the product.
    5. Use comparison tables to position the product against competitors or against substitutes the customer already uses.

    [L0919.018]

  • Address buyer concerns directly in the product description and in reviews. Identify the main doubts a customer might have, such as a shoe being too tight, then answer them in the product description and in review text, for example a review stating the fit was perfect. This builds trust and speeds up the decision to buy. [L0773.024, L0774.026]

    • whether the product hurts or suits sensitive skin

    • whether it combines with other skincare

    • how long one bottle lasts

  • Address the specific objections and regrets a buyer fears before purchase, such as a setting that feels bulky, stones that look smaller or larger than expected, or proportions that do not suit the hand. (Source E 2025-26) [L1016.039]

  • Formula: name the common solution the buyer already uses and tell them to ditch it, then switch to your product for a specific result, then state the benefit with a word of emotional urgency. This surfaces the problem before offering the solution.

  • When presenting reciprocity gifts such as free shipping or discounts, frame them as a personal investment from leadership rather than corporate promotion. Working method and copy structure

  • Treat product copy as separate parts that are written and reworked one at a time, then combined into one page. This makes it easier to improve each part without rewriting the whole text. [L1420.001]

  • A complete product copy has six segments: the primary promise or headline, a "paint a picture" section, the unique mechanism, the power benefits, the proof points, and a call to action. Check every product page for all six. [L1420.002]

  • Arrange the segments in a fixed order so the finished page reads as one logical sequence from promise to call to action, following the segment order above. [L1420.003]

  • The segments do not have to be written from top to bottom. Start with the headline, the features and benefits, or the product story, then fill in the other segments afterward. [L1420.004]

  • The "paint a picture" segment helps the customer imagine the life or result they want after buying. It sits between the headline and the product mechanics, so the reader sees the outcome before the details. [L1420.006]

  • The segment method works for a single product page in an ecommerce store and for a sales page inside a sales funnel. It applies to any product type, so the same six segments can be reused in both formats. [L1420.011]

  • Writing product copy as one continuous text from start to finish often leads to weak transitions and missing segments. Mapping the blocks first shows gaps before the copy is finished. (older, 2021-22) [L1420.008]

  • To practise, run the product mapping exercise and write copy with the segment method on the first product. If a team member writes product copy, give them the same method and have them build the copy from the same blocks. (older, 2021-22) [L1420.009]

  • Copy alone does not sell an ecommerce product. It has to work together with product images and video that show the product in use and the benefits it delivers. Strong images without copy and strong copy without images both underperform, so plan them as one set. (older, 2021-22) [L1460.006]

  • Persuasive copy that beats competitors gives an advantage, because many competitors have good images but weak copy. Improving copy that persuades prospects more effectively is expected to raise the conversion rate. (older, 2021-22) [L1460.007]

  • Copying a weak website or landing page and expecting good results does not work. The store page needs its own improvement in copywriting and presentation. [L1469.009]

  • Copy is one lever for raising the conversion rate and sales from existing traffic, but it does not work alone. A strong product, good traffic generation and other on-site conversion work are needed as well. (older, 2021-22) [L1493.001]

  • Many small and medium-sized ecommerce stores put far less time and effort into their website copy than it deserves. This leaves sales on the table even when traffic and products are already in place. (older, 2021-22) [L1493.002]

  • Ecommerce copy does not need to be expert-level to noticeably increase sales. In most cases it only needs to be more persuasive than what competing stores offer the same customers. (older, 2021-22) [L1493.003]

  • Model product and sales copy on the sequence a good in-store salesperson follows, since an online visitor has no one to ask. (older, 2021-22) [L1460.012]

    1. Identify the buyer's problem.
    2. Clarify the outcome they want.
    3. Give a clear view on how to solve it.
    4. Recommend the specific product.
    5. Ask for the purchase.

    A good product may sell itself, but the copy still has to explain why it fits this buyer. [L1460.012]

  • Use headlines pulled from five-star Amazon reviews to find marketing angles. [L1260.030]

  • Use one benefit-driven headline, one benefit line, and a maximum of two short paragraphs. Use longer copy only for complex products that need education. Otherwise follow with an image or GIF, then benefit bullets. [L1260.030]

  • AI-generated copy can produce very brief micro-story blocks that do not fill a benefit section. Expand each section copy so it matches the image size and explains the feature. [L1331.027]

  • Product descriptions must sell the product with concrete benefits, features and specifications, including dimensions. A description that only names the product does not persuade buyers. [L1346.018]

Primary promise and headlines

  • Landing page headlines work best as a big, bold claim or benefit that states the desired outcome right away. People move toward pleasurable outcomes and away from painful ones, so surfacing the main benefit first captures attention immediately. [L1210.001]
  • Headlines on the landing page should be emotional and benefit-driven, so the visitor feels that the product solves a current pain point after reading the headline. Visitors first check the main image, the headline, and the product name before they scroll. [L1382.032]
  • Benefit headline formula: [desired outcome] + [a word that signals novelty] + [a word that removes perceived effort or sacrifice]. Novelty signals something different from past failed attempts. The effort-reducing word works because the desired goal is usually perceived as resource-intensive, slow, or hard. [L1210.002]
    • Meal-replacement brand: "The simple new way to lose weight without giving up dessert."
    • Home-office chair: "The fresh, comfortable way to stop back pain at your desk."
  • Benefit headline formula: state the outcome the buyer wants, add an effortless qualifier, then name the everyday result they keep afterwards. Structure: [desired outcome] + [effortless qualifier] + [result they live with]. It works because a visitor understands within seconds what the product does for them and what their life looks like afterwards. [L1363.054]
    • "Sleep through the night without waking to a cold room, and start every morning rested."
    • "Keep your kitchen counters spotless with one wipe, and spend your evenings on what you enjoy."
  • Semi-headline: place a one-line statement directly under the product name that names the buyer's specific pain or desired result and signals who the product is for. Visitors arriving on mobile decide within seconds whether the product fits their problem, and a precise problem statement makes them feel understood. Formula: [specific pain or outcome] + [short promise of how the product delivers it]. [L1259.061]
    • "Sore feet after every shift? Cushioned insoles that keep you moving all day."
    • "Frizz ruining your style? A leave-in serum that smooths hair in one pass."
  • Test headlines with this procedure: [L1261.128]
    1. Identify the audience and the one or two claims that matter most to them.
    2. Write a specific headline that addresses that audience and includes a concrete claim or offer.
    3. Write body copy that contains every important claim, with figures, and a clear next step.
    4. Include a coupon or reply device so the reader can act later.
    5. Test the headline against alternatives while keeping the body copy the same, and keep the winner by cost per customer.

Unique selling points and shorthand

  • Unique selling points work as shorthand for visitors who skim or already have high intent. Write them as short benefit lines that make the visitor believe they will reach the desired outcome. [L1207.009]
  • Add short USP lines (unique selling points or benefits) below the headline for visitors who skim or who already have high intent. Each USP should be one quick line that reinforces the desired outcome and lowers effort or sacrifice. [L1209.009, L1210.005]
  • Shorthand USPs are short, benefit-led phrases placed near the top for skimmers and high-intent visitors who do not read the full body copy. Effective USPs lower effort and sacrifice, for example by stating suitability for all ages or that no experience is required, which lowers the barrier to entry. [L1210.005, L1209.009]
  • Test every unique selling point by asking whether it lowers effort or sacrifice on the way to the outcome. Points that do not reduce effort, sacrifice, or risk add little to the page. [L1207.010]
  • Remove barriers to entry with USPs such as "suitable for all ages" or "no experience required", because they signal that anyone can get started. Lower the perceived effort and skill needed to buy and use the product. [L1209.010]
  • Pointing out in advance which qualities to notice makes buyers perceive those qualities, whereas identical products presented without guidance are chosen almost at random. Name the features that set the product apart so the buyer sees them. [L1261.149]

Unique mechanism and features-to-benefits

  • The unique mechanism explains why this product delivers its result differently from alternatives. Power benefits and proof points then back up the main promise, so each claim has support on the page. (older, 2021-22) [L1420.007]
  • Benefit copy should describe what the product does for the customer to reach their goal, not list product features. [L1363.008]
  • Write product copy around the benefit the buyer gets in daily life, not around a list of features. Explain why the buyer would keep reading and why they would buy. [L1194.043]
  • Benefit line pattern: state the feature, then say what the customer gains from it in their own terms (time, money, health, peace of mind). (older, 2021-22) [L1160.016]
    • Warranty: a lifetime warranty means the customer never has to pay again if the product fails.
    • Size: a compact unit fits a home, so the customer no longer needs to travel for the same result.
  • Product features should be presented as proof that the promised outcome is achievable, not as stand-alone specifications. Each feature should support the desired result, for example by showing that the product fits a wide range of sizes or is comfortable to use, which raises the perceived likelihood of success. [L1210.011]
  • Feature-to-outcome pattern: state a feature with a concrete range or measurement, then link it to a benefit that supports the promised result. [L1210.012]
    • Backpack: "Fits laptops from 13 to 17 inches, so it works for students and commuters alike."
    • Kitchen knife: "Hand-forged 8-inch blade that stays sharp for months, so prep takes minutes."
  • Feature bullets for a fitness hula hoop should cover universal use (fully adjustable design), a wide size range (span 20 to 42 inch for all waist sizes), a soft comfortable fit, and stability (it does not fall down, unlike conventional hula hoops). [L1207.028]
  • A size range of 20 to 42 inch on a fitness hula hoop supports the claim that one product fits all waist sizes. [L1207.029]
  • Feature proof example for a sizing product: adjustable design with a 20 to 42 inch waist range to fit all waist sizes, soft comfortable fit, and a modern design. Each feature answers the question of whether the product will work for this specific buyer. [L1209.019]

Proof and credibility

  • When a promise sounds big, shoppers want proof. Specific features such as a clinically proven ingredient raise the perceived likelihood that the outcome will be achieved. [L1207.027]
  • Clinically proven ingredients or claims act as proof that increases the perceived likelihood of reaching the outcome, especially when the buyer doubts the promise. Add a verifiable proof element when a benefit sounds too good to be true. [L1209.020]
  • A study of a large ecommerce store that optimized its product titles reported about 20% more traffic, about 70% more ROI and a higher conversion rate. The gain came from appearing for more specific, relevant searches. [L1328.031]
  • Show a specific count of satisfied results to prove a claim, such as 300 employees who received a raise in one week after studying a course, or 280 new bank depositors in one month from circular letters alone. Counts certified by records carry more weight than adjectives. [L1369.032]
  • A "best choice" badge without any supporting reason means nothing to a shopper. Use badges and claims that describe a concrete benefit the buyer can understand. [L1346.019]

Simplicity and three-step setup

  • Reduce a process to three simple steps to lower the barrier to entry. People understand threes quickly, which avoids cognitive overload. [L1207.030]
  • Three-step setup format: presenting setup or usage as three simple numbered steps reduces perceived complexity and avoids cognitive overload. Complex processes should be distilled into three clear steps. [L1210.013]
    • Water filter: "One: fill the jug. Two: insert the filter. Three: start pouring."
  • Setup procedure: [L1209.021]
    1. Show the setup as a simple numbered sequence with exactly three steps (e.g. "one two three let's go").
    2. Keep each step short and concrete.
    3. State that the product is simple to use, so the buyer sees low effort and time required.

Effort and comparison framing

  • Humans conserve energy and look for maximum output for minimum input. Presenting less effort and less time for the same result makes the purchase feel like a quick win. [L1207.032, L1210.014, L1209.022]
  • People try to conserve effort and get the maximum result for the minimum input, so promises like "less effort, more results" make a product more attractive. Message reduced effort, sacrifice and time together. [L1209.022, L1210.014, L1207.032]
  • Compare your product's cost and results with the alternatives buyers already try, such as supplements or doctor visits. Showing only features does not explain why the product is worth buying. [L1194.030]
  • Framing example: 30 minutes of a workout is presented as equal to a two-mile walk in calories, which connects a new value to a reference the buyer already understands. Choose a familiar reference point (distance, time, cost) that makes the benefit easy to grasp. [L1209.011]
  • Effort-comparison framing: describe the required activity in terms of a familiar everyday reference the buyer already understands, such as relating a short workout to the calories of a known walking distance. This makes an unfamiliar result easier to judge and more believable. [L1210.006]
    • Supplement: "One scoop gives you the protein of a full chicken breast, without the cooking."
    • Language app: "Ten minutes a day gets you the vocabulary of a semester-long class."
  • Cost-calculation formula: ask the reader to add up what his current method costs in money and time, so he reaches the gap himself before you present your product. A figure the buyer calculates himself is more convincing than one the seller states, and he feels the cost now. [L1369.028]
    • Delivery van: add up each month's fuel, insurance, repairs and driver hours for your current delivery van, then compare that total with the monthly cost of the service offered.
    • Data entry: total what your team spends each month on manual data entry and rework, then compare that total with the software subscription.

Similarity and social proof

  • Shoppers quickly check whether others are similar to them as a threat test. Wording such as "customers like you" signals that the product is meant for people like the visitor. [L1207.019, L1209.014]
  • Buyers judge whether someone is a threat by checking similarity, so describing reviewers as "customers like you" signals that the shop's customers are like the visitor. Use the similarity principle in testimonial and review wording. [L1209.014, L1207.019]
  • Similarity pattern: [customers like you] + [specific result or time]. The similarity cue lowers the feeling of threat and makes the result feel reachable. Referring to the buyer as part of a group signals that the buyer is not a threat and that others like them succeeded. Pairing this with a count of satisfied buyers strengthens trust. [L1207.020, L1210.009]
    • "Busy parents like you can finish this routine in 10 minutes."
    • "Beginners like you see results in the first week."
    • Skincare brand: "Customers like you saw clearer skin in 14 days."
    • Running shoe: "Runners like you finish their first 10K in 60 days."

Problem, agitation and solution

  • Start with a pain that the audience already agrees with, such as a universal complaint, then introduce the product as the fix. Shoppers are more ready to buy when the problem feels familiar. [L1207.023]
  • Problem-then-solution structure: state a universal pain point in a short, easily agreed sentence, then introduce the product as the fix. Dramatizing the result with a vivid metaphor adds emotional weight. [L1210.010]
    • Bathroom cleaner: "Scrubbing tile is exhausting, so we made cleaning take seconds."
  • Problem-solution copy works well below the hero: name a frustrating problem, then present the product as the fix. Keep this section simple. [L1363.015]
  • Problem-agitation-solution formula: state the buyer's problem in clear terms they already agree with, stir up the emotions the problem causes such as anger, fear, guilt or embarrassment, then reveal the product as the answer with its benefits. People act more readily to avoid pain than to gain something. [L1370.062]
    • Backup service: "Your laptop can die tonight and take five years of family photos with it. Every hour without a backup is a gamble, and the losses hit hardest when you least expect them. Our service copies everything every night so a broken drive costs you a coffee, not your memories."

Objection handling

  • Address objections such as cheap knockoffs before the buyer raises them, for example by stating that imitations are not the same product. Objections such as cheap knockoffs or many competitors should be addressed on the page before the buyer raises them. Framing the brand favourably against competitors, for example by contrasting results, makes the brand look better in comparison. Handling the objection on the page removes a reason to leave. [L1209.023, L1210.015]

  • Objection-handling pattern: name the most likely doubt in a short statement that answers it before the buyer asks. [L1210.016]

    • Leather wallet: "Cheap wallets crack in months; ours is built to last ten years."
    • Electric toothbrush: "Most timers are inaccurate; ours buzzes exactly every 30 seconds."

  • Make the buyer feel the offer is fair and not too good to be true by answering the doubts up front. Skepticism often stops buyers who otherwise have the money and interest, so an unaddressed objection costs response even when the offer is solid. [L1370.089]

  • Answer objections in order: [L1370.090]

    1. State the direct answer clearly.
    2. Add a supporting testimonial, case history or short story that proves the answer.
    3. Restate or reference the guarantee or free trial offer.

    Use a FAQ page with the objections phrased as questions when that suits the format.

  • Objection-framing opener: admit that the offer converts only a small percentage of recipients, express personal concern that someone might miss out because of an explanation gap, mention how many customers praised the product, and then say you held a brainstorm to list the reasons someone would say no. Then list each reason and answer it. This turns objections into a sincere, helpful presentation. [L1370.091]

  • Guarantee terms should spell out what happens: the buyer returns the item at the seller's expense and receives a full refund, with no obligation during the trial period. Stating the terms clearly removes doubt better than general reassurance. [L1369.033]

  • Guaranteed letter: offer a small payment, such as $10 to $50, to any recipient who reads the entire letter and feels their time was wasted. The payout should be affordable, the message strong enough that the reader will want to read, and the total cost measured against the profit from the campaign. [L1370.083]

Emotional and logical reasons

  • Motive selection checklist before writing a sales letter: [L1369.122]
    1. Ask whether the product would make the buyer richer, healthier or happier.
    2. Ask whether it would raise his standing with the people he respects.
    3. Ask whether it would let him do, write or say something better than before.
    4. Ask whether it would protect his money, time or reputation from loss.
    5. Ask whether it would help the people he loves.
    6. Choose the strongest answer as the main motive and present it only from the buyer's point of view.
  • Only-some-can-qualify approach: present membership or access as an exclusive group to appeal to the reader's desire for recognition and status. This works especially well for premium memberships and premium card-style offers. [L1370.087]
  • Ego appeal: present the product or service as a status symbol by asking what it says about the reader if they lack it, for example asking why they have no fax number when asked for one. Ego drives purchase decisions alongside practical benefits, and the same appeal can be reused for new technology such as cell phones or websites. [L1370.088]
  • Lead with the buyer's pain and the promise second: a computer-seller sales letter opened by asking whether the reader hated their computer, listed the frustrations, then made concrete promises such as plain-English talk and a free thirty-minute consultation. [L1370.065]

Calls to action and reply devices

  • Tell readers what action to take and make it easy by providing a coupon, phone number or simple reply method. Readers forget decisions quickly, so reminders built into the ad improve follow-through. [L1261.129]
  • Set the offer's terms in the ad itself, such as the free sample, the deadline or the store where it can be picked up. Terms that are vague reduce response. [L1261.130]
  • Include a reply card with spaces for name, address and the item wanted, plus a stamped return envelope, so that ordering takes one action. Make the card easy to fill in and clearly marked as the way to order. [L1369.023]
  • Include a prepaid return card or envelope with each mailing and tell the reader to tuck a check or dollar bill into the letter for a fast order. Make it easy to pay or order with the same enclosure. [L1369.034]
  • Many business readers skip to the last paragraph of a letter to learn what it costs and what to do, so the hook that tells them the action must sit in that paragraph. A close needs two parts: the persuasion that shows the gain, the risk of delay and the guarantee, and then a hook that tells the reader what to do. [L1369.031]
  • Write the close in this order: [L1369.029]
    1. Tell the reader the exact action to take, using the reply card, the envelope or a specific phrase to send.
    2. Say what the reader gets by acting now.
    3. State the specific penalty for not acting, such as a lost price, lost stock or a lost opportunity, with a date.
    4. Give the means to act at once, such as a stamped envelope or a prepaid card, so no step is left to the reader.
  • Letting a prospect say "tomorrow" or "later" is one of the largest causes of lost sales, because a postponed buyer rarely returns. Give a concrete reason to decide now, such as a deadline or a limited quantity. [L1369.027]

Small commitments and trial steps

  • Most buyers resist a costly decision and want time to think, so a small commitment now makes the larger decision more likely later. Tell the buyer that the big decision can wait, and ask only for the small step. [L1369.025]
  • Ask for a yes on a minor point first, such as color, size, delivery date or a sample. Agreement on a minor point carries the main offer along in about three cases out of four. Make the minor point a clear choice the buyer can make in seconds. [L1369.026]
  • Supply the impulse in this order: [L1369.024]
    1. Identify the motive that makes the buyer want the offer.
    2. Choose a small first action linked to that motive, such as requesting a free trial, a sample or information, and make it cost nothing and oblige nothing.
    3. Make the next step easier than stopping, such as delivering the product with a trial period.
    4. Ask for the purchase only after the buyer has used or handled the product.

Direct-response sales letters

  • Four factors make a reader act immediately: they know and trust the sender, they like the product, they believe the urgency story and they find the premium exciting. Write these four factors on a card and check each one in every letter. [L1370.095]
  • Fortune-telling formula: make a specific prediction about the future of the buyer's market or situation, then present the offer as the way to prepare for or profit from it. People are fascinated by predictions and remember bold, provocative ones. [L1370.063]
    • Store owner: "Shipping costs in your niche will climb again next spring. Stores that lock in rates now will keep their margins while others cut prices. Here is how to lock in your rates before the increase."
  • Winners-and-losers formula: contrast two groups who started in the same place, one that acts on the offer and one that does not, and present the offer as the difference between them. It creates fear of being in the losing group and motivation to join the winners. [L1370.064]
    • Online course: "Two shop owners opened the same month with the same products. One reads the weekly pricing report every Monday and now holds a steady margin. The other guesses, and is still discounting to stay afloat."
  • Use a short question-based lead for a recipient who is buying something they already own or fear buying. Phrase the questions around the frustration and then state that you are on the reader's side. [L1370.066]
  • Sales sequence for a high-value product sold by mail or in person: [L1369.030]
    1. Send a series of informative circulars that show the product's lifestyle benefit without asking for a purchase.
    2. Invite the prospect to a special demonstration or trial with a set date and time, and include the spouse or partner.
    3. Let the prospect operate the product and point out its features during the trial.
    4. Offer a customized option that matches the preference the prospect expressed.
    5. Ask for the signature once the prospect has experienced the product.

Further details

  • Step 1: Research the subject thoroughly. Step 2: Write far more bullets than you'll need (at least 2-3x more than will appear in final copy). Step 3: Select the strongest, most intriguing bullets only. Step 4: Arrange selected bullets strategically, placing the most powerful ones at the beginning (front cover, headline area) and end (closing, call to action). Step 5: Use themed groups of bullets that hit the same topic from 2-3 different angles using different formulas. [L0068.030]

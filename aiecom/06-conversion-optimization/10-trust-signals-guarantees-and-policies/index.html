6.10 Trust signals, guarantees and policies

Trust as the core of conversion

  • Trust is the strongest driver of a sale, and most abandonment comes from perceived distrust. Typical causes are unclear shipping times, missing security signals, unclear return rules and a lack of customer reviews. [L1208.002]

  • Modern customers recognise untrustworthy websites, compare prices across competitors and hesitate to share their email address. Trust and retargeting are both needed to turn browsers into buyers. [L0257.004]

  • When a shopper has never heard of the store, trust is the main thing being sold. Every visible signal before checkout either adds or removes trust, and trust earned on the first purchase also decides whether the buyer returns. [L0355.023]

  • Trust comes from professionalism, not quick tricks. It is built through strong design, clear copy and excellent customer service. An online business must look as professional as an offline one to be trusted. [L0423.021]

  • Design and professionalism are underrated. A store with a clearly better look can win traffic from competitors with poor design. Professionalism covers details such as grammar and a visible phone number, while design covers visual appearance. [L0910.018]

  • Visitors lose trust when the store looks unhygienic, with errors, bugs, useless plugins or unwanted pop-ups. Trust also fails when branding is weak or design is inconsistent, when no customers or social proof are visible, or when no staff or communication channels are shown. [L0423.020]

  • A store that does not look trustworthy can lose buyers at checkout even when add-to-carts are fine. When people add to cart but do not buy, review the store's appearance and trust signals. [L0361.034]

  • In dropshipping, many visitors have not heard of the brand. Trust must therefore be built at once, using social proof (others already buying, reviews) and authority (press mentions, expert or doctor endorsements, notable customers). [L0918.024, L1363.003]

  • Risk matters most when the buyer does not know the seller. A new or unknown shop needs risk-reducing elements earlier and more prominently than an established brand. Place trust signals near the headline and the buy button, not only at the bottom of the page, because shoppers who doubt the seller will not reach the detailed sections. [L1209.034]

  • Trust signals fall into three types:

    • Authority: experts, vets, celebrities, respected magazines.
    • Social proof: large customer counts, ratings, reviews, testimonials showing that many others already buy.
    • Risk-lowering statements.

    Authority and social proof should appear at the top of the page, before product detail. [L0918.005]

  • Product-page trust elements that lift conversion include active social media accounts, customer reviews, social proof badges, bonus offers on high-traffic products, competitor comparison ("do better than what they do"), financing options, live chat and Lucky Orange session recording. (Older advice, 2022.) [L0249.031]

  • Trust signals such as authorised retailer badges do not produce an immediate jump in sales. They raise trust and conversions gradually, so expect a steady gain rather than a dramatic one. [L1050.006]

  • Repeat trust elements in the cart. Shipping, a return policy, a warranty and an active promotion help the shopper decide at checkout, so repeating them there is a cheap test. [L0389.009]

  • Trust elements to place on the page:

    • International delivery information.
    • Secure payment information and secure checkout statements.
    • Payment and security badges.
    • Customer service availability.
    • Easy return terms.

    Each one reduces a specific perceived risk. [L1209.012]

  • Shoppers reduce perceived risk when a page openly states international delivery, secure payment, responsive customer service and easy returns. If these points are missing, buyers fear unanswered problems, damaged goods or being unable to return an item, which lowers purchase likelihood. [L1210.008]

  • Use a trust banner with the usual dropshipping trust signals: international delivery, secure payment, fast customer service and easy returns. [L1363.010]

  • Trust elements and the risk each one lowers for the shopper:

Trust element Risk it lowers for the shopper
International delivery Doubt that the store ships to their country
Secure payment Fear that payment details are not safe
Fast customer service Fear of getting no reply when a problem occurs
Easy returns Fear of keeping an unwanted or damaged product
30 day money back guarantee Loss of money if the product disappoints (loss aversion)

[L1207.016]

  • Before a shopper buys, they often check whether the store is a real, active business. A store that cannot be found looks suspicious, so keep the store's basic footprint visible and consistent. [L0653.001]

Reviews and social proof

Why social proof works

  • Social proof works as a mental shortcut. Shoppers feel safer when they see many other people who are happy with the product, so show happy customers high on the page. [L1207.005, L1209.005]
  • Customer testimonials and review counts act as social proof. A high star rating shown together with a large number of happy customers builds trust, even if the testimonials are not verified. [L1210.003]
  • Social proof such as reviews, real customer photos and user-generated content should be shown. Even in dropshipping, use a professional product photo shoot and detailed descriptions so visitors can judge the product as they would in person. [L0390.026]
  • Customer photos and reviews work as social proof on the product page and increase conversion. They should be collected deliberately rather than left to chance. [L0417.012]
  • Before-and-after photos from real customers are a separate form of proof from social proof, because they show how the product works in the customer's favour. Use them alongside testimonials and customer counts. [L0516.037]
  • Show store reviews on product pages. Buyers spend more when they trust that the store will deliver the product. [L1115.069]
  • Repeat trust on the page: a star rating in the hero section, expanded with more testimonials and a count of five-star reviews further down. This gives the social proof more weight. [L0919.015]
  • Social proof includes reviews, star ratings, verified testimonials and third-party posts. Authority comes from press mentions, research-backed ingredients, expert or doctor endorsements and recognised review platforms such as Trustpilot. [L0919.014]
  • Combine several proof types in the same product copy, such as customer reviews paired with video demonstrations, or reviews paired with third-party testing results, rather than relying on one proof type. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0295.010]

Where to place reviews and testimonials

  • Show a selection of reviews near the top of the homepage, not hidden at the bottom, and add a clear link to read more. Social proof must be seen before the visitor scrolls far. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0409.012]
  • Show reviews from existing customers on the product page as soon as they exist, presented in an easy-to-read way. A brand-new product has no reviews yet, so this element takes time to build up. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0396.010]
  • Place a quoted customer testimonial near the top of the landing page. A photo of a happy customer with the product strengthens it but is optional. A short quote alone still works. [L0871.023]
  • A landing page must build trust fast, with a testimonial and a clear guarantee, before the visitor reads the full sales copy. Two things drive a first-time purchase: the offer and trust. [L0871.022]
  • A short quoted testimonial of one or two lines, with a small image and a five-star rating, works well when placed in the first scroll. It builds trust from the first visit. [L0916.019]
  • Social proof sections such as Facebook testimonials, Instagram stories and a Results section should be placed on the page to show real customer feedback. Presenting them in a format familiar to customers builds trust before purchase. [L1330.017]
  • A Results section can present social proof about the product and supports conversion when placed on the page. [L1330.021]
  • Trustpilot widgets can be placed on cart and checkout pages to show your rating as social proof. The lesson's example rating was 4.6 out of 5, rated 'Excellent'. [L0560.017]
  • Social proof placed next to the product and bundle choices supports quick buying decisions. Example: a fall sock product page showed a 4.8-star rating with over 7,000 reviews, a 'Fall & Winter Must Have' positioning and a visible 'Best Seller' label on the bundle. [L0347.022]
  • Typical trust elements on a sales page: a Trustpilot rating, a customer count such as 150,000+ satisfied customers, a 'featured in' row of recognised media brands, trust badges, and a line such as 'trusted by thousands' with testimonials beneath it. [L0919.016]
  • Recent-order notices, where a live message names a recent buyer, their state and how long ago they ordered, are social proof. Record them in the proof points column, together with how and where they are displayed. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0893.040]

Counts, ratings and what the number means

  • Use a specific, growing review count in the copy, such as '3,500+ five-star reviews', rather than a vague phrase like 'many happy customers'. Large precise numbers signal active, ongoing social proof. Place the count near the hero section or near the add-to-cart button. [L0919.041]
  • Once a store has a large review count, the exact live total adds little, so a rounded figure such as '10,000+' is enough. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0409.011]
  • Note the difference between the two count styles: rounded totals ('10,000+') suit a large, settled count, while a precise, growing count ('3,500+ five-star reviews') signals that the social proof is still active. [L0409.011, L0919.041]
  • Tell visitors in plain words what the review count means, for example 'people love us', instead of leaving them to interpret a number. Repeat a key message in a different format elsewhere on the page, but never repeat the same phrase in the same spot. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0409.010]
  • A quantitative example: a rating shown as 4.8 out of 5 stars from 1,000 happy customers. Both the average score and the number of reviewers help shoppers trust the product, and specific numbers build more trust than vague praise. [L1207.021, L1209.015]
  • A rating of 4.8 out of 5 from a thousand happy customers is a strong social proof signal. [L1207.021]
  • Customer totals such as 'over 40,000 customers' or '45,000 happy customers' must come from your own order records, not from a competitor's page. Update the number whenever your count changes so the claim stays accurate. [L0600.044]
  • A customer-count formula: 'Over [large number] [customers] and counting', placed near the top of the page next to ratings. It works because a big, growing number shows that many people already buy and trust the brand, which lowers the perceived risk for a stranger. Example 1 for a kitchen gadget: 'Over 250,000 home cooks and counting.' Example 2 for a skincare brand: 'Trusted by over 80,000 customers and counting.' [L0918.033]

Copy patterns and templates

  • Social proof pattern: a rounded count with a plus sign, the action 'bought' and a recent timeframe, which reads as popular and current. Two new examples: '250+ people bought this in the past month' and '900+ shoppers bought this in the past year'. [L0389.007]
  • A purchase-count threshold makes the social proof widget appear only when the number is high enough to persuade. A small figure such as '10 bought in the last week' can weaken trust, so set the threshold above that level. The example minimum used in the lesson was 150. [L0389.006]
  • Social proof text block for a product page. Paste the text and keep both variables unchanged. The variable $value is replaced automatically with the purchase count and $timeframe with the chosen period (day, week, month or year). Changing the wording is allowed, but both $value and $timeframe must remain in the text, or the widget breaks. [L0389.004]
$value+ bought in past $timeframe
  • Social proof pattern for a large number and a rating: one line with the number and rating, followed by a short plain-language sentence that says what it means. It works because a big number alone is not interpreted by the visitor, so the store should state the emotional meaning. Two new examples: '4.8 stars from 3,200+ riders. They ride further, and they tell us so.' and 'Over 5,000 five-star reviews. Customers say it is the best sleep they have had.' (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0409.042]
  • Typical copy of a shipping and social proof block is covered in the guarantee and policy section below. [L1310.043]

Collecting and checking reviews

  • Use reviews from real buyers only, even if the store is new. Writing product reviews yourself and presenting them as customer reviews misleads buyers and can violate consumer protection rules on fake reviews. [L1325.020]
  • Reviews that mention the quality of customer service are the most valuable ones to feature on the store. Excellent customer service ranked as the most important factor for consumer trust in a company in a Zendesk survey. [L0908.003]
  • A Zendesk survey reported that 88% of customers trust online reviews as much as personal recommendations from friends or family. Treat this as a vendor-sourced benchmark, not a verified figure. [L0908.002]
  • Testimonials and FAQ answers should come from real customers and real questions, not invented copy. FAQ items usually capture objections, so they are answered with factual reassurance rather than persuasive copy. [L0917.012]
  • Showing a store with few social media followers can still build trust, because visitors expect an active account. A page-likes campaign can help, but it does not replace real customer reviews. (Recorded 2024.) [L0965.083]

Guarantees and risk reversal

Why guarantees work

  • Risk reversal is everything a seller does to make sure the customer is not worse off after buying, such as a returns policy or a price match guarantee. Record every such element, since a returns policy is often the main form. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0893.020]
  • Money-back guarantees and hassle-free returns reduce the perceived risk of buying and build trust. Mention them near the call to action, because anything that lowers risk can increase conversion. [L0516.036]
  • A money-back guarantee with a clearly stated period, such as 30 days, reverses the risk of purchase. The buyer knows they can return the product and get their money back, which reduces fear of loss and increases the likelihood of buying. [L1210.017]
  • Risk reversal works partly because of loss aversion: people weigh a possible loss more heavily than an equal gain, so a guarantee that removes the downside of buying addresses that fear. State the guarantee period clearly, such as 30 days, so the buyer can see exactly what they would lose or regain. [L1210.028]
  • Shoppers fear losing money, so the page should reduce the perceived risk of buying with visible guarantees and protection, such as a money-back promise, so the visitor feels safe to purchase. [L1363.004]
  • Trust signals such as free shipping, a 30-day money-back guarantee and 'try risk-free for 30 days' near the top of the page remove purchase anxiety. The wording should make clear the company will refund an unhappy customer, which gives certainty rather than persuasion. [L0917.002]
  • A money-back guarantee tends to raise conversion rate because customers feel safer buying. Refunds may rise slightly, but the extra sales usually outweigh them. [L0683.011]
  • Trust badges and checkmark-style benefit lines add credibility to a product page when placed near the product or buy area. Only show badges and claims that are true for the store, because false credibility signals damage trust once a visitor notices. [L1380.055]

Writing the money-back guarantee

  • Always include a 30-day money-back guarantee for first-time visitors to a store. This significantly increases perceived safety and conversion. Include email contact in the guarantee section so customers can claim refunds. [L0321.006]
  • Most stores that only say 'money back guarantee' without detail are weak. A better guarantee names the risk-free period (for example 30 days), states what the customer gets back (a full refund) and addresses returns and replacements directly. [L0577.002]
  • Guarantee comparison from the lesson: a 60-day money-back guarantee was rated a medium-strength offer, and a generic 14-day guarantee covering only damaged or unwanted items was rated weak. A clearly stated 30-day risk-free period with full-refund wording was preferred. [L0577.078]
  • Competitor stores state a money-back guarantee with a clear time window, most often 30, 60 or 90 days. Put the guarantee next to the buy button and make the window and refund terms easy to find. [L0600.024]
  • Money-back guarantee copy to adapt for your own product. Replace the product name, and adapt the warranty length to your product. Do not copy it verbatim; rewrite the wording for your product and brand. [L0577.005]
Try the new [PRODUCT NAME] risk-free for 30 days. If you don't love it, we will simply give you a full refund. Our new [PRODUCT NAME] also comes with a 24-month warranty and no returns needed policy. If you have an issue with our product, we will simply replace it for you, no questions asked, and no need to return the item to us. The checkout process is guaranteed to be secure with Visa, Mastercard, Amex and Discover or PayPal.

[L0577.005]

  • Warranty lengths on guarantees: electronic products need at least a one-month warranty, while non-electronic products can use a shorter period such as six months or a one-year warranty. [L0577.006]
  • Show the supplier's warranty on the product page, for example a one-year manufacturer warranty, because it helps buyers choose between similar stores. [L0667.027]
  • Use a warranty only in the form the supplier actually provides, and state its length exactly as the supplier does. [L0667.027, L0577.006]

Where to place the guarantee

  • Show the money-back guarantee and refund guarantee clearly on the checkout page, and also on product pages in a collapsible section placed above the product description. This is especially important during high-sales periods. [L0387.007]
  • Place the money-back guarantee in several spots on the store: in a dropdown menu, within the product imagery and lower on the page. [L0577.007]
  • Place trust badges directly below the add-to-cart button. Use small payment icons (PayPal, Visa, Mastercard), a free-shipping message and a 30-day money-back guarantee. [L0916.020]
  • Add a trust badge to the logo or checkout area. Then use a fresh pair of eyes to spot simple UX bugs (price, add-to-cart button, compare price) that are easily missed. [L0640.042]
  • The four-column pre-footer trust row can show a free-shipping threshold, brand origin, fast and secure checkout and an easy-returns promise. Each column needs its own image and header, and a link is only added once the target policy page exists. [L1309.066]
  • Pattern for a Highlights banner block: a short bold heading that names one reassurance (shipping, returns, price or service) and one brief line below it that answers the doubt behind that reassurance. It works because visitors always ask why they should buy from this store rather than a competitor, so each block removes one objection. Use blocks only for guarantees the store actually delivers. Two new examples: 'Order Tracking' with 'Follow your parcel from dispatch to your door,' and 'Secure Checkout' with 'Pay safely on every order, with no card details stored.' [L1310.043]
  • A trust-element list for a product landing page: a 30-day money-back guarantee, payment badges, trust icons and star ratings, shown in a branded page layout with clear sections. [L1382.030]
  • Trust elements on the product page: a clean branded layout, a 30-day money-back guarantee, payment badges, trust icons and a star rating. [L1436.034]
  • Money-back guarantees and checkmark-style benefit lines work near the product or buy area. Place them where the buyer decides. [L1380.055, L1210.007]
  • Trust badges from well-known payment providers and security brands, placed near checkout, borrow the authority of those brands and reduce perceived risk. They should appear where the buyer decides to pay. [L1210.007]

Returns statements

  • Adding a free-returns policy statement directly below the Add to Cart button on product pages generates a +3.5% conversion-rate lift. [L0197.008]
  • Shoppers fear losing money if they buy and become dissatisfied with the product. Displaying the returns policy at the point of decision reduces this perceived risk and lowers purchase hesitation. [L0197.010]
  • A free-returns policy reduces the perceived risk of buying, because the customer knows they can send the product back if it is not right. Show it on the homepage right next to the free-shipping offer. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0409.007]
  • Show the return policy and shipping times prominently on the website, and display plenty of customer reviews. Buyers who distrust online stores, often after slow deliveries elsewhere, need these signals before paying. Reviews reassure them that others have bought successfully. [L1208.016]
  • Leaving delivery areas, customer service contact and return terms off the product page makes visitors assume the purchase is risky. Buyers who cannot find a return policy often leave instead of buying. State these three points visibly on the page. [L1209.033]
  • Clear, published policies act as trust signals and reduce doubt at checkout. Stating return windows, shipping times and contact details up front lowers the chance of disputes and chargebacks later. [L1250.015]
  • Short returns headline pattern: a no-cost signal, an ease word, the word 'returns' or 'swaps', and a plain permission to send the item back. It works because the buyer sees the risk removed within a few words and can scan it on any page. Examples: 'Free, simple size swaps: if it doesn't fit your room, send it back at no cost.' and 'Free, easy exchanges for any reason, with a full refund if you change your mind.' Only use wording that matches a policy you actually honour. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0411.037]
  • A returns policy visible only in the website footer effectively does not exist for customers, since only about 10% of visitors look in the footer. Any important policy must be visible on the page where the buying decision is made. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0411.034]
  • A friendlier return policy can increase returns, so track the returns rate alongside profit. [L0389.009]
  • Show a no-return policy on the product page when a product cannot be returned. Then the customer can be pointed to it if a refund dispute or chargeback arises. (Older advice, 2020.) [L0664.070]

Price match and price guarantee

  • Show the price-match policy on product pages, for example in tabbed product sections, and also link it in the footer. Competitors that offer it on the product page have an advantage. [L0894.026]
  • The price-match tab often seals the decision because visitors see they will get the best price. Showing it on the product page is therefore important. [L1130.026]
  • A price guarantee page makes buyers feel secure and increases conversions. It is worth having even though few visitors will ever claim it. [L1130.006]
  • Price-match guarantee template. Use it as the basis for your own policy, with the restrictions included:
We maintain competitive pricing as a priority. Within six months of purchase, if you locate the same item at a lower price from another retailer, we refund the difference. This policy also applies if our own website later shows a lower price for the same item you bought within that timeframe. Submit refund requests by emailing a product link from a competitor's site or our own within six months of your order date. Restrictions on this Price Match guarantee include: purchase must occur on our site; promotional items like rebates and BOGO offers are excluded; the item must be in stock at the competing retailer; competitors must be online-only (no physical store presence); discounters and auction sites (eBay, Overstock) don't qualify; the competitor must be an authorized retailer; and the guarantee covers item price plus shipping but excludes sales tax.

[L0145.006]

  • Show the price-match tab in the product page for trust, and show the same restrictions there so the guarantee stays honest. [L0894.026, L0145.006]

Shipping, returns and policy pages

  • Purchase friction increases when customers must guess about shipping, tracking, damage handling and refund eligibility. A clear Shipping & Returns page that answers all common questions before checkout prevents support inquiries and cart abandonment. [L0145.005]
  • A Shipping & Returns page must answer these six questions before checkout, or it risks cart abandonment. Every answer must be explicit and clear, because customers will not infer unstated details about payment timing, delivery speed or refund eligibility. [L0145.012]
    1. When will my payment be charged?
    2. How soon will my items ship?
    3. Will I receive a tracking number?
    4. How can I track my order or get delivery updates?
    5. What happens if an item arrives damaged?
    6. What is your return and refund policy if I don't like the product?
  • Make key business pages easy to find, such as About, Contact, Shipping, Returns and Price Match. Customers look at these before buying, and the more expensive the product, the more they rely on them to reduce perceived risk. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L1303.023]
  • An order-tracking page can raise conversion rate, because it shows visitors that the store is reliable before they buy. [L1299.026]
  • Phrase shipping positively on the site. Instead of 'free shipping', write 'we pay for shipping'. Instead of '10-12 business days', write 'we ship your item within 1-2 days' when the supplier ships within 24 hours. If delivery is slower, add a positive reason such as high demand and an assurance of tracking within 12-24 hours. [L1260.032]
  • Stating data-protection measures such as encryption and restricted access to payment data builds customer confidence. Keep these statements accurate, because they describe what the store actually does. [L1250.038]
  • Display the shipping carrier logos in the shipping section of product pages, where buyers look when they check delivery. Recognised carriers reduce doubt before purchase. [L1050.011]
  • Reduce a long row of trust badges to four or five of the most valuable ones, such as a money-back guarantee and express shipping. Too many badges overwhelm the customer. [L1225.031]

Payment logos, badges and seals

Payment methods and footer signals

  • Payment methods you accept (for example Shopify Payments or PayPal) work as trust signals when shown in the store footer, because visitors recognise the brands behind them. No special image is needed. [L1050.007]
  • Shopify adds payment icons to the footer automatically. They show which payment options a customer can use and help build trust before purchase. [L1326.012]
  • Tick only the payment methods your Shopify store actually accepts, because every ticked method appears in the footer as a trust signal. Showing a payment method you do not accept misleads visitors. [L1050.019]
  • Do not use large payment logos as trust badges, because they look scammy. Small, discreet payment icons look more professional and still signal that payment is safe. [L0916.021]
  • Display payment and security badges from brands you genuinely work with, such as McAfee Secure, Visa and Mastercard. Shoppers borrow trust from established names they already know. [L1207.015]
  • Show logos of well-known security or payment providers such as McAfee Secure, Visa or Mastercard to borrow their authority and trust. The buyer transfers trust from known brands to the shop. [L1209.013]
  • A local payment provider on the store builds trust with customers who already use it. In the lesson, the Swiss provider TWINT was shown, and only shops in Switzerland use it, so it signals a local store. [L0642.045]
  • Trust can be built by showing a recognised local payment-provider logo and a local flag on the store, especially in a country where customers know that provider. [L0650.032]

Badges and seals: what the evidence shows

  • Trust badges and credit-card icons below the add-to-cart button are optional now. They used to matter more and do not harm the store if used. (Recorded 2023.) [L1283.072]
  • Trust badges increase conversion, and studies show the origin of the badge does not matter. Even a stock image labelled as a trusted store performed about the same as a well-known security brand. (Older advice, 2020.) [L1111.083]
  • Any trust badge is better than none. A study found that even a simple self-made security icon raised conversions about as much as expensive trust badges, and TrustGuard was mentioned as a badge provider. (Older advice, 2019.) [L1002.032]
  • Split tests of trust badges showed almost no conversion difference between badge types. If you use badges, choose ones that signal an encrypted or secure store, such as the badges Shopify provides, instead of generic 'trusted' images. (Older advice, 2022.) [L0665.126]
  • Trust badges such as authorised dealer badges or a trust seal can add credibility. A trust seal should only be installed after the store has consistent sales for at least three to six months. Before that, the store lacks the data to see whether the seal helps or hurts. [L0910.022]
  • A free trust seal from an antivirus brand such as McAfee may reduce sales, because visitors may associate it with viruses. Check sales data before and after installing any seal, and remove the seal if conversions drop. [L0910.023]
  • TrustGuard offered a 60-day money-back guarantee, so a store can test whether conversion rates improve before committing to the monthly price. If it does not justify the cost, cancel and ask for a discount. In the lesson, the monthly price was negotiated down from a higher rate to about 15 dollars. [L0910.024]
  • Trust badges and credit-card icons: how the advice has changed.
    • 2019: any badge is better than none. [L1002.032]
    • 2020: badge origin does not matter. [L1111.083]
    • 2022: choose badges that signal a secure store. [L0665.126]
    • 2023: optional, harmless if used. [L1283.072]
    • Current rule: use badges that are true for your store, and check conversion data before keeping a seal. [L0910.023, L1380.055]

Charity, authority and third-party claims

Charity pledges

  • A charity pledge builds trust only when it names the actual organisation and shows its logo and information. Vague claims such as supporting abandoned dogs without naming a partner look shady to new visitors. [L0355.014]
  • Even a very small local nonprofit is enough for a charity pledge. Name it, show its logo and include information about it, so visitors can look it up and verify the partnership. [L0355.015]
  • A store that donates 10% of sales to a cause should state the 10% figure and name the beneficiary. The percentage alone, without a named partner, does not create credibility. [L0355.016]

Authority and press

  • Authority signals include mentions of major publications and expert endorsements. An expert endorsement that also contains a benefit statement from a relevant professional gives both authority and social proof at the same time. [L0918.028]

  • Press mentions, expert endorsements, notable customers and research-backed ingredients all build authority. Use each only when it is true for the store. [L0919.014, L1363.003]

  • Add third-party studies, reliable outside sources or a small self-run survey with transparent methodology to the page. Quote specific percentages from them, because statistics help buyers justify the purchase. [L1016.046]

  • A named ingredient or component backed by published clinical research can serve as proof in a feature list, because the buyer sees a recognised mechanism behind the promised result. Use such a claim only when the evidence is real and can be cited, since an unsupported 'clinically proven' statement damages trust. [L1210.027]

  • Claims such as damage reduction, frizz control or clinical-style wording need concrete proof on the page before they can convert. Such proof includes how the technology works, test data or guarantee terms. An audit should flag homepage claims without this support as a trust gap. [L0886.031]

  • Best-in-class claim pattern: formula superlative + category + location + measurable count or rating. It works because a bare superlative is disbelieved, while a verifiable number makes the claim credible. Examples: 'Most-reviewed sleep mask seller in Germany, with 4,800 verified reviews.' and 'Rated 4.7 out of 5 by 2,300 verified buyers across our pet bed range.' (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0411.038]

  • Third-party certifications from independent organisations (such as organic certification confirming production standards) serve as proof. Display the certification badge and the organisation's name in product copy. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0295.006]

  • A product tested and rated by an independent testing organisation can carry the rating and test results in product copy as proof. Example: Choice, an Australian organisation that tests products for effectiveness and provides ratings. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0295.007]

  • Customers assume sellers will make positive claims about their own products, so skepticism is natural. Independent third-party proof points confirm that an emotional purchasing decision is also rational, which enhances the persuasiveness of all preceding copy. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0295.001]

  • Featured-in logo bars often come from paid sponsored posts or affiliate placements rather than organic press coverage. Treat such logos as paid authority signals, and only display them where the placement is accurate and can be backed up. [L1348.032]

  • Do not fabricate press or media mentions. Show 'as seen in' logos only if the brand was actually featured. [L1363.011]

  • Press mentions shown as a marquee of logos, rather than a large block. Keep reviews visible and repeated on other pages. [L1225.034]

  • A low-cost alternative to paid press: get a review or feature from niche blogs or affiliate publishers, who then receive affiliate traffic, or create your own niche blog on WordPress that features the product. [L1363.013]

  • Linking out from the landing page to outside press articles is debated. It can add authority and help SEO, but it takes visitors away from the purchase path. Decide per store. The lesson's own stance was not to link them. [L1363.014]

  • Trust signals such as authorised retailer badges work best when added brand by brand, in this order:

    1. Add the authorised-retailer badge to the collection page of each brand.
    2. Add the same badge to the product pages of that brand.
    3. Add further signals (payment methods, carrier logos, memberships, charities, customer list) as they become available.

    [L1050.016]

  • Authorised retailer badges raise trust gradually, not as an immediate jump in sales. [L1050.006]

Phone number, social media and local cues

Phone number

  • Include the phone number prominently for higher-ticket items, with the header recommended. Most calls come from people verifying legitimacy before placing an order, not from rejections. (Older advice, 2020.) [L0268.032]
  • Display the phone number large on the homepage and on every page of the store, so visitors see it without searching. Making the number highly visible builds trust and, paradoxically, reduces how often people actually call. [L0910.009]
  • Placement of the phone number: the header is recommended for higher-ticket items (2020 advice), and the lesson's later rule is to show it on the homepage and every page. Both are compatible: the header covers every page, and a large number on the homepage supports it. [L0268.032, L0910.009]
  • A phone number whose last three or four digits spell a niche word, such as 333-MICS for a podcast-microphone store, adds credibility at no extra cost. Number-search tools show which vanity numbers are available. [L0910.010]

Social media as a credibility check

  • Before a shopper buys, they often check whether the store is a real, active business by looking for its Facebook page or Instagram account. Having both profiles and keeping them updated protects against the impression of a scam site and raises conversions. [L0653.001]
  • Social media is a credibility check rather than a sales channel. The profiles must exist and show recent activity, so visitors who look them up find signs of a live business. [L0653.002]
  • A survey cited in the lesson reported that 78% of respondents said a company's social-media posts affect their buying decisions. This is a secondary figure from a single survey and should be verified before being quoted. [L0653.003]
  • Brand social pages are mainly for credibility, not follower counts. First-time visitors often search the store name on Google, and active Facebook and Instagram profiles that appear in the results reassure them before they buy. [L0654.001]
  • The goal of the social pages is credibility, not going viral or collecting many likes. Visitors who look the business up should feel confident enough to place large orders. [L0654.022]
  • Post on the brand's Facebook and Instagram pages once or twice a week to show the business is active. The posts do not need big news; product highlights, new supplier announcements or short updates are enough. [L0654.002]
  • Post at least once a week, even if the post only links to a product in the store and gives a short reason why customers like it. This is an acceptable minimum for keeping the page looking active. [L0654.004]
  • Frequency of posting: the recommended rhythm is one or two posts a week (L0654.002), and once a week is the acceptable minimum (L0654.004). Use the higher rhythm when you have material, and never let the minimum slip. [L0654.002, L0654.004]
  • Do not create social pages, post once and then go a year without posting. A visitor who sees that the last post is a year old will assume the business has closed, which defeats the purpose. [L0654.003]
  • Weekly-post concept prompt. Fill in the placeholders and use it as the prompt:
I'm [your name], and the owner of [your store domain]. I need concepts for our weekly posts on Facebook and Instagram, covering the next 30 weeks. Each week, please provide one post idea that's ready to publish without edits, such as a product spotlight, a store product link paired with a reason customers like it, a brief business update, or a tip tied to [your niche]. Keep every idea short, appropriate for a small online shop, and aligned with our brand.

[L0654.005]

  • After saving a Facebook page bio, check the live page to see whether the text is cut off. If it is, shorten the bio, save again and recheck until the whole text displays under the profile picture and header. [L0654.026]
  • Services that artificially add likes and comments to ads do not directly improve CPM or quality ranking. Visible engagement can encourage real users to comment, which accelerates engagement growth organically and lowers CPM over time. [L0298.053]

Local and personal cues

  • Add a personal note from the staff or owner on best-selling product pages. It should explain the product's benefits in plain words, instead of relying only on generic feature lists, and be based on what reviews say customers like. The note builds credibility and reduces questions. [L0910.021]
  • Customers look for signs of a real business behind the store. Show who runs the store and how to reach them, because that answers the question of whether the seller exists. [L0423.020]
  • Sell differentiators that are true, such as a woman-owned, veteran-owned or surfer-owned status, free shipping, a price-match guarantee, a 24/7 customer service channel or secure checkout. [L0342.035]

Differentiator icons and honest claims

  • The 'why buy from us' icons in the pre-footer communicate what sets the store apart. They can show free shipping, a price-match guarantee, a 24/7 customer service channel, secure checkout or the owner's status. The Source A theme allows up to seven icons, but one to seven can be used. [L0342.035]
  • Use only differentiator claims that are true for the store. For example, show secure checkout only when the store runs on Shopify's checkout. Each claim needs a matching icon and a short label. [L0342.036]
  • A visitor who sees a claim the store does not keep will notice, and false credibility signals damage trust. [L1380.055]

Honesty limits

  • Do not pretend things are what they are not. Perception-building must stay above board, honest and trustworthy, which matters most in highly competitive markets or when reselling products that other sellers also carry. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0411.014]
  • Show only the real average star rating and the real number of reviews on a store. Do not edit widget text to display a higher rating or a larger review count than the store actually has. [L1299.021]
  • Generating fake reviews with AI and importing them into a live store to make it look established is deceptive. It can violate consumer-protection rules and app or platform policies, and it can put the store at risk of being shut down. [L1299.022]
  • Writing your own reviews is acceptable only when they are based on real experiences or on material the supplier has approved for use. Inventing fake customer stories or posing as different people is not allowed. [L0908.008]
  • Placeholder reviews for layout and QA only. This prompt produces test content and must never go live, be used in ads or be presented as customer proof. Use the prompt only for design and testing, with the output marked as required:
Act as a copywriter producing believable placeholder reviews, strictly for layout and QA work. Write 12 brief mock reviews, each one to three sentences long, for an item called [PRODUCT NAME]. Every entry needs an invented first name with a country flag emoji (for example Sarah 🇸🇬), a headline of one to five words, and a body of one to three sentences that refers to one concrete trait or advantage such as build quality, delivery, packaging, or outcomes. Put a star score from 1 to 5 after each review line. Vary the tone: some upbeat, some neutral, and one mildly negative. Start the output with the line "SAMPLE REVIEWS, FOR DESIGN/TEST ONLY. DO NOT PUBLISH." Each review goes on its own line in this pattern: SAMPLE, [Name] [flag], "[Headline]", [body]. ⭐️⭐️⭐️⭐️ Include no personal data and never suggest these came from real buyers. Keep everything invented and limited to layout or testing. These reviews must never go live on a storefront or into ads, and must never be used as genuine customer proof.

[L1299.023]

  • Published statements about the store's data and processes must be accurate, because they describe what the store actually does. [L1250.038]
  • Percentages such as 91 or 97 percent of users noticing a result need a published survey base: how many people answered and how long they used the product, for example 100 users over 90 days. A figure without this basis is an unsupported claim and can create advertising risk. [L0600.036]
  • Only show 'as seen in' logos if the brand was actually featured. Do not fabricate press or media mentions. [L1363.011]
  • Testimonials and FAQ answers must come from real customers and real questions. [L0917.012]
  • Trust badges and claims are only useful when true for the store. False credibility signals can damage trust once a visitor notices. [L1380.055]
  • Badges from a seal provider must be checked against sales data, because a seal that does not help can hurt. [L0910.023]
  • Use a claim such as 'clinically proven' only when the evidence is real and can be cited. [L1210.027]
  • Featured-in logos from paid placements must be backed up by the actual placement. [L1348.032]
  • The 78% social-media statistic and the 88% Zendesk review benchmark are secondary or vendor figures. Verify them before quoting. [L0653.003, L0908.002]
  • Do not quote the 78% social-media statistic or the 88% review-trust benchmark as verified facts. Verify them before use. [L0653.003, L0908.002]
  • Customer totals must come from your own order records, not from a competitor's page. [L0600.044]
  • Do not show payment methods you do not accept. [L1050.019]
  • Do not present a self-written review as a customer review. [L1325.020]

Further details

  • Social media profiles for ecommerce stores build visitor trust through proof of active business operations rather than through follower volume or viral reach. Prospective customers often search for your store name on Google before completing a purchase; active, professional social profiles appearing in both search results and linked from your website reassure first-time visitors that your business is legitimate and currently operating. [L0157.001]

  • The 'About us / our story' page converts poorly, with roughly 3% of visitors reaching it, but it should still exist for trust. [L0577.004]

  • Competitor stores show free shipping, a money-back promise and easy returns as trust badges near the product. Place these badges close to the add-to-cart button and state the exact terms. [L0600.025]

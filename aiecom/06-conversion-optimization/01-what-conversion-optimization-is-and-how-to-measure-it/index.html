6.1 What conversion optimization is and how to measure it

What conversion optimization is

  • Conversion optimization is the process of making changes to your store to increase the percentage of visitors who make purchases. [L0135.002]
  • A conversion is a completed purchase. Conversion optimization is the work of changing a live store so that more visitors become buyers. [L0583.002]
  • It means increasing the share of unique visitors who make a purchase, so it is about getting more of the people who already arrive at the store to buy. [L0582.001]
  • Traffic volume without conversion capability creates no business value, so conversion optimization is as critical to profitability as traffic generation itself. [L0136.003]
  • Conversion rate optimization increases sales and profit from traffic a store already receives, without paying for more traffic. Once visitors reach the store, it is one of the strongest levers available. [L0598.001]
  • Conversion rate is an optimization metric, not a success metric. Use it to find improvement opportunities and to check whether a change is positive, but never as the final measure of whether the business works. [L0400.001]
  • Conversion rate optimization is broader than page design. It covers traffic sources (cold paid social, search, email, retargeting), lead capture of contact details, and offer quality. [L0408.011]

Goals and the five areas

  • Conversion optimization requires work in five areas: site navigation and menu structure, product page design, customer communication methods, follow-up and transactional emails, and trust signals including social proof and urgency elements. [L0135.003]
  • The store's menus, product pages, contact and communication channels, and follow-up emails each affect whether a visitor decides to buy. [L0583.003]
  • The strategic goals are building visitor trust, increasing buyer confidence, accelerating purchasing decisions, and maximizing the return on future traffic investments. [L0135.005]
  • Changes should build proof, trust and urgency so that visitors feel comfortable buying now rather than later. These three elements are the main levers of conversion optimization. [L0583.004]
  • The main lever is removing friction from the buying process. Anything that makes a visitor hesitate, postpone, or doubt the site is a reason to leave, so each obstacle should be removed, and the simpler the buying process, the better. [L0582.009]
  • Urgency is one conversion strategy. Store pages must also contain enough information to answer the visitor's questions. [L0582.010]
  • Customer reviews add social proof and build trust. A social media presence shows that the business is run by real people and is a real company. [L0582.011]
  • Most online shoppers visit at least three competing websites before deciding to buy, so your conversion rate is directly competitive. [L0136.001]
  • Visitors who hesitate usually leave with one of three thoughts: they will buy later, the site may not suit them, or they are not sure what they want. Each thought is a reason to leave, so the store should answer them before they form. [L0582.016]
  • High conversion rates depend on targeted traffic. The best source is mostly Google, because those people are actively searching for exactly the product being sold and are very likely to buy. [L0582.014]

Conversion rate: definition, formula and benchmarks

Formula

  • Conversion rate = number of sales divided by number of visitors, multiplied by 100. Example: 2 sales from 100 visits is a 2 percent conversion rate. (older, 2020) [L1088.057]
  • Example of the target in practice: 100 visitors generate 2.5 sales, and 200 visitors generate 5 sales. [L0012.005]

  • The 2.5% figure is the benchmark for qualified paid traffic and the planning figure for a store. The 1% to 2% figures describe typical or early stores and older guidance. Use the higher figure as the goal once traffic is well targeted. [L0012.005, L0253.014, L0856.036, L1251.047, L0663.035]
  • For branded stores, the Source C range is 3% to 8%, rising to 9% to 10% as they grow. [L0871.029]
  • The rate is judged against AOV. A store with a high AOV can accept a lower conversion rate, and a store with a low AOV should aim for a higher one. [L1235.018]
  • A conversion rate under 1% across all traffic, including organic visitors, is not necessarily a serious problem. (older, 2020) [L0551.051]
  • A store that has been running for a while and still shows about 1% overall, with a 2% strong day, should be judged by its own trend. Benchmarks such as a 1% average mean little without the context of the specific business. [L0400.004, L1251.047]
  • The first sale usually comes after roughly 100 qualified visitors. If a store gets 300 to 400 visitors and no sale, look at where the ad spend goes and trim waste until about 100 qualified clicks are reached. (2023) [L0804.085]
  • A conversion rate near 100% is not a sensible goal, because visitors sit at different stages of the buying journey. A store that only converts visitors who are already ready to buy leaves no room for people who still need to get to know the brand. [L0390.005]
  • A 100% conversion rate is the theoretical ideal but is not realistic. A 2.5% target is achievable and is the benchmark to aim for. [L0582.005]
  • Judge the rate only with a real sample. Hundreds of visits per month on the product or segment are needed before drawing conclusions about the conversion rate. (older, 2021-22) [L1456.003]

Conversion rate, AOV and profit per sale

  • A conversion rate of 2% combined with a good AOV is a healthy funnel. In the lesson's example, two sales per day from $100 spend indicates a healthy funnel. [L1354.017]
  • Higher-priced products usually convert at a lower rate. A $3,000 product will almost always convert lower than a $30 product. (older, 2021-22) [L0400.011]
  • A low-priced apparel store that converted at 8% can still earn less total profit than a store with $2,000 products converting at a lower rate. (older, 2021-22) [L0400.011]
  • The real measure of successful conversion optimization is higher profit per sale and lower cost per sale, not the conversion rate number itself. (older, 2021-22) [L0400.015]
  • Customer acquisition cost: a store can be willing to spend up to 10% of the product sale price to acquire each customer, so budget accordingly when analyzing margin. (older, 2020) [L0268.031]
  • Average order value: the base estimate is $80, and the lesson estimates $80 to $90 before upsells. With cart upsells, cross-sells and bundles, AOV can be above $100. [L0871.029]
  • Revenue formula: Revenue = visitors x conversion rate x average order value. Example: 500,000 x 2.5% x $80 = $1,000,000 per month. [L0871.030]
  • Conversion elements have a large effect on revenue. With 1,000 visitors, a store can produce 10 sales or only 1 sale depending on how well its page converts. This is why trust and conversion sections are worth investing in early. [L1330.003]
  • Example from the Source C lesson: 161 store visits over a weekend with one sale is below the 1.5% target, since three sales would have reached it. [L0650.029]
  • The store in the lesson had a good conversion rate, because most visitors bought, and a reasonable AOV, supported by offers and bundles. [L1439.041]
  • Focusing only on the sale in front of you can cost the trust needed for repeat business. Aggressive selling at every step damages the relationship, and that relationship can be worth around ten times the first sale. Balance short-term conversions with long-term respect. [L0523.049]

The 40-40-20 principle

Core split and origin

  • Conversion probability can be split into three parts: about 40% depends on reaching the right audience, about 40% on the quality of the offer compared with competitors, and about 20% on how the offer is presented. The exact split is debated, but presentation is the smallest factor. (older, 2021-22) [L0408.001]
  • The 40% offer share is judged relative to what competitors offer in the same market, not in isolation. (older, 2021-22) [L0898.001, L0408.001]
  • The principle originates from early direct response mail marketing. It still applies to online stores because the channel changed but buyer psychology did not. Direct mail was the precursor to email and internet marketing. (older, 2021-22) [L0408.002]
  • In the framework, traffic is the first 40 and conversion rate optimization is the second 40. Getting the right visitors to the right page at the right time has a large effect on how many visitors convert, so it needs its own focus. (older, 2021-22) [L1392.001]

Where the rest of the probability sits

  • The classic direct-response form of the split is 40% targeting, 40% offer strength, and 20% design or presentation of the offer. It shows that 60% of conversion chances occur after the click, on your site. Targeting is the first 40% and determines whether the remaining 60% can work. (older, 2021-22) [L1507.007]
  • About 80% of conversion probability does not depend on how the website looks. A good-looking store with weak targeting or a weak offer will still convert poorly, while strong targeting and a strong offer can sell even with a plain page. (older, 2021-22) [L0408.003]
  • Website presentation covers how the offer is laid out on product pages: what the page looks like, how it is structured, and how the offer is placed on it. It is the smallest of the three factors, so refine it after targeting and offer are sound. (older, 2021-22) [L0408.010]
  • Roughly half of paid-traffic performance depends on what happens on the website after the click. A strong ad alone cannot compensate for a weak store. [L1031.031]
  • Once traffic targeting is correct, the conversion rate depends mainly on the website and not on the ads. When conversions are weak, 60% or more of such cases come from website problems rather than ad problems. (older, 2021-22) [L1228.007]
  • Conversion rate gains come from on-site changes. Traffic quality is the only ad-side factor that affects conversion, whether a store gets 50 or 500 visitors per day. [L1116.042]

Conflicting values and when each applies

  • Source B places 60% of conversion chances after the click (on the site) and 40% in targeting. (older, 2021-22) [L1507.007]
  • Source C places 60% of conversions in ad creatives (emotion and initial interest) and 40% in website optimization (copy, images and trust signals that maintain that interest). Website design maintains the emotional journey started by the ad. Use this split when deciding between ad creative and site work. [L0321.010]
  • Source A puts roughly half of paid-traffic performance on the website. Use this when judging paid campaigns, which depend on both. [L1031.031]

Common mistakes

  • Store owners often diagnose low conversion by redesigning product pages (colors, placement, removing elements), when the real cause is usually upstream in traffic targeting or the offer. Check audience and offer before spending hours on page design. (older, 2021-22) [L0408.004]

Why conversion is judged against your own baseline and paid traffic

Blended rates distort the picture

  • Blended store conversion rates include bot traffic, organic searches, and repeat visits, which distorts the true picture. Ignore blended rates when analyzing performance. (older, 2022) [L0272.020]
  • Blog and organic traffic lowers the blended rate without indicating a problem. Judge conversion performance from paid sources rather than the overall site rate. [L0941.048]
  • Bot traffic drags down overall site conversion rate, so the benchmark should be based only on paid ad traffic. [L0836.008]
  • Organic search traffic often converts at a lower rate than highly targeted paid traffic such as Google Shopping ads. (older, 2021-22) [L0400.010]

Analyze each paid channel separately

  • Analyze conversion rates by source: Google Shopping, Facebook Remarketing and Google Remarketing separately. These paid channels should hit 2.5% or more. (older, 2022) [L0272.020]
  • Baseline target of 2.5% for paid traffic channels (Google Shopping ads, Facebook Dynamic Remarketing, Google Remarketing). This is the minimum acceptable performance for these sources. (older, 2022) [L0272.019]
  • The baseline paid traffic conversion rate is 2.5 percent, meaning about 2.5 buyers per 100 visitors. Aim higher, but treat 2.5 percent as the minimum benchmark for paid traffic. [L0941.047]
  • Measure the conversion rate from qualified paid traffic (Google Shopping and remarketing campaigns) rather than from all site traffic. The target is about 2.5%, and it should be held roughly steady even when average order value rises. [L0836.007]
  • Judge performance from Google Shopping, Facebook retargeting and Microsoft Ads. [L0941.048]
  • Google Shopping thresholds in older material: aim above 1%, and if under 1%, optimize. Target above 1.5% as a baseline. (older, 2022) [L0665.057]
  • Google Shopping below 1% needs optimization. (older, 2020) [L0551.051]
  • Newer material sets the qualified paid traffic target at about 2.5%, so the 1% and 1.5% figures should be read as older trigger points. [L0836.007, L0665.057, L0551.051]

Paid traffic economics

  • When a business relies on paid traffic, its conversion rate must reach a certain level before advertising on that platform is affordable. This makes conversion problems much more costly. (older, 2021-22) [L0893.002]
  • Halving the conversion rate halves the maximum affordable cost per click. With a $200 target CPA, a 1 percent conversion rate supports a max CPC of $2, while a 0.5 percent rate supports only $1. (older, 2021-22) [L0446.008]
  • Conversion rate and the bid you can afford are directly linked. To bid higher while keeping the same profit per sale, the conversion rate must rise. If the conversion rate drops, existing bids can become unprofitable. (older, 2021-22) [L0446.009]
  • Raising the conversion rate is often the most direct way to pay for more traffic at the same margin. (older, 2021-22) [L1228.006]
  • A jump from 1% to 2% conversion rate, which halves cost per acquisition overnight, is possible but rare. Do not budget or plan a business around results like this. (older, 2021-22) [L1364.003]

Set goals against your own business

  • Set conversion rate goals only against your own business, since your traffic mix, niche, customers and website are unique. Focus on improving your current rate rather than chasing a fixed target such as 1% or 2%. (older, 2021-22) [L0400.012]
  • A new store typically converts around 2% to 3% at the start. This baseline should be tested and improved over time rather than accepted as fixed. (older, 2019) [L0663.035]
  • Meaningful conversion rate improvements take time and repeated testing. Large jumps rarely happen in the short term, so be skeptical of claims about secret conversion hacks. (older, 2021-22) [L0400.013]
  • Conversion rate depends mainly on three factors: traffic source, product price point and customer journey. [L0400.010]
  • Do not compare your conversion rate with other businesses or with numbers claimed on social media or at events. A single conversion rate figure says nothing about profitability, and some businesses with a million dollars in revenue were still unprofitable in a month. (older, 2021-22) [L0400.009]

Mobile versus desktop

Reference values

Benchmark reference (conversion rates as quoted for large online brands and general goals):

Metric Value
Average desktop conversion rate of large brands about 4%
Average mobile conversion rate of large brands about 2%
Average relative mobile conversion rate of large brands about 53%
Minimum goal for relative mobile conversion rate 50%
Long-term goal for relative mobile conversion rate 70%

[L0390.003]

  • Aim for at least 50% relative mobile conversion rate first, and work toward 70% over time. A lower mobile ratio is often normal up to a point, because many mobile visitors are early in their buying journey. [L0390.004]
  • Aim for a relative mobile conversion rate of about 50% of the desktop conversion rate. [L0423.005]
  • A weaker mobile rate can come from campaigns, seasonality, the media mix, or the mobile site itself. Separate these causes before concluding that the mobile site is the problem. [L0423.006]
  • Desktop converts at a higher rate than mobile even though most traffic is mobile. Shoppers often find the store on mobile and buy later on desktop, so plan retargeting for both devices. (older, 2020) [L0664.069]

Device behaviour

  • Mobile produced about one in three e-commerce sales, and mobile add-to-carts rose by about 25% year over year. Many shoppers switch devices before buying, so a mobile share of clicks does not necessarily mean mobile is the problem. [L0510.050]
  • Lower-priced products with short sales cycles tend to convert on the device where they were first viewed, often mobile. Higher-priced products with longer sales cycles usually involve several sessions and devices, often ending on desktop. [L0510.051]

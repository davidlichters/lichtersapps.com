18.19 Product, claims and advertising legality

Restricted and prohibited products

  • Start every product decision with this filter. Products that raise legal concerns face obstacles: payment processors are reluctant to approve them, and major traffic and advertising platforms restrict promotion, which limits how far you can scale. [L0031.004]
  • Do not sell or list products that require a doctor's prescription. Customers may submit fake prescriptions, and you could be held legally responsible if the wrong person obtains a prescription-required item. Avoid these products entirely. [L0255.071]
  • Do not sell firearms, other weapons, knives, swords, adult products, vehicles, drug-use equipment, tobacco products or gambling products. Google, Shopify and Facebook prohibit promoting these categories, which blocks most of your marketing. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0994.026]
  • Fringe products such as vaporizers, e-cigarettes and many supplements make it hard to keep a payment processor. PayPal and Shopify Payments can cut off your account when you sell them. [L0769.013]
  • The source does not claim to know whether these products are legal in every market. Check the legal status yourself before choosing a product. [L0769.014]
  • Paintball guns, bowhunting equipment, archery equipment, scuba diving tanks and hyperbaric chambers can fall under weapon, pressure-vessel or medical-device rules in some countries and on some marketplaces. Check the legal status and platform policy in the target market before building a store around them. [L0546.019]
  • Self-defense products, such as a keychain, can be treated as weapons in the UK and US. Check the weapons laws of every target country before listing. A product that is illegal to sell in a market cannot be marketed there, so verify this before building ads. [L0995.047, L0999.071]

Certifications, licensing and regulated categories

Compliance sequence for a regulated product:

  1. Confirm the required certifications for your country and category. [L0041.016]
  2. Work with your supplier, or hire a China-based testing agency to handle compliance testing. [L0041.016]
  3. Check licensing or extra requirements on official government sources in your country. Do not rely on forum guesses for licensing questions. (Older advice, 2021.) [L1279.082]
  4. Verify legal compliance for every target country before launch. [L0280.033]

Additional rules:

  • When moving from dropshipping to manufacturing branded products, secure FDA approvals for regulated product categories, create instruction booklets, and verify legal compliance for all target countries. [L0280.033]
  • Child-related products, such as car seats, cribs, bassinets, playpens, high chairs, toddler beds and baby rockers, are usually subject to child safety standards, for example federal crib and car seat rules in the US. Check the required certificates and labeling for the target country before listing them. [L0546.018]
  • Product claims for DIY, building or safety-related projects must be checked against local building codes and safety standards before publication. Add a disclaimer that projects must be confirmed with local regulations before work starts. [L0794.035]
  • Google and Meta restricted-category policies show which products need extra steps, such as disclaimers or policy agreements. Many restricted products can still be sold after those steps. [L0855.129]
  • California Proposition 65 warnings may be required on product pages. Ask your suppliers which warning wording they recommend. A warning can be shown as plain text on the site, or through an app that offered a single free warning. (Older advice, 2025 source; no age mark applies.) [L0837.044]

Health, medical and supplement claims

Claim rules:

  • Health or pain-relief claims, such as 'remove pain', 'make leg straight again' or other medical promises, will eventually trigger account suspension, even if sales occur at first. These claims violate platform policies. [L0209.039]
  • Avoid bold health claims in ad creative, captions and voiceover, such as saying a product will make you lose weight or cure a condition. Use softer phrasing that describes relief or benefit instead. [L0871.077]
  • Ad copy must not claim results such as 'clinically proven' unless there is evidence to back them. Unsupported medical or health claims in ads can get the ad rejected or create legal risk. [L1423.013]
  • Verify health or science claims with laboratory tests or qualified review before using them. Unverified claims can be refused by respected media or challenged. Keep the test records on file. [L1261.125]
  • Health-related product claims may need legal disclaimers. Ask the manufacturer for its compliance documents rather than adding claims without legal backing. [L0855.128]
  • Copying health or supplement claims from competitor pages creates regulatory and advertising risk, especially when the claims cannot be proven. Publish only health claims you can substantiate, and remove any claim you cannot back. [L0600.034]
  • For health and finance products, avoid health or financial result claims in headlines and page copy. Legal rules on what can be promised differ by country, and regulators can act against non-compliant claims. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0416.014]
  • Products in the health space allow fewer claims in copy, so the wording of benefits must be checked against those limits. Mapping still works for unfamiliar, technical or expensive products, but health-related benefits must be phrased with restraint. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L1160.013]

Safer wording:

Avoid Safer direction Ids
Detoxifying the body, releasing toxins, healing tissue Neutral wording such as 'a relaxing experience'; or have evidence ready and check each country's ad rules (older advice, 2021-22) L0465.022
Losing weight, curing a condition, removing pain Softer phrasing that describes relief or benefit L0871.077, L0209.039
'Clinically proven' without evidence Use only with real evidence on file L1423.013

Supplements:

  • Verify each product's ingredients with third-party identity testing to confirm the active ingredients are present. This is harder for botanicals, but it is stronger proof than a label alone. [L0334.047]
  • For US supplement ads and product pages, add a disclaimer that the statements have not been evaluated by the FDA and that the product is not meant to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease. Place the same wording on ad creatives and landing pages before launch. [L1164.054]
  • Health-related ebooks and supplement gifts should carry a note that the content is educational and does not replace advice from a qualified healthcare professional. Keep product claims soft and framed as support, not as a fix. [L0862.031]
  • Do not build character pages with fake medical authority, such as an invented doctor, for medical treatments, supplements or health claims. This can imply endorsement of unapproved treatments, may be illegal and may breach platform terms. Non-medical authority, such as a parent or an experienced hobbyist, is much safer. [L1476.004]

Income, finance and results claims

  • Income claims cannot be guaranteed, and promising returns to customers or prospects is not allowed. Describe only results you can measure. (Older advice, 2020.) [L0852.099]
  • For Meta ads promoting financial products to prospects aged 55+, fabricated testimonials, unverified star ratings, review counts and unattributed social proof create FTC liability.
    • Use only real, verified social proof with documentation, or leave social proof empty.
    • Fabricated evidence is an FTC violation, not a creative weakness. [L0221.014]

Reviews, testimonials and endorsements

  • Never copy reviews from a competitor's website, a competitor's supplier site, Amazon or any other online store. This is not allowed and can lead to legal trouble. [L0908.006]
  • Reviews imported from a supplier's marketplace and shown as your customers' reviews can breach consumer-protection rules against fake reviews, such as the US FTC rule in force since October 2024, and platform policies. Show only genuine reviews, or clearly label imported reviews as coming from another source. [L0476.036]
  • Attribute quotes, reviews or expert bylines only to real people who agreed to them, and keep every claim consistent with what the product actually does. Invented doctors or fake testimonials count as misleading advertising in most markets. The same applies to fabricated statistics or news-style sources. [L0578.038]
  • Do not publish invented testimonials, made-up customer counts or fabricated guarantees, such as '2,000,000+ happy customers'. These claims can mislead buyers and create legal risk under consumer-protection and advertising rules. Use only reviews and counts you can verify. [L1188.034]
  • Every specific claim must be true and provable. Readers take specific facts at face value, and the advertiser is accountable for them. Publish only figures that come from real tests or measurements. [L1261.124]

Comparative claims and patent language

  • Comparative claims, such as 'produces more than the closest competitor', need test results or documented evidence behind them before they go into product copy or ads. Keep the test report on file. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0465.021]
  • Use the label Patent Pending only while a patent application for that feature is filed and not yet granted. Do not describe a feature as patented until the patent has actually been granted. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0465.031]

Fake news sites, advertorials and disguised mailings

  • Use legitimate articles on legitimate websites. Never create fake news sites or fake blogs to disguise advertising. Regulators such as the FTC have acted against this practice, and it damages business credibility. Ethics and honesty protect the long-term business. [L0523.071]
  • Tricked or disguised mailings annoy some readers, especially in small markets where people talk to each other. Limit disguised mailings to targeted prospects or customers who will be interested once they open the mail. This reduces annoyance and raises response. [L1370.039]
  • Before writing any legal consequence into a letter, check the debt collection, consumer protection and fraud laws in your own country. Historical letters threatened criminal charges that may not fit current rules. Describe only actions you are legally allowed to take. (Older advice, 2019-2022 letters.) [L1369.103]

Giveaways, discounts and subscriptions

Giveaways:

  1. Write giveaway terms and conditions that satisfy the legal requirements of each country where entries are accepted. Adapt the terms per country. [L0797.009]
  2. Award the advertised quantity of product. The landing page and the ads must state that about nine out of ten entrants will lose, and the same disclosure must appear in the terms and conditions. [L0797.010]

Discounts:

  • Before launching any discount, check that it complies with the consumer-law rules of each market you sell in. For Australia, this means the ACCC requirements. Treat this check as part of launch preparation, not an afterthought. [L1404.033]

Subscriptions:

  • Undisclosed subscription terms on a product page lead to unexpected recurring charges, one-star reviews and refund requests. State the subscription, billing frequency and cancellation rules clearly before checkout. [L1164.018]

Shipping promises

  • Mention fast, insured shipping in product or search copy. In many countries, including Australia and the US, the seller must replace a shipment that goes missing even if the customer did not pay for insurance. The seller therefore carries that insurance cost anyway. [L0575.015]

General rule for every market:

  • Verify the import, licensing and tax rules for your specific product and country before selling. [L0963.157]
  • Check the legal status of each product in each target market before building ads or a store around it. [L0769.014, L0546.019]

10.2 Unit economics before spending: break-even, target ROAS and budget

Why the Numbers Come Before the Spend

  • Starting ads without knowing the target cost per sale usually leads to overspending and to taking home less profit than planned. Calculate the break-even and target CPA before the campaign spends money. [L0446.011]
  • The data you need before scaling paid traffic is the break-even and target cost per sale, per product or product group. Without these answers you cannot tell when a channel can take more budget or when it must be reduced. [L0446.020]
  • (Older, 2021 to 2022) Compare the actual CPA, ROAS and CPC of running campaigns with the break-even and target figures. The gap shows whether to scale a paid channel up, keep it, or pull it back. [L0446.012]
  • (Older, 2021 to 2022) Bid and budget changes must be checked against the target cost per sale and target ROAS calculated beforehand. Without these targets, bid changes are guesses, and guessing with ad money usually loses money. [L0744.011]
  • (Older, 2021 to 2022) Before raising bids or spend, check that the new bid is still profitable at the conversion rate you are actually getting. Assuming that more traffic will convert at the same or a better rate is risky and rarely holds. [L0446.010]
  • (Older, 2018) Most sales in this model come from direct-response Google Ads that send shoppers straight to a product page. Each product's ad spend must therefore stay profitable after shipping cost and merchant fees, or the sale should not be advertised in that region. [L0466.070]
  • When starting Google Ads campaigns, the primary objective is to buy data by identifying which search terms and products achieve your target 1% conversion rate before scaling budget. [L0059.003]
  • In an ideal scenario, each keyword receives at least 100 clicks and each product receives at least 100 visitors before evaluating scaling decisions. [L0059.004]

Break-even ROAS and Target Cost per Sale

  • Run Google Ads at a ROAS equal to or higher than your calculated break-even ROAS to avoid losses. Below break-even ROAS you lose money on ad spend even if revenue is generated. Running above break-even creates profit to reinvest or retain. [L0329.002]
  • Use the break-even ROAS as the reference for setting Google Ads bids, since ROAS is a column in Google Ads that helps decide how high to bid. If you use a paid advertising agency, give them this number as the target for optimizing your campaigns. [L1357.006]
  • (Older, 2021 to 2022) The goal of bidding by product is to set the allowed ad spend per sale based on gross profit, for example spending up to 50% of gross profit on acquiring a sale. Higher-priced products with the same margin can therefore justify higher bids. [L0742.023]
  • (Older, 2018) Some competitive niches need about $150 to $250 to get a Google Product Listing Ad campaign started. Budget for this before launching in a crowded product category. [L1274.081]

ROAS Formula and the 10x Benchmark

  • ROAS (return on ad spend) = revenue from ads / ad spend. A target of 10x means 100 dollars of ad spend should return 1,000 dollars in revenue. [L1279.059]
  • Maximum acceptable ad cost per order equals average order value divided by the target ROAS. Example: an average order value of 1,000 dollars at a 10x ROAS allows up to 100 dollars of ad cost per sale. [L0472.006]
  • (Older, 2021) For a strong Google Shopping campaign, target at least 10X return on ad spend within two to five days of the click. [L0469.077]
  • Target ROAS of 10x: every 1 dollar of ad spend should return at least 10 dollars in sales. This target applies after the first two to three weeks of the campaign learning phase, not on day one. [L0472.005]
  • (Older, 2021) Minimum ROAS threshold: maintain at least 10x return on ad spend to achieve profitability. Scale upward gradually while keeping ROAS above 10x. [L0270.009]
  • (Older, 2021) Keep at least 10:1 ROAS, meaning 10 USD back for each dollar spent, while raising the budget. Increase spend for as long as the ratio holds. [L1090.092]
  • For Google Shopping, the baseline target is a blended ROAS of at least 10x across all campaigns. Products priced too low for their cost per sale fall below this target and need optimizing first. [L0748.001]
  • (Older, 2022) The return on ad spend target for Google Shopping is at least 10x. If an agency runs category campaigns instead of Alpha and Beta, judge them only by ROAS against this minimum. [L0665.079]
  • The 10x ROAS target and the ad-cost rule of thumb (ad cost per sale equals one tenth of the order value) were given specifically for Google Ads. Treat them as a Google Ads benchmark, not a universal target for all ad platforms. [L0472.070]
  • (2023) The minimum target ROAS stayed at 10X. [L0804.067]
  • (Older, 2021) Goal is to achieve 10x return on ad spend, which should be attainable within a few weeks with minor campaign optimization. [L0270.008]
  • (Older, 2021) Maximum ad spend per sale = 10 percent of the sale price. Example: a $1,500 sofa allows up to $150 of ad cost to earn one sale. [L0469.090]
  • (Older, 2021) At a cost per click of $1, a $150 budget buys 150 clicks. If those clicks produce 2 sales, the ad cost per sale is $75. If only one sale results, the cost per sale doubles. [L0469.091]
  • (Older, 2021) Conversion rates and click costs vary by product, so use the 10 percent ceiling as a starting point and adjust bids with real campaign data. [L0469.092]

Campaign-Level ROAS Targets

  • Target ROAS for the Alpha campaign is around 15X (about $15 revenue per $1 ad spend), and for the Beta campaign around 8X ($8 per $1). Alpha bids are set higher because its keywords convert better. [L0743.006]
  • The ROAS figures for the two Shopping campaigns (about 15X for the high-converting campaign and about 8X for the broader one) are planning benchmarks for how much revenue each dollar of ad spend should bring back. They justify setting different bids per campaign. They are not entered as a target in a Manual CPC setup, where the bid is set directly. [L0743.027]
  • (Older, 2022) Google Shopping with about 5x ROAS should receive most of the budget. Facebook retargeting below 1x ROAS should be cut or stopped, since it overspends on people who are already reachable. [L0854.042]
  • (2020 example) For the 15x example in the scaling procedure, see the procedure under Scaling Rules. [L1111.112]
  • Source conflict: Source C gives a 3x to 4x target ROAS for a Google Shopping start with a $500 to $1,500 starting budget, while the Source A sources above set 10x as the minimum for Google Shopping. Both values are given here because they come from different sources and different setups. Use 10x as the profitability floor and check which setup you are running before applying 3x to 4x. [L1508.006, L0748.001]

Ad-Spend Ceilings: The 10% Rules

  • Set maximum PPC ad spend at no more than 10 percent of total monthly revenue. Example: $25,000 monthly revenue allows a maximum of $2,500 PPC spend, and $2,500 monthly revenue allows a maximum of $250 PPC spend. [L0257.015]
  • (Older, 2020) Cap ad spend at 10% of MAP per sale. For a $1,000 MAP product, the ad budget per sale should not exceed about $100. [L1111.099]
  • (2023) A sales budget of 300 dollars per day for about 1,100 sessions was given as a high but acceptable amount of clicks, if they convert. [L0964.069]
  • With an average order value of $1,000, you could spend up to about $100 in ads to acquire one sale. Even so, start with a $5 to $30 daily budget and let Google learn, rather than spending $100 per day. [L0808.031]
  • (Older, 2022) Use a 90/10 budget split: 90% of the ad budget goes to Google Shopping (alpha beta) and 10% to remarketing. Google Shopping typically performs better on new traffic than other channels. [L0854.040]

Price Thresholds ($200 Minimum)

  • Products priced under $200 are generally not worth running Google Shopping ads for, because reaching a 10x ROAS on them is very difficult. Use $200 as the minimum price for products that receive ad spend, with products near $197 treated as borderline. [L0748.006]
  • Lower-priced products are hard to make profitable under a 10x ROAS target because ad cost per sale must stay low. Shops that want paid traffic should avoid sales prices far below a few hundred dollars. [L0472.007]
  • Before setting up Shopping ads, list every product in the store priced above $200 and check which ones are not yet selling. Products already listed above the threshold are the first candidates to receive ad spend. [L0748.018]
  • Excluding unprofitable products from Shopping campaigns also reduces negative keyword work. An excluded product cannot be triggered by a search term and cannot spend money, so fixing the product set first removes a source of wasted spend before search terms need review. [L0748.019]
  • Starting a new Google Shopping campaign at a low daily budget, such as $10 per day, is acceptable. Low monthly spend alone should not be read as failure, because the cause may be bids or product prices rather than the budget. [L0748.020]

Margin Screening

  • Products without a 2X wholesale margin get organic traffic only. Ads should concentrate on products where wholesale is at most half of the sale price. (Older, 2022) [L0429.077]
  • Run ads only for products where the margin can cover ad spend. Do not advertise low-margin products; negotiate better prices or drop those products. (Older, 2021) [L0962.065]
  • Advertise every product where your margin supports the cost of ads. Remove products from suppliers that do not enforce MAP, because they will not perform. (Older, 2019) [L1275.046]
  • Products with a small margin should be excluded from paid campaigns when ad spend would be unprofitable, while high-margin products keep their paid campaigns. [L0666.042]
  • Do not spend ad budget on products where competitors sell at unprofitable prices, below your 2x wholesale minimum. Example: with a $500 wholesale cost, if competitors sell at $800 (20% below your $1,000 price), your ads would likely not convert against those cheaper offers. [L1146.011]
  • Check Google Shopping listings to see what competitors currently charge for the same product. Compare those prices with your 2x wholesale minimum before setting your sale price or deciding whether to run paid ads. [L1146.015]

What to do with products you cannot sell profitably against competitors

  1. List the product on your store at your full minimum price of 2x wholesale.
  2. Let it be found through free traffic sources rather than paid ads.
  3. Do not run paid ads for it while it appears next to competitors selling at unprofitable prices. [L1146.012]

MAP Screening

  • Run Google Ads only on products from suppliers that actively enforce MAP (Minimum Advertised Price) policies. Do not allocate ad spend to products from suppliers without MAP enforcement, as competitors will price below your cost and eliminate profitability. (Older, 2019) [L0243.048]
  • Products whose sellers price below the brand's MAP, which removes your margin, should be excluded from Google Shopping campaigns. Suppliers do not all enforce MAP, so check enforcement before advertising a product. [L0807.022]
  • For products with MAP restrictions, use asterisks and exclusions on the /promotions page to list brands that cannot be discounted. This satisfies supplier agreements while still running promotions to your full warm audience. [L0210.035]
  • If a MAP-compliant supplier also sells on marketplaces such as eBay, Amazon, or Walmart at MAP, you may list them on your site. Do not pay for ads to them, and rely only on organic traffic for those sales. (Older, 2019) [L1275.047]

Expected Clicks and Conversions

Clicks needed per sale

  • At 2.5% conversion, two and a half sales come from every 100 targeted visitors. [L0834.059]
  • A quality-traffic goal of 2.5% from Google Shopping means at 300 clicks you should expect at least 6 to 7 sales, possibly up to 10. [L0274.008]
  • (Older, 2020) About 100 unique clicks should produce around 2 sales within about 3 days at the 2.5% goal. [L1111.100]
  • Increase the Google Ads budget until the store gets about 100 targeted clicks per week, which should lead to roughly 2 to 3 sales. Count clicks across all campaigns combined. (Older, 2019) [L0356.048]
  • (Older, 2022) Traffic check: if this produces about 75 unique clicks per day at $1 CPC, with the 2.5% target conversion rate, the store can generate consistent revenue. [L0969.104]
  • (Older, 2022) Traffic consistency check: for a store to run consistently, roughly 75 qualified unique clicks per day at about 2.5% conversion is enough traffic in the beginning, combined with remarketing to recapture visitors who did not buy. [L0969.105]

Budget to clicks and sales

  • A daily Google Ads budget is roughly the monthly budget divided by 30. (Older, 2019) [L0663.006]
  • At $1 cost per click, a $33 daily budget buys about 100 clicks every three days. If the conversion rate is 2%, that produces about two sales in that period, which gives enough data to decide on scaling. (Older, 2019) [L0663.007]
  • With a $33 daily Google Ads budget, $1 cost per click, and a store that converts at 2%, a first sale is expected within about three days after the store, supplier approvals and products are set up. (Older, 2019) [L0663.045]
  • With $1,000 per month, $1 average cost per click, and a 1% conversion rate target, expect approximately one conversion every three days. This frequency provides sufficient data points to test and optimize before scaling spend. [L0059.002]
  • At $1 CPC and a 1% conversion rate with a $33 daily budget, you get roughly one conversion every three days (100 clicks over three days). [L1341.008]
  • With a broad Google campaign, a cost per click under $1 is realistic. For example, a $180 budget might produce about 200 clicks. (Older, 2019) [L1002.049]
  • (Older, 2019) A $3 cost per click is probably too high for most niches. Only a broad, very competitive keyword with huge search volume could justify a bid that high. [L1068.064]
  • Adjust the starting budget to the cost per click. With a $2 CPC the budget may be raised to about $60 per day. With a $0.50 CPC it could be cut to about half of $33, or kept at $33 to get more clicks and conversions sooner. [L1341.009]
  • Sales should appear within 1 to 3 days of a click if ads are optimized. [L0964.054]
  • (2020) Sales should show within a few days if the campaign is set up correctly. [L0967.081]
  • (Older, 2019) A first sale is expected within about three days after the store, supplier approvals and products are set up. [L0663.045]
  • (Older, 2019) A starting Google Ads budget of about $1,000 per month covers the entire product catalog, not each product separately. [L1069.078]

Learning-speed math

  • A lower daily budget takes proportionally longer to gather enough click and sales data for optimization decisions. A $5 per day budget needs six times as long as $30 per day to produce the same data (30 divided by 5 is 6). [L0855.002]
  • A budget of 15 dollars per day roughly doubles the time needed to collect enough data for decisions, compared with $30 per day. [L0856.015]

Conversion-Rate Benchmarks

  • Target conversion rate for Google Ads campaigns is 2.5 percent. Use this as a benchmark to determine if campaign performance is on track. (Older, 2019) [L0243.003]
  • Target conversion rate from Google Shopping ads is 2.5%, meaning two and a half sales per 100 targeted visitors. (Older, 2021) [L0834.059]
  • Google Ads conversion goal for the store is a 2.5% conversion rate. About 100 unique clicks should produce around 2 sales within about 3 days. (Older, 2020) [L1111.100]
  • A typical e-commerce site running Google Ads should expect a conversion rate of roughly 1% to 2%. A store with a lower rate such as 0.5% can still be profitable if its lifetime value or cart value is higher, so the acceptable rate depends on the business economics. [L1341.002]
  • As a rough benchmark, the buy-ready segment at the bottom of the funnel converts at about 1% to 3%. Check this figure against your own account data before planning budgets. [L0720.005]
  • Conversion-rate planning figures in the sources differ: 1% (target for a $1,000 monthly start), 2% (example store), and 2.5% (Google Shopping goal). Use the figure that matches your own account data, and treat 2.5% as the goal and 1% to 2% as the typical range. [L0059.002, L0663.007, L0834.059, L1341.002]
  • Traffic at a 2.5% target conversion rate should be checked against your own data before budgets are set. [L0969.104]

Scaling Rules

  • Start Google Ads with small budgets and optimize every two weeks. Starting with large budgets and letting campaigns run without tweaks is what causes thousands of dollars to be spent with zero conversions. (2024) [L0666.037]
  • Start Google Shopping campaigns with small initial budgets. Scale spending upward only as performance data proves profitability. [L0163.008]
  • Buy data first: the starting budget is for collecting data on which products get clicks and sales, not for scaling. [L1090.090]
  • (Older, 2021) Starting with a small budget is for collecting data: impressions, clicks and purchases show which products people find and buy. Use that data to add negatives and adjust cost per click per product, then increase budget once return on ad spend improves a few days after optimizing. [L1090.090]
  • Scale the budget gradually with ROAS above 10x, and only as profitable campaigns prove the ratio. [L0270.009]
  • Products with a strong MAP-to-wholesale spread and good conversion are the only ones that should receive increased spend. [L0966.108]
  • The scaling procedure for Google Ads budgets is:
    1. Set a target ROAS for the current budget, for example 15x on $2,000 spend.
    2. Improve efficiency until that target holds.
    3. Only then scale the budget, expecting ROAS to fall somewhat as spend grows. (Older, 2020) [L1111.112]
  • Increase the budget as long as ROAS holds; the ratio is the signal to raise spend. [L1090.092]
  • Add money in steps: after the first 2 to 4 weeks of learning, judge the campaign on the data and do not scale on day one. [L0808.029]
  • The learning phase for the ROAS target is the first two to three weeks of a campaign. [L0472.005]
  • Use the 2019 approach: scale up the campaigns that are profitable while cutting the ones that are not. [L0356.046]
  • Keep the test budget unchanged until it is profitable, apply the Google Ads optimizations, and only then scale the budget by reinvesting profits from existing sales. (2025) [L0667.001]
  • Scale spending for a product only after it proves profitable and reinvest store profits into ads once the free credits are used. [L0468.121]
  • The budget for the second half of the first month is raised only after conversions appear, not before. (Older, 2022) [L1113.056]
  • Budget can increase with a profitable ROAS, with no upward limit set in the 2023 source. [L0274.009]

  • The 2024 offers are: a Google Ads credit of $1,000 after $1,500 in ad spend, and a Microsoft Ads credit of $500 after $500 in spend, with both requiring spending by November 30. [L0360.044]
  • The Google Ads credit offer ($500 credit for $500 spend in 60 days) and the Bing offer ($500 credit for $250 spend) together can add up to $750 in free ad credit. This justifies spending at least the required amount during the launch window. (2024) [L0971.061]
  • Source conflict on the Microsoft or Bing credit: one 2024 source gives $500 after $500 in spend (L0360.044), and another gives $500 after $250 in spend (L0971.061). Use the terms shown in the offer itself. [L0360.044, L0971.061]
  • Source conflict on the Google credit window: the same $500 for $500 offer is described with a 60-day window (L0718.002, L0971.061), while the matching offer described in L0717.004 has a 30-day window. Check which offer type is shown. [L0718.002, L0971.061, L0717.004]
  • (Older, 2020) Google, Bing and Facebook ad credits offered at signup were worth about $200 to $300 in total for a new store. These are 2020 offers, so check current offers before relying on them. [L0468.120]
  • (Older, 2020) After the free ad credits are used, set aside a couple hundred dollars for ads and then reinvest store profits into ads. [L0468.121]

Bonus Offers and Conversion Cost

  • A bonus gift can lower advertising cost, not only raise sales. When the bonus removes the reason to compare prices, buyers purchase on their first visit instead of leaving, searching again and clicking a paid ad a second time, so the cost per sale falls. [L1293.036]
  • You pay for every click in Google Ads, including clicks from visitors who never buy. Irrelevant or poorly matched traffic therefore costs money with no sales in return. (Older, 2021 to 2022) [L1228.003]
  • Irrelevant and wasted spend is reduced by excluding unprofitable products and by adding negatives after data is collected. [L0748.019, L1090.090]

Further details

  • In Google Ads, the ad for a niche with a higher ad spend should show roughly 1,100 sessions for about 300 dollars per day; the question to ask is whether the traffic converts into sales, not just whether the clicks are high. [L0964.068]

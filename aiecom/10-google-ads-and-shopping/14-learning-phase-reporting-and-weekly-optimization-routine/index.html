10.14 Learning phase, reporting and weekly optimization routine

  • Every new campaign goes through the learning phase again, even when the advertiser has historical data from an older campaign. Past data helps a little, so it is better than nothing, but it does not remove the learning phase. [L0965.005]
  • Major setting changes or new campaigns restart the learning phase. Relearning is acceptable when the current data is mostly wasted traffic, because it is better for the campaign to learn from a correct setup than from lots of traffic with few sales. [L0837.024]
  • Multiple significant changes in quick succession restart the learning phase repeatedly, so avoid multiple changes during this window. [L0224.001]
  • After a large budget increase, or a restart, Google needs time to relearn. A large budget at restart can prevent efficient spending, so grow the budget gradually after each stable step. (Older advice, 2021.) [L1112.019]
  • If a campaign spends its full daily budget during the learning phase, the bids are sufficient. If the budget is not spent, raise bids to start getting traffic. [L1094.031]
  • After large bid increases, Google re-tests keywords, which can cause a short dip in sales. Judge the change only after enough new data has come in. (Older advice, 2023.) [L0470.067]
  • There is no fixed number of weeks a campaign must run unchanged. The time needed depends on how fast the budget is spent, so judge a campaign by its impressions rather than by the calendar. (Older advice, 2023.) [L0470.015]
  • Google Ads itself is low maintenance once set up correctly. Optimisation can happen every 7 to 15 days, or the account can run with minimal intervention, because the platform optimises itself. [L0296.015]

Time from Launch to First Data

  • New Google Ads campaigns can take up to one week to get approved, so give the account a few days before expecting data. [L0715.001]
  • After launch, ads may need up to five business days before they start getting impressions, while clicks and sales come later. [L0715.003]
  • Products approved in Google Merchant Center take 1 to 3 days to enter the auction and start showing impressions. Do not adjust campaigns immediately; give 1 to 3 days for data to appear. (Older advice, 2023.) [L0274.047]
  • New Shopping campaigns usually need about 2 to 3 days after approval before they start getting impressions and clicks. [L0750.002]
  • Once campaigns are approved, impressions usually start right away and clicks follow. For a new store, expect roughly one to three days from approval for traffic to start showing. (Older advice, 2019.) [L1068.063]
  • Once Google Shopping ads are approved, they show impressions and clicks within a few days, which shows which product pages to optimise first. Use this data before optimising the whole catalogue. (Older advice, 2021.) [L0968.064]
  • Give a new Google Shopping campaign a few days to level out before changing bids or budgets. (Older advice, 2020.) [L0852.065]
  • Alpha and beta campaigns that are structured correctly and have sizeable traffic should produce the first sales within about 2 to 5 days. (Older advice, 2022.) [L0854.044]
  • Launch with alpha and beta campaigns, let them collect data, then adjust keywords, bids and product pages based on that data. Do not judge profitability from day one. [L0856.003]

Conversion Timing

  • The conversion window is typically 1 to 5 days after the click. Users see the original ads and remarketing ads during this period before purchasing. [L0274.010]
  • The typical buying window for Google Shopping traffic is 1 to 3 days after the click. A campaign with about 100 targeted visitors a day should therefore produce at least two sales a day. (Older advice, 2021.) [L0834.060]
  • Once Shopping campaigns are optimised, conversions typically appear within about three days after the click. (Older advice, 2023.) [L0855.003]
  • Conversions may take up to about 24 to 48 hours to appear in Google Ads. Check the attribution model before judging a campaign's conversions. [L0510.026]

When Not to Change

Inside the Learning Window

  • During the learning phase, make changes to negative keywords rather than to budgets or bids, so Google does not restart learning. Negative keywords can be added from day one. (Older advice, 2023.) [L1094.032]
  • Do not start scaling Google Shopping ads immediately after launching campaigns. Allow 2 to 4 weeks for the learning phase and enough data collection before making major changes to bids or budgets. [L0193.001]
  • Do not change the campaign structure before at least 2 weeks have passed since the ads started getting impressions and clicks. [L0750.001]
  • Avoid drastic changes to bids, budgets or structure while a new Shopping campaign is still collecting data. About 71 clicks over roughly two and a half weeks is too little to judge conversion. [L0971.094]
  • New Shopping campaigns need time to learn, because quality score improves when visitors stay and browse several pages. Avoid drastic bid or budget changes early on. Raise CPCs only on the specific products that need more traffic. [L0971.057]
  • Test Shopping campaign changes about once a week and give each change a full week of data. Allow a two- to three-day learning curve after each change, so a change made on Monday and changed again on Tuesday resets the learning. Friday is an example change day for a store with low traffic. [L0856.016]
  • For a brand-new account, do not make structural changes during the first 2 to 4 weeks of the learning period. If the account spends money with no sales, lower the budget while you investigate. [L0857.006]
  • Do not make major changes before the brand-new account's learning period of about 2 to 4 weeks has passed. [L0857.005]
  • The first seven days of a new Google Ads account and store are not representative, because campaigns and product pages are not yet optimised. Judge conversion rate and spend only after the store has been optimised and enough visitors have arrived. (Older advice, 2022.) [L1092.110]
  • Do not judge a new account over a small sample. Four weeks with 124 impressions and one click is too small to judge conversion; judge over hundreds to thousands of visits per week. (Older advice, 2020.) [L1088.049]
  • Do not draw conclusions from data right after the holiday shopping season, because buying slows in the weeks that follow. Wait about two weeks into January before making decisions. (Older advice, 2020.) [L0551.048]

Sample Size and Decisions

  • Small click counts are not enough to judge a campaign. For example, 161 clicks or even 300 clicks is a small sample, and the next batch can change results completely, so avoid drastic changes until more volume arrives. (Older advice, 2021.) [L0801.064]
  • Do not make big decisions on a product until it has about 100 clicks. Before 100 clicks, only make small adjustments such as CPC changes. [L0941.068]
  • Wait until a campaign has at least a few hundred clicks or visitors, typically over about a week, before drawing conclusions. (Older advice, 2022.) [L0803.036]
  • Judge a Smart Shopping change by comparing a recent period with an earlier period of the same length, not by individual days. Daily results can swing widely while the overall trend still improves. [L0454.050]
  • Changes take a day or two to show in the data. Check after changes every few days, then weekly or every other week once the campaign is stable. (Older advice, 2021.) [L0469.082]
  • Google Ads data is only trustworthy after many more visits. With a small sample, wins or losses can be random. (Older advice, 2023.) [L0855.025]
  • For a new store with few sales, some sales may come from random causes that do not repeat. Look for a repeating ad source or keyword across more data before drawing conclusions. (Older advice, 2022.) [L0429.016]

Product Exclusion

  • Do not exclude a new Shopping product just because it has no sales after a couple of weeks. Decide based on click volume: about 100 clicks is the lower end, and 300 or more clicks gives a more reliable data basis, since conversion rates can still level out between 200 and 300 clicks. [L0666.001]
  • Conversion rates for a single product are highly variable in small samples. Early zero-sale results are often randomness rather than a broken product. [L0666.003]
  • A store with about 60 active products that has run Google Ads for around a month usually has enough data for significant optimisations, so waiting for more data is rarely necessary. Start with the troubleshooting steps for clicks without sales. [L0666.063]

Budget, Bids and Live Changes

  • For high-ticket stores, bids do not need daily changes because there is little benefit in it. With steady performance, bids can stay unchanged for months. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0744.009]
  • If a campaign spends an account's budget without sales, do not change bids on impulse; first confirm the campaign is set to spend the budget. A $20 daily budget that produces only one click in the first days is too little data to optimise from. (Older advice, 2021.) [L0834.126]
  • Changes can be made directly in a live Google Ads campaign. There is no need to pause the campaign first. Pausing only makes sense if you intend to stop spending. [L0972.060]
  • Changes should be made based on the data that exists, not on a fixed schedule. This may mean every 2 to 3 days or every two weeks. Changing dozens of campaigns from a daily export is far too extreme, though negative keywords are the exception and can be added at any time. (Older advice, 2023.) [L0470.017]
  • Google Ads optimisation score is not a reliable decision tool. Stores with low scores can be very profitable, so do not change the campaign because of that number alone. (Older advice, 2021.) [L1090.107]
  • Do not run new ad platforms during the low-competition window. Keep what works and raise budgets there instead. (Older advice, 2020.) [L0468.122]
  • Do not let a profitable campaign that is already making money sit untouched, because a campaign that looks stable slowly loses performance over weeks and months unless someone keeps actively improving it. [L0463.001]
  • Do not leave a Shopping campaign unchanged for weeks; review the data early and optimise sooner rather than later. (Older advice, 2023.) [L0855.004]

Columns, Dashboards and Reports

Core Metrics to Monitor

  • Monitor these key metrics to evaluate Google Ads performance: Impressions, Clicks, Cost, Cost per all conversions, Average CPC, Benchmark maximum CPC, Click share, Search impression share, Search absolute top impression share, Search lost impression share, and Benchmark CTR. [L0059.005]
  • Add these columns to Google Ads campaign reports: Cost, Conversion Value, Conversion Value ÷ Cost (ROAS), and Search Impression Share. These metrics guide all bid and budget decisions. [L0193.010]
  • Review Google Ads reports every week or every other week, and track whether impressions, clicks and conversions are trending up, down or flat. (Older advice, 2021.) [L0968.057]

Revenue Columns

  • In Google Ads, configure your columns to display revenue metrics. Navigate to Columns > Conversions and select both Conversion value over cost (your ROAS) and Conversion value per click (your revenue per click). These two columns are essential for bid optimisation. [L0299.009]
  • Conversion value and revenue are identical terms in Google Ads reporting. Your conversion value comes from your e-commerce platform's total order value tracking. [L0299.010]

Comparison Table for Ads

  • Impression share shows what portion of possible impressions your ads actually received. Check it once negative keywords and campaign segmentation are tight, because a 30 percent share means 70 percent of potential impressions are missed. (Older advice, 2021.) [L0968.059]

Impression Share and Competitive Metrics

  • To add Search Impression Share to a report, open Columns, then Modify columns for ad groups, go to Competitive metrics, select Search impr. share and Search lost IS (rank), then Apply. Search lost IS (rank) shows the share lost because of ad rank. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0744.042]
  • In the Modify columns panel under Competitive metrics, Search abs. top IS and Click share can be added next to Search impr. share and Search lost IS (rank). Search abs. top IS shows how often ads appear in the top position, which helps compare placement with share of impressions. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0744.060]
  • To check impression share and similar metrics, click Columns, then Customize columns, and add the column you need, such as impression share, to the campaign view. [L0805.012]
  • Customize columns lets you add metrics such as impression share to the campaign table to see how often an ad appears for search terms. [L0805.058]
  • Google Ads reports can break down performance by day and hour (Reports > Breakdown > Time and day) to see clicks and sales for each hour of each weekday. Campaigns can be scheduled for those hours. [L0971.059]
  • To see which search terms triggered clicks in Google Ads, go to Insights and reports and open Search terms. [L0471.030]
  • Keyword-level breakdowns show where impressions and clicks come from. Add irrelevant search terms as negative keywords, and lower the default cost per click for less profitable product groups instead of removing them. (Older advice, 2021.) [L0853.054]
  • In Google Shopping, setting up each product as its own product group lets impression share be measured per product, not only for the whole campaign. Adding more products does not automatically raise impression share. [L0666.062]

Product Identification

  • In Google Ads reporting, click Columns at the top right and add a product information column so product IDs show product details. The product ID in Google Ads matches the Shopify product ID, so the number can be searched in the Products list of the Shopify admin. [L0836.063]

Dashboards

  • Google Ads has a native Dashboards feature for free. Create a new dashboard (it starts blank), add widgets such as conversions by device, conversion rate by device, geographic performance by state or a campaign view, resize them, and click Save. Saved dashboards can be reopened at any time to check metrics and bid adjustments without navigating through campaigns. [L1236.004]

Analytics Custom Reports and Attribution

Building the Custom Report

  • When building a custom Google Analytics report for Google Ads, use the metric "Transactions" instead of "Conversions", because conversions may not report correctly in custom reports. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0256.012]
  • The Google Analytics dimension for the level below ad group is Keyword for search text campaigns. For Google Shopping campaigns the equivalent is Product group, because Shopping ads target products rather than keywords. The report template dates from when search text ads dominated. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0721.010]

Procedure: create a custom Google Analytics report to track Google Ads performance at the search-term level. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0256.013]

  1. Go to Customisation > Custom Reports and click Create Custom Report. [L0256.013]
  2. Enter Title: Google Ads Performance Report. [L0256.013]
  3. Set Report Type to Explorer. [L0256.013]
  4. Add Metrics by searching for and selecting each: Advertising Clicks, Advertising Impressions, Advertising CTR, Advertising CPC, Advertising Cost, Transactions, Advertising Cost per Transaction, E-commerce Conversion Rate, E-commerce Revenue, Advertising ROAS. [L0256.013]
  5. Add Dimension Drilldowns in order: Campaign, Google Ads Ad Group, Keyword, Search Query. [L0256.013]
  6. Add Filters: (Traffic Type = paid) AND (Source/Medium = google / cpc). [L0256.013]
  7. Click Save to populate the report. [L0256.013]
  8. Drill down from campaign through ad group and keyword to individual search terms to compare clicks, cost, transactions, conversion rate, revenue and ROAS. [L0256.013]
  • The custom Google Analytics report for Google Ads shows revenue and ROAS attributed to individual campaigns, ad groups, keywords and search terms. This is visibility the Google Ads interface itself does not provide, enabling granular performance analysis and optimisation at the search-term level. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0256.015]

Attribution Models

  • Last-click attribution is the standard tracking model. The most recent traffic source click within the same session (approximately five minutes of on-site activity) receives 100 percent of the conversion credit. Example: if a customer clicks an ad on Monday, watches YouTube on Monday, but converts via email on Wednesday, email receives full credit. [L0257.011]
  • When 100 percent of traffic originates from AdWords, the tracking system shows only approximately 60 percent of conversions as AdWords and 40 percent as Direct/Shopify. Attribution becomes increasingly fragmented as you diversify traffic sources. [L0257.012]
  • Assisted conversions in Google Ads show how many multi-touch conversions a campaign influenced, not just the final click. This gives a more accurate view than last-click attribution alone of a campaign's true contribution in multi-click customer journeys. [L0257.013]
  • Use Assisted Revenue in Google Ads to see the full attribution picture beyond last-click conversions. It reveals how multiple touchpoints across sessions contribute to each sale. [L0033.003]
  • Google Ads and Google Analytics attribution models: First click gives the conversion to the first interaction. Last click gives it to the last interaction. Linear splits one conversion across all clicks, producing fractional conversions such as 0.5 for a Shopping click and 0.5 for a text ad click. [L0510.025]
  • Check the attribution model before judging a campaign's conversions, because different models assign the same sale to different ads. [L0510.026]
  • Keyword attribution: to see which keyword produced a sale in Google Ads, open the keyword breakdown inside the campaign that brought the sale. In Google Analytics, conversion tracking shows which campaign sold the product, while organic sales often appear as non-set or not-set keywords. (Older advice, 2022.) [L0429.068]

Tracking Discrepancies

  • Shopping ads and Facebook ads are not the only traffic sources to track. Google Ads, Shopping and Facebook all over-claim conversions, so compare them against store analytics. (Older advice, 2021.) [L0247.067]
  • Compare Google Ads conversions with real orders. Sales can be attributed elsewhere if customers visit from an ad, bookmark the page and buy later, or buy from an organic search or email link. [L0359.074]
  • The search term report in Google Ads is not updated in real time, and its dates use the account's time zone (GMT-05:00, Eastern Time). Compare date ranges with that in mind, and do not judge a search term from the last day or two of data. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0880.028]
  • Google Shopping does not show every search term that triggers your ads. A campaign with 1,000 impressions may list only a few search terms, so the search term data is incomplete and cannot fully explain performance. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0879.031]
  • When checking whether an excluded Shopping product group still gets clicks, use a date range that starts after the exclusion. A longer range such as 30 days still shows older data. [L0836.029]
  • Check whether a cost-per-click increase has been applied to all product groups, because some products can be excluded from bid changes and stop getting traffic. Audit the account regularly, since errors can appear without warning. (Older advice, 2019.) [L0357.037]

Weekly Routine for a New Store

Procedure: weekly Google Ads optimisation routine for a new store. [L0715.004]

  1. Set a fixed weekday and time for the account review, for example with a recurring alarm. [L0715.004]
  2. Audit both the alpha and the beta campaign and note what is working and what is not. [L0715.004]
  3. Add new negative keywords to both alpha and beta. [L0715.004]
  4. Adjust bids for products that get too few impressions or clicks. [L0715.004]
  5. Once the account has 50 or more sales, switch the campaigns to Enhanced CPC. [L0715.004]
  6. When one brand clearly wins, move it into its own alpha campaign. [L0715.004]
  7. Exclude products or brands that are losing money or disappointing customers from all campaigns. [L0715.004]
  8. Products that get clicks but no sales go to conversion optimisation of the store. [L0715.004]

Daily Audit in the First Week

Procedure: daily Google Ads audit in the first week. (Older advice, 2019.) [L1002.044]

  1. Day one: check where your ads appear and which search terms triggered them, then add irrelevant terms as negative keywords. [L1002.044]
  2. From day two: check click-through rate by comparing clicks with impressions. An impression is one view of your ad. [L1002.044]
  3. If click-through rate is low, compare your prices with competitors' Google Shopping listings and check whether they honour MAP. [L1002.044]
  4. Compare your product images with competitors' images and improve yours. [L1002.044]
  5. Repeat the audit every day, aiming to have the main optimisations done by about day four or five. [L1002.044]
  6. Around halfway, expect visitors to add items to the cart and to use live chat or send inquiries. By the end, aim for the first sale. [L1002.044]
  • Do not wait for perfect data before optimising, especially if the store must be profitable soon. Even a small budget like $20 per day gives enough information to start. (Older advice, 2019.) [L1002.045]

Early Shopping Campaign Optimisation

Procedure: early Google Shopping campaign optimisation in the first days and week. (Older advice, 2021.) [L0801.061]

  1. Check which keywords produce impressions and which produce clicks in the Google Ads account. [L0801.061]
  2. Identify search terms and products you do not want to appear for. [L0801.061]
  3. Add negative keywords for unwanted searches in the Shopping campaigns. [L0801.061]
  4. Adjust cost per click by product, lowering it for products that get impressions but that you do not need to sell. [L0801.061]
  5. Let the data accumulate before making more changes. [L0801.061]
  • Once the account is running, a new Shopping campaign should be judged with a few hundred clicks, not a few. The early phase is about launching, collecting data and optimising from it, not about judging profitability. [L0856.003]
  • Shopping campaigns should be checked for impressions and clicks early, so that drastic changes are avoided and small adjustments can be made. [L0971.094]

Spend Audit

Procedure: to audit ad spend. (Older advice, 2022.) [L0803.034]

  1. Look at where money goes and identify spend that is not producing sales. [L0803.034]
  2. For products or keywords that get consistent spend, first optimise the product pages so more visitors buy. [L0803.034]
  3. If the pages are already optimised and visitors still do not buy, cut the ad spend sooner rather than later. [L0803.034]
  4. Move the freed budget into the products that sell. [L0803.034]
  • Regularly review search terms, product-level performance and bids, pausing or reducing spend on products and queries that do not produce profitable sales. (Older advice, 2023.) [L0430.007]

Keyword Review in Alpha and Beta Campaigns

Procedure: to analyse Google Ads keyword data in alpha and beta campaigns. (Older advice, 2022.) [L0803.037]

  1. Open the campaign and select Keywords. [L0803.037]
  2. Review impressions, clicks and the search terms that triggered each ad. [L0803.037]
  3. Add negative keywords for terms that will not lead to sales or are too broad. [L0803.037]
  4. Optimise the product pages that get clicks, to convert those visitors. [L0803.037]

Ongoing Optimisation Routine

Procedure: optimisation routine for Google Ads data. [L1078.003]

  1. Review the performance data and ask how deeply it can be broken down (by keyword, placement, audience, time, device or other segments). [L1078.003]
  2. Stop or cut what is not working, such as keywords, placements or ads that spend without producing results. [L1078.003]
  3. Improve and expand what is working, such as scaling budgets or adding similar keywords and audiences. [L1078.003]
  4. Repeat this review continuously. [L1078.003]

High-Ticket Management

  • For high-ticket dropshipping Shopping campaigns, only two recurring management tasks are needed: search term control through negative keywords, and bid and budget adjustment. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0744.004]
  • Shopping campaigns start producing sales once switched on, but ongoing management is where profitability improves. Each optimisation round tends to lower ad spend per sale and raise profit. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0744.001]
  • Optimising a store with about 60 active products after a month is a sound basis for making decisions. [L0666.063]
  • Shopping campaign benchmark for performance checks: the advertiser's threshold for investigating a problem is a return on ad spend below 10x. Check click-through rate, cost per click, clicks and conversion rate in Google Ads first. Only then look at Google Shopping for MAP violations. Do this roughly every two weeks with smaller budgets, or several times a week with larger budgets. (Older advice, 2024.) [L0805.018]

Holiday Campaign Preparation

Procedure: to prepare holiday Google campaigns. (Older advice, 2022.) [L1113.061]

  1. Schedule each promotion campaign weeks in advance with a start date and an end date. [L1113.061]
  2. Leave at least a couple of days of buffer so you can make edits if a campaign is not approved. [L1113.061]

Change History

  • Google Ads Change History shows all changes made to accounts with timestamps. Use it to understand what changed and when, and to roll back problematic changes if needed. (Older advice, 2022.) [L0249.060]

Benchmarks

Benchmark Principles

  • There is no reliable average click-through rate or click share benchmark for Google Ads, because results depend on the spend and how the campaigns are segmented. Judge performance by profit per campaign. (Older advice, 2019.) [L0356.047]
  • No universal impression or click benchmark exists, because results depend on the product, the brand and the niche. Judge performance against your own products and goals. (Older advice, 2021.) [L0469.079]
  • Judge a new Google Ads account over a sample of hundreds to thousands of visits per week, not over a few clicks. [L1088.049]

  • Click-through rate equals clicks divided by impressions. Example: 7 clicks from 800 impressions is about 0.9%, which is a signal to compare your listing with competitors' listings before raising the maximum bid. (Older advice, 2019.) [L0663.075]
  • A campaign with 35 clicks from almost 3,000 impressions has a click-through rate of about 1.2%. That is very low and should be investigated. (Older advice, 2019.) [L1002.048]
  • Google does not show a Quality Score for Product Listing Ads, so CTR becomes the main metric to optimise for those campaigns. Compare your CTR with the benchmark CTR of competitors and aim to beat it. [L1226.018]
  • If competitors have higher benchmark CTRs in Product Listing Ads, they are winning the Quality Score battle and paying less per click. Conversion Rate

  • Google Shopping and Product Listing Ads had an average conversion rate of about 2.3%, while about 97.7% of visitors did not buy on their first visit. These figures were cited in the talk and should be checked against your own account data. [L0510.010]
  • Use Google Shopping and Remarketing as the primary qualified traffic sources for conversion benchmarks, and treat their conversion rate target of about 2.5% as the reference. (Older advice, 2024.) [L0836.015]
  • Google campaigns should aim for an add-to-cart ratio around 7%, higher than Meta or TikTok, because search is intent-based. The landing page should match the keywords the buyer searched. [L1194.050]

Impression Share

  • Use the search impression share metric as a baseline to compare what the budget captures with the full market share needed to be seen by most eligible shoppers. (Older advice, 2019.) [L0357.038]
  • Impression share shows what portion of possible impressions your ads actually received. A 30 percent share means 70 percent of potential impressions are missed. (Older advice, 2021.) [L0968.059]
  • A share of 2% means 98% of eligible searches are missed. (Older advice, 2018.) [L1109.050]
  • Example: in a campaign generating about $69,000 monthly revenue, Impression Share sat at 32% instead of 66%, so the open question was whether better campaign work alone could double revenue. [L0463.003]
  • A Search Impression Share near 100% means an ad group already shows for nearly every relevant query its negative keyword list allows, so that targeting has no room to grow. More volume would then require relaxing the negative keyword list, which usually brings back irrelevant searches. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0744.055]
  • A reasonable early sample for Google Shopping is around 200 impressions. If impressions remain far below this with a normal bid and budget, check the campaign setup. (Older advice, 2021.) [L0834.064]
  • Impression share in Google Shopping depends on product quality and on which dashboard view is checked. Adding more products does not automatically increase impression share, but better-performing products improve it. [L0666.041]
  • To increase a low search impression share, add the keywords your products already show for and keep adding negative keywords. Better click-through rate then raises quality score, which brings more impressions at a lower cost, while raising the bid alone may not get more impressions. (Older advice, 2023.) [L0470.012]
  • Check the search impression share column once the account is set up. A share of 2% means 98% of eligible searches are missed; if impression share is very low, see the inch-up method below. (Older advice, 2018.) [L1109.050]

Diagnosing High Impressions and Low Clicks

  • When a Google Ads campaign shows high impressions but very few clicks, the most likely cause is that it appears for irrelevant or overly broad search terms. Review the search terms report and add the off-target terms as negative keywords. [L0856.121]
  • Shoppers who see the ad as less relevant than competing ads, or a search term that is not relevant, will not click. Protecting click-through rate protects quality score and lowers cost per click. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0744.038]

Procedure: handling high impressions but low clicks. (Older advice, 2021.) [L0469.084]

  1. Check the search terms triggering the product ad. [L0469.084]
  2. Add a negative keyword for every search term that is unrelated to the product. [L0469.084]
  3. If the keywords are relevant but clicks are still low, search the product on Google Shopping. [L0469.084]
  4. Compare the competing listings and identify what they do differently that makes shoppers click. [L0469.084]

Procedure: diagnosing high impressions with uneven distribution. (Older advice, 2018.) [L1109.050]

  1. In your Google Ads account, look at which products and search terms generate impressions. Look for uneven distribution, for example 600 of 1,000 daily impressions coming from one keyword or product. [L1109.050]
  2. Compare the click-through rate of the concentrated impressions with the rest. If the concentrated part is irrelevant and has a low CTR, that is the problem. [L1109.050]
  3. Add the irrelevant search terms as negative keywords. CTR rises because Google then shows the product only for better matches. [L1109.050]
  4. Search for your own products in an incognito Chrome window in Google Shopping to see your position against competitors. [L1109.050]
  5. If competitors above you have the same price, images and shipping, raise your bids and differentiate your offer to win higher positions. [L1109.050]
  6. Check the search impression share column. A share of 2% means 98% of eligible searches are missed. [L1109.050]
  7. If impression share is very low, raise bids across the board using the inch-up method: increase bids gradually, not budget, until daily spend reaches the ideal level, then keep optimising. [L1109.050]
  • Google Shopping does not show every search term that triggers your ads, so search term data cannot fully explain performance. Use it to find mismatches, not as a complete picture. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0879.031]

Case: established high-ticket Shopping funnel. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0744.054]

  • Starting point: a shared budget of $300 per day across three campaigns, high priority at a $0.30 bid and an average CPC of $0.26. [L0744.054]

  • Over 30 days the search terms report listed 6,971 terms, many of them generic and low-value. [L0744.054]

  • One campaign showed the Limited by budget status. Google's budget explorer recommended $370, a rise of about $70 that was judged acceptable for a profitable account. [L0744.054]

  • The alternatives were lowering the high priority bid from $0.30 to $0.20 to bring CPC to $0.20 or below, or adding negatives. [L0744.054]

  • Each review then covered only the date range since the previous check, and negatives were added to the master list so all three campaigns received them. [L0744.054]

  • Daily check for high impressions and clicks: look at the search terms, then add negatives for irrelevant ones, and check competitor listings. (Older advice, 2019.) [L1002.044]

  • Break down the Google Shopping campaign daily by keywords with impressions and keywords that spent money, add irrelevant keywords to the negative list every day, and raise the budget only once the account is profitable. (Older advice, 2019.) [L1068.059]

Procedure: daily improvement of the Google Shopping campaign. (Older advice, 2019.) [L1068.059]

  1. Open the keywords inside the Shopping campaign. [L1068.059]
  2. Review which keywords received impressions. [L1068.059]
  3. Review which keywords spent money. [L1068.059]
  4. Add irrelevant keywords to the negative keyword list every day. [L1068.059]
  5. Raise the budget only once the account is profitable. [L1068.059]

Diagnosing Low CTR

  • A low CTR affects the performance of the whole campaign, not the whole account. A few low-CTR products among many have little effect, while low CTR across most products in a campaign has a significant effect, though the exact size is unknown. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0879.023]
  • A Google Shopping click-through rate in the 0.3 to 2.7 percent range usually comes from three causes: very broad keywords that attract non-buyers, competitors or suppliers pricing below your MAP, or a bid so low that your listing sits at the bottom of results. Remove non-buying keywords and check pricing before raising bids. (Older advice, 2019.) [L1110.049]
  • A rate of 0.32 to 2.71 percent indicates a problem with keywords, pricing or bids. Aim for at least 3 percent. (Older advice, 2019.) [L1110.038]

Procedure: troubleshooting a low Google Shopping click-through rate. (Older advice, 2019.) [L1110.039]

  1. In Google Ads, open the keyword view of the campaign and find keywords with many impressions but few or no clicks. [L1110.039]
  2. Add non-buyer, broad keywords as negative keywords. [L1110.039]
  3. Search those keywords on Google yourself and check the shopping results. [L1110.039]
  4. If competitors sell the same product for less, check for pricing discrepancies or MAP violations. [L1110.039]
  5. If you rank near the bottom, raise your bid by about 20 percent, wait a few days, then check your position and click-through rate. [L1110.039]
  6. If the position is still low, raise the bid again. [L1110.039]

Procedure: diagnosing a low CTR on Shopping ads. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0879.028]

  1. Compare your ad with competitors' ads for the same product, looking at image, title, price, review stars and any promotion. [L0879.028]
  2. Check whether competitors are selling at a lower price across the board, which can explain why people click on them instead. [L0879.028]
  3. Check your position in the auction. With 10 or more advertisers, ads at the bottom get fewer clicks, so a small bid increase may move the ad into the middle of the pack and raise CTR. [L0879.028]
  4. Check the search terms your ad appears for to find mismatches. [L0879.028]

Procedure: diagnosing low click-through rate in a Shopping campaign. (Older advice, 2024; source Source A.) [L0745.004]

  1. Check whether prices are competitive. [L0745.004]
  2. Check whether the campaign shows for irrelevant keywords and add negative keywords after doing negative keyword research. [L0745.004]
  3. Run the target searches yourself and judge whether your product images are engaging enough. [L0745.004]

Procedure: diagnosing low CTR on relevant keywords. (Older advice, 2019.) [L0243.005]

  1. Log into Google Ads account. [L0243.005]
  2. Navigate to keywords section. [L0243.005]
  3. For keywords showing many impressions but few clicks (low CTR on relevant terms), check if competitors are violating MAP pricing, if your bid is too low, or if your product image is inferior to competitors' images. [L0243.005]
  4. Make targeted improvements to high-quality keywords before optimising or removing weak keywords. [L0243.005]
  • CTR of 2% or lower on relevant keywords signals one or more problems. Keywords may match irrelevant search queries, your product price may be higher than competitors (violating MAP pricing), your bid may be too low to place you high in search results, or your product image and description may be weaker than competitors. (Older advice, 2019.) [L0243.004]

Seasonality and Q4 and Black Friday Patterns

Planning Around the Buying Window

  • Google Ads optimisation for seasonal niches should be planned around the buying window of the season, because spend and bids that work in peak weeks can waste budget in off-season weeks. (Older advice, 2023.) [L0430.006]
  • Seasonality adjustment for a planned peak such as Black Friday or Cyber Monday: set an expected conversion rate increase, for example 25% higher, for the period. The strategy then bids as if the conversion rate will rise during that time. Use it for promotions with a defined start and end. [L0454.019]

Q4 Pattern

  • A sharp drop in click-through rate in Q4 is often caused by higher ad prices when big advertisers spend their budgets, which pushes your position down. Check first which products and keywords lost clicks, then compare competitors' prices, main images and shipping badges. (Older advice, 2019.) [L0356.053]
  • Ad costs typically rise during Black Friday and Cyber Monday. From mid-December to the end of December, ad costs often drop significantly as many advertisers pause, a window called Q5. Stores launched by Black Friday can use this period to buy traffic more cheaply. (Older advice, 2025.) [L1097.036]
  • For non-seasonal products, expect a sales spike around Black Friday and Cyber Monday, a weaker stretch in December, and a recovery in early January. Ad costs have historically dropped in that period, which makes it a good time to win extra sales. (Older advice, 2023.) [L0470.066]
  • Ad costs often drop in the last two weeks of December and through January, after large advertisers cut budgets following Cyber Monday. This can be a cheaper window to reach buyers. (Older advice, 2024.) [L0471.042]

Year-on-Year Comparison

  • Seasonal comparison for Black Friday and Cyber Monday: in Google Ads, use the date range selector to compare the same week (Thanksgiving through Cyber Monday) across two years, checking clicks, CPC and which products sold in each year. (Older advice, 2025.) [L0472.010]
  • Google can shift budget to different products over time, so a year-on-year review should check whether last year's best sellers still get clicks and page visits. Bids and CPC should be refined to send budget to products that convert. (Older advice, 2025.) [L0472.011]

Competitor Supplementary Campaigns

  • Holiday sales drops are often caused by competitors running additional short supplementary campaigns on top of their main campaigns. These extra campaigns raise auction prices for everyone, so the fix is not to change main bids alone. Plan promotional campaigns of your own, such as email to abandoned checkouts. (Older advice, 2025.) [L0472.012]

Holiday Timing

  • Do not draw conclusions from the holiday shopping season until about two weeks into January. (Older advice, 2020.) [L0551.048]
  • Schedule holiday campaigns weeks in advance and leave a buffer of at least a couple of days for edits. (Older advice, 2022.) [L1113.061]

  • New Google campaigns go through a learning phase that averages 2 to 4 weeks, during which Google optimizes them. Results from this period are not representative of final performance. [L1393.006]
  • A new Google campaign can take 2 to 3 weeks to learn and may get almost no clicks in that period. Do not judge a new campaign before that learning window has passed. [L1508.018]
  • Two to three weeks of data is enough to start optimizing a Google campaign. If there are no sales and no checkouts for a week after that, change the campaign setup. [L1285.053]
  • A new store with about 1,000 products on a $20 daily budget may need around four weeks for Google's learning phase, and some products may get almost no impressions during that time. [L1287.040]
  • The daily budget and cost per click together set how fast data arrives. Conversion rate uncertainty can stretch the learning time from 10 days to 40 days, for example when untargeted traffic converts at 0.25% instead of 1%. The timing of the first conversion therefore depends on the actual conversion rate of each campaign. [L1341.006]

Checking New Products

  • After uploading new products, Google Ads goes through a learning phase. Check new product performance at least weekly, sometimes every few days, and adjust budgets and negative keywords accordingly. (Older advice, 2021.) [L1279.064]

When Not to Change

  • Do not edit campaigns or account settings during the Google learning phase, because each change restarts or disrupts optimization. Let the campaigns run as configured until the learning phase is over. [L1393.007]
  • The exception is a campaign with no sales and no checkouts after two to three weeks, which calls for a setup change. [L1285.053]

Restarting After a Pause

  • Ads that were turned off and turned back on enter the learning phase again. Results can drop only if the campaigns had very strong historical performance before being paused. [L1283.027]

Reporting: Columns, Dimensions and Metrics

Columns to Add for Google

  • The same funnel logic can be applied to Google Ads: unit economics, cost per click, cost per purchase against target, and ROAS against break-even. The tracker itself is built around Meta metrics, so Google metrics need their own columns. [L1354.031]

Ad-Level Check

  • At ad level, check spend, rank, clicks, click-through rate and quality score together, because a weak ad can be spotted before it drains budget. In the example, a quality score of 4 against a target of 7 was a reason to pause that ad and introduce a new one. [L1236.013]

Dimensions to Split In a Dashboard

  • Additional dimensions worth splitting in a dashboard include desktop bid modifiers, gender, product IDs, search terms (to find converting queries) and headlines. [L1236.020]
  • Comparing headlines by click-through rate reveals which messages attract clicks. [L1236.020]

Combined Sample Size

  • When judging a campaign, add the clicks from all ad platforms you run, such as Google Ads and Microsoft Ads, to reach the total sample size. Optimize based on that combined sample rather than on each small platform total alone. [L1254.009]

Analytics Custom Reports and Attribution

Search Terms in Two Places

  • Review paid traffic for a product by checking search terms in two places: the search term report of each campaign and ad group in Google Ads (shows terms that produced impressions and clicks), and a custom Google Analytics report that breaks search terms down to individual product level. Use both where possible, or at least one. (Older advice, 2021 to 2022.) [L1456.008]

Decision Data for ROAS

  • Decide which products, ads or campaigns to pause, scale or highlight based on Google Ads data, not on Analytics or accounting totals. Use Google Ads metrics when the goal is to improve ROAS. [L1406.002]

Audit Routines and Cadence

Spend and Search-Term Audit

  1. Look at how much ad spend goes to each product. [L1448.006]
  2. Break spend down by dimensions such as search terms, age ranges, devices and products. [L1448.006]
  3. Identify search terms that produced zero revenue. [L1448.006]
  4. Remove wasted areas: age ranges that do not fit the target customer, devices that do not convert, and products that should not appear for a given query. [L1448.006]
  5. Proactively block the wasteful search terms as negative keywords. [L1448.006]

Campaign Hierarchy Audit

  • Review the campaign hierarchy and confirm all relevant targeting types are active: audiences, YouTube ads, display ads, Bing ads, Facebook ads and Gmail ads. Missing targeting types are unused reach, and checking them is part of a campaign audit. [L1448.007]

Impression Share Check

  • Check impression share: if it is low, the account is missing potential impressions. Frequent ad testing can raise click-through rate, which improves quality score and lowers cost per click, so the same budget reaches more buyers. [L1448.008]

Ongoing Optimization

  • Ongoing Google Ads optimization includes keyword expansion, bid monitoring and bid strategy adjustment with a ROAS target, adding negative keywords to block unprofitable spend, improving CTR, lowering cost per click through quality score, and reviewing costly keywords. Cutting non-productive spend is part of the same routine. [L1448.013]

Weekly Product Audit

  1. Look at cost trends per product: note which costs are rising or falling. [L1277.075]
  2. Look at conversions per product: note which conversions are rising or falling. [L1277.075]
  3. Work out what caused the change, whether good or bad. [L1277.075]
  4. Adjust bids, budgets or product groups accordingly; cut spend on products that have spend but no sales. [L1277.075]
  5. Add negative keywords for terms that trigger ads without sales. [L1277.075]
  6. Repeat weekly, or at least every two weeks for small budgets. (Older advice, 2020.) [L1277.075]

Diagnosing Impressions Without Clicks and Low CTR

Starting Point

  • A sub-1% CTR can be normal for a broad campaign, so judge CTR against how targeted the campaign is before acting. (Older advice, 2018.) [L1274.061]

Five-Step Procedure to Raise CTR

(Older advice, 2018.)

  1. Segment your campaigns into alpha and beta first, then add brand campaigns and best-seller campaigns. [L1274.063]
  2. Add negative keywords at campaign level, based on the search terms that show your ads but do not match your ideal buyer, such as free, guide or in-store purchases. The data is in your Google Ads account. [L1274.063]
  3. Check your bid: search your product in an incognito Google window and see where your listing appears. If you sit at a low position, such as 12th, raise your bid. [L1274.063]
  4. Check the keywords that give you impressions without sales, then search them in an incognito window to see whether competitors are breaking MAP. [L1274.063]
  5. Compare competitor ads for promo text such as free shipping, and for product images that look better than yours, then improve yours to match or beat them. [L1274.063]

Ad-Level and Headline Tests

  • At ad level, check spend, rank, clicks, click-through rate and quality score together, because a weak ad can be spotted before it drains budget. A quality score of 4 against a target of 7 was a reason to pause that ad and introduce a new one. [L1236.013]
  • Comparing headlines by click-through rate reveals which messages attract clicks. [L1236.020]
  • Frequent ad testing can raise click-through rate, which improves quality score and lowers cost per click. [L1448.008]

Low Impression Volume

  • For high-ticket products with no impressions, raise the bid in their ad group, for example from a default CPC of $0.40 to $0.45. (Older advice, 2025 source; new-store context.) [L1287.040]

Further details

  • Analyzing inconsistent monthly Google Ads spend (same budget, different sales): 1. Review data from best-performing month. 2. Check which products received impressions and clicks in that month. 3. Identify which products have higher conversion rates. 4. Adjust bids to direct budget to those high-performing products. 5. Remove unrelated keywords that attracted clicks with no conversions. [L0268.036]

  • With 270 products and $50/day budget, expect 6-12 months for full campaign optimization. Google's 2-week learning phase is just the beginning; ongoing optimization through bid adjustment, negative keywords, and product exclusion takes months. [L0276.061]

  • After a Google Ads account has been paused for a long period, such as 18 months, create new campaigns instead of reactivating old ones, because the old ones would re-enter the learning phase. [L0836.031]

22.21 Manage risk and fallback plans

Assess the Risk Before You Commit

Calculated Risk Versus Reckless Risk

  • Business requires taking calculated risk. Avoid reckless risk, but accept that some risk is necessary to succeed and to serve customers. [L0411.017]
  • A calculated risk is taken only after the concrete goal and the benefit it brings are defined, not for the thrill of the risk itself. Each bet should have a stated upside, a stated downside, and a mitigation plan before any money or time is committed. [L0863.001]
  • Putting everything on a single outcome, such as going all-in on one venture or one market, is gambling rather than managed risk-taking. A managed risk taker limits how much of the total is exposed to any one result. [L0863.005]
  • Risk tolerance should match your life situation. Avoid taking risks that threaten a mortgage or dependents, and only take large risks you can afford to lose. [L0780.035]
  • Build safety bars before spending heavily: start with a limited budget, test products, and move on if one fails. Spending a large budget on one product early leaves no money to test a second product and makes mistakes more painful. [L1374.019]
  • Credit cards only make sense for an established, profitable business that needs cash flow while waiting for payments to clear. They give room to leverage only when income is already coming in. [L1394.009]

Steps Before a Major Risk

Work through these steps in order before taking a major risk:

  1. Define the actual goal and what achieving it would give you. [L0863.002]
  2. Estimate the potential reward. [L0863.002]
  3. Estimate the potential risk, including the worst realistic outcome. [L0863.002]
  4. Decide how to mitigate that risk. [L0863.002]
  5. Write a plan B for the case where everything goes wrong. [L0863.002]
  6. Estimate how long it would take to return to your current situation if the plan fails. [L0863.002]

Test a goal with a worst-case analysis in this order:

  1. Define the upside if the goal is reached. [L0826.006]
  2. Define the downside if it is not pursued, such as illness, job loss, or a falling value of an asset like a house. [L0826.006]
  3. Define the worst-case scenario and decide which concrete action would prevent or reduce that downside. [L0826.006]

Compare the Status Quo

  • Staying in a stable job is also a risk, because raises are often small (about 3-8% per year in the lesson) while the cost of living rises by a similar amount. Compare the long-term trajectory of the status quo against the proposed change, not only the risk of the change. [L0863.003]
  • Treating a fully packaged, zero-effort option as risk-free ignores its costs. Heavily guided arrangements can cost several times more and remove flexibility, which is a loss of its own. [L0863.006]

Plan B and Fallbacks

Plan B Recovery Estimate

  • A plan B should answer the question of how long it would take to get back to your current life if everything fails. Typical recovery steps include finding another job, rebuilding savings, and replacing essentials. The answer is often much less severe than people fear. Making this estimate turns a vague fear into a measurable risk. [L0863.004]

Concrete Fallback Example

A realistic fallback for a failed life change can include:

  • Moving back in with family
  • Taking a lower-paid job
  • Buying a cheaper car
  • Owning fewer clothes

Listing these concrete downgrades before committing shows whether the worst case is actually survivable. [L0863.015]

Continuity Plan

  • Always have a continuity plan with a second destination in case the first one becomes unlivable. Without it, a sudden problem forces an unplanned move. [L1000.013]
  • Moving to one destination with no backup plan led to a rushed relocation when the air quality became unlivable. Plan the fallback before leaving, not on the day it is needed. [L1000.014]

Backup Sales Channels

  • Account suspensions and lost sales can be demoralizing for a one-person business. Plan backup sales channels in advance so one blocked account does not stop revenue. [L1214.057]

Time-Boxing and Exit Rules

Time-Boxing Uncertain Bets

  • Uncertain bets, such as launching a new content channel, should be time-boxed with a known fallback. For example, committing two months of full-time effort to a channel with no proven audience is acceptable only if the outcome of failure is survivable and understood in advance. [L0863.007]
  • Do not commit too heavily to one campaign type, because Google can phase it out. Keep your work flexible enough to move to another campaign type when one disappears. [L0489.006]

Cutting Losses

  • If a venture keeps losing money despite major investment, exit it early and take the lesson, rather than continuing to fund it out of hope. Cutting losses sooner usually protects the rest of your cash and your core business. [L1039.037]

Declining Niches

  • Set aside profits as they come in instead of reinvesting everything, especially in products that may fade. Avoid heavy stock purchases for a niche that is showing signs of decline. [L1173.040]
  • Track the active ad count for a niche over time to see if buyers still care. Emotional or problem-driven products can hold demand, but people's interest in a problem can shift quickly. [L1173.041]

Liability and Legal Risk

Liability Brainstorm

  • Conduct liability brainstorm sessions listing 100 potential ways your business could be liable, then solve the ones that can be fixed with one email. This principle draws on the idea that only the paranoid survive. [L0041.047]

Litigation Pattern Check

  • Use ChatGPT to identify the most common litigation patterns and risks in your industry. Common litigation fixes can prevent major legal issues before they occur. [L0041.048]

Quitting a Job and Leaving Security

  • Before quitting a full-time job, validate that the online business can be sustained as a side business, then resign. [L1441.003]
  • Company perks such as a car, pension and high salary can make staying feel easier than leaving, so plan the exit deliberately. [L1441.003]

Stretching the Comfort Zone

  • Keep stretching your comfort zone after the early hurdles, such as calling suppliers, are mastered. Operators who stop pushing themselves tend to stall in growth. [L1000.008]

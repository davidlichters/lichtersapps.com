22.23 Work with partners, family, team and delegation

Partners and spouses: agree before you start

  • Build an emergency fund before starting a business with a spouse, so the household can absorb losses or slower income. [L0829.001]
  • Discuss how much financial risk each partner is willing to accept before the business starts. [L0829.006]
  • Agree on finances and family responsibilities before launch. Money is named as the top reason for divorce, so treat the business as one part of the family plan, not a way to take from it. [L0832.002]
  • Both partners must agree on the vision and the big picture before committing. [L0829.011]
  • Share a long-term vision that covers income goals, the number of stores, and family plans, and confirm that both partners are on board. A mismatch on children or career can break the partnership later. [L0832.017]
  • Rent a separate office as early as possible, so work and home life stay apart. [L0829.002]

Partners and spouses: roles and fit

  • Each partner should understand their own personality type and the other's before dividing responsibilities. [L0829.003]
  • Each partner must be clear about their own strengths and weaknesses, so tasks go to the person best suited for them. [L0829.009]
  • Keep your ego under control and listen to the input of others, including partners, advisers and peers. [L0829.020]

Partners and spouses: communication and feedback

  • Communicate openly and frequently with your partner about progress, problems and expectations. [L0829.017]
  • When giving feedback to a business partner, balance praise with constructive criticism. Without this balance the working relationship suffers. [L0829.007]
  • Regularly notice and compliment each other's contributions to keep motivation high. [L0829.010]
  • Celebrate every goal and success as it is reached, not only at the end, to keep the team motivated. [L0829.013]
  • Keep a sense of humor in the partnership, especially during stressful periods, because humor reduces pressure between partners. [L0829.008, L0829.014]

Partners and spouses: boundaries, mood and rest

  • Keep separate hobbies and personal time outside of work. Constant togetherness at home and at work leads to friction, so respect that each partner has interests of their own. [L0829.005, L0832.011]
  • Protect work-life balance deliberately, so the business does not consume the whole relationship. [L0829.012]
  • Combine intense work periods with deliberate rest and fun, so that effort stays sustainable over time. [L0829.016]

Founder first: learn before you delegate

  • As a beginner, do not outsource immediately. Learn product research, store building and ad launching yourself first, then begin delegating. [L0280.037]
  • Do the full email-finding process yourself at least once to understand it. Then write clear instructions for a VA. Almost every step can be delegated, except choosing the topics and deciding which targets to pursue. [L0601.033]
  • Build a system and know every part of the business before delegating it. Only then can you judge whether a hire is doing the work correctly. [L1431.063]
  • Build a team early when you lack time, but give each assistant the tasks you understand yourself first, so you can judge the work. [L1432.025]
  • Running ads yourself gives full control over the data. Fulfillment, customer support, creatives and store building can be done with freelancers while ad management stays in-house. [L1184.059]
  • Use the time freed up by delegation for higher-value work such as product research, ad creative and offer development, not routine tasks. [L1358.025]
  • For delegation ideas and the mindset of handing off work, The Four-Hour Workweek by Timothy Ferris is a useful source. [L1360.005]

Outsourcing timing: when each rule applies

Sources give different starting points. Use the one that matches your situation.

Situation Action Source
Complete beginner, no store or ad experience Learn research, store building and ad launch yourself before outsourcing [L0280.037]
New store, more time than money Invest your own hours early in PPC to learn the setup and campaign mechanics, then outsource [L0463.007]
More money than time Pay a specialist to run the ads [L0643.060]
You have time Learn traffic generation yourself; this will be the most valuable skill for the business. Allocate about one hour a day to it [L0643.060]
Lack of time, team forming Build a team early, but give each assistant the tasks you understand yourself [L1432.025]
Still doing everything alone after about six months This signals you are not willing to hand tasks to someone better placed. Some solo hustle in the first six to twelve months is normal [L0701.009]
  • Doing everything yourself is the biggest confidence killer. [L0701.009]
  • Pay-per-click work becomes more time-intensive as spend grows. A new store with more time than money should invest its own hours early, to learn the setup and understand the campaign mechanics, before outsourcing. [L0463.007]
  • Do not take on challenges by making excuses or by delegating them away. Growth comes from doing the work, not from waiting for perfect conditions. [L0365.009]

Outsourcing decisions and emotional cost

  • Consider emotional cost alongside financial cost. If you hate managing ads enough to stop doing it, outsourcing justifies the cost even when strict hourly ROI math shows it is marginal. Preventing burnout and regaining mental energy have business value. [L0217.040]
  • Delegating work significantly reduces the owner's stress. This improves business longevity and lowers the risk that the owner burns out and closes, sells or exits a business that could have been kept. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L0499.005]
  • Outsourcing complements time management and productivity methods. An owner who does every task will eventually face more work than time allows, however well they organize themselves. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L0499.006]
  • Trying to handle everything personally, including pure distractions, leads to overload, however good the owner's self-organization is. Delegate tasks that do not need the owner. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L0499.007]
  • Done-for-you services offered by a course team are optional, and most cost extra. The course content alone contains everything needed. Paid help can speed up progress or cover tasks you dislike, such as website design, product uploads or market validation. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1496.008]

Systems before people

  • Get organized before hiring anyone. Have a system with a clear task overview, progress tracking and an approachable channel for questions. [L1431.014]
  • Begin each day by checking the task board for your own tasks and your team's tasks. The goal is that every card is done within its deadline and the store is running. [L1431.015]
  • Aim for a system that can be pressed to run, with a human checking the nuanced parts. Give AI the right training and a proven methodology, so it can run a repeatable process with only the necessary oversight. [L1478.011]

The owner ladder

Source B's owner ladder is older advice (2021-22). Its structure still applies.

  • Scaling sales without growing operations and finances at the same pace usually leads to burnout or business failure. Growth holds only when the back end can carry the extra volume. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1334.001]
  • An owner who still answers customer questions, fixes checkout or product pages, handles stock-outs or updates prices cannot scale. Leaving daily operations is the precondition for growth. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1334.002]
  • The owner ladder has five levels (Source B, 2021-22): [L1334.003]
    1. Doing all the work yourself. [L1334.003]
    2. Paying others to do it. [L1334.003]
    3. Managing those who do the work. [L1334.003]
    4. Managing those who work on the business. [L1334.003]
    5. At the top, only collecting profit distributions. [L1334.003]
  • The lesson set the distribution rhythm at about once per quarter. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1334.003]
  • Working in the business means performing tasks: customer service, website and product work at the base, and marketing such as SEO, PPC and email at the higher level. Working on the business means building a machine that runs without you, including hiring, creating systems and managing finances. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1334.004]
  • One person cannot run marketing, operations and finance at scale, so outsourcing work such as SEO and PPC is required. An HR manager can handle hiring, an operations manager can build processes, and a CFO can manage finances if the owner is weak in those areas. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1334.005]
  • Owners may keep tasks they enjoy and do well, such as customer calls or running PPC, as long as the choice is deliberate. The owner can sit anywhere between doing basic tasks and doing none, but not because they believe they cannot delegate. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1334.006]
  • The owner stays ultimately responsible for the business and should understand what each person does. Strong hires should then be trusted to do their work without constant checking, because over-supervising holds them back. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1334.016]

Hiring order and roles

  • Map all roles first. A solo owner fills every box of the company's role chart at once and switches roles all day. Mapping the roles shows which boxes the first hires should fill. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1334.007]
  • Do not look for a mini-version of yourself to hire. Give each hire one main role with at most one or two side roles, so that no one is stretched too thin. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1334.008]
  • Hire maintenance roles such as customer service and order handling before growth roles such as marketing. Growing sales into an unready operation multiplies problems: 10 times the sales means 10 times the problems. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1334.009]
  • Scale hiring one role at a time. Build product listers, then customer service, then operations. This prevents chaos and lets you perfect each function before adding the next layer. [L0263.049]

Capacity planning and staff budget

  • Once a business is in the growth phase, operating capacity rather than sales is the main limit on growth. Ask how many orders and sales the team and systems can absorb before tasks slip, such as supplier orders that are forgotten or customers chasing late orders. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1334.010]
  • Aim for about 80% capacity utilization, not 100%. Full capacity burns out staff and leaves no time for improvement, and running at the limit throttles growth. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1334.011]
  • In seasonal niches, save surplus in the staff account during the peak season to pay for the slow season. Avoid laying off people in slow months; give them other useful work instead. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1334.012]
  • Hire before you need the person, because recruiting and training take longer than expected. Allocate more to staff than the current need requires, so there is a buffer, since new hires need time before they add results. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1334.014]
  • Budget committed to staff should be spent on hires, not left idle. Money that sits unused while the plan says it is for staff means the plan is not being followed. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1334.015]
  • Hire into the staff allocation on a schedule, not on impulse. Check the budget against real revenue and remaining staff funds before adding any new role. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1334.042]
  • Plan each hire as an ongoing role that may last for years, not as a short placement. Repeatedly rehiring costs time and money and is one of the worst outcomes in team management. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1054.003]

Leadership, performance and trust

  • Evaluate leadership on two dimensions: Performance (skills, results on the job) and Trust (integrity, reliability, character). High performers with low trust are toxic and damage culture long-term. Medium performers with high trust are more valuable than high performers with low trust. [L0263.035]
  • Organizations over-measure performance (thousands of metrics) and under-measure trust (zero metrics), which causes them to promote toxic people. Reverse this: make trust equally important. [L0263.035]
  • Allow creative team members to be creative without micromanaging. Micromanagement prevents you from getting their best work and contradicts why you hired them. [L0260.023]
  • If you want to go fast, go alone; if you want to go further, go together. Lean on your team's perspectives and include diverse viewpoints when developing brand strategy. [L0260.022]

Treating staff with respect

  • Even without a formal employment contract, once someone works for you, you are their employer, and they experience the relationship that way. Treat outsourced staff as you would a local employee, with support and development. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1054.002]
  • Many owners fail at outsourcing because they value a remote assistant less when their pay is low compared with local wages in their own country. That attitude leads to weak effort, poor results and little success with outsourced staff. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1054.001]
  • Greet your team at the start of each day, even when you have no task to discuss. Ignoring staff creates the same poor impression as a boss who walks past the team without acknowledging anyone. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1054.021]
  • Hold performance reviews that are positive and focused on development. Name what the person does well, then identify weaknesses together and ask how the work could be improved and what they need. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1054.027]
  • Harsh threats, such as telling someone to fix their act or be fired tomorrow, do not produce improvement. The person never gets a real chance to explain what they find difficult. Give them a chance and work with them using empathy. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1054.028]
  • Apply empathy in every interaction with staff. Imagine being in their position and ask whether you would be happy with how you treat them. If the answer is no, change your approach. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1054.040]
  • As soon as you hire staff, you are a leader, and your example sets the tone for the team. A poor manager cannot expect good work from the team, so treat people with the respect you would want yourself. (Older advice, Source B, 2021-22.) [L1054.041]
  • Treat other members with respect and avoid bickering, because conflict does not help results. Address problems that disrupt the community directly, and keep the focus on positivity, helping others and results. [L0365.011]
  • Success is a team effort, so no single person is responsible for it and every contribution matters. Do not look down on others; everyone is valued equally regardless of their stage. [L0365.012]

Honesty and values in the business

  • Do not misrepresent your experience, skills or how long you have been doing business. This applies to customers, suppliers and community members alike. [L0365.004]
  • Lying about your skills, experience or track record to sell products or to create extra income in a community is a serious mistake. Such misrepresentation tends to be discovered, and those involved can be removed from the community. [L0365.005]
  • Handle every business issue with honesty and transparency, both with colleagues and with customers. Ethical integrity is the highest standard for long-term success. [L0365.008]
  • Stay humble and keep learning, because there is always a way to improve regardless of revenue level. Track changes in marketing continuously and share what works. [L0365.010]

Family and life outside the business

  • Schedule regular calls with family back home, including grandparents, so children stay connected and relatives are not left out. Plan activities together to make the time away worthwhile. [L1000.016]
  • Keeping store phone duty while living in a different time zone meant waking at 4:30 a.m. every day to take calls. Hand over phone handling to a trained assistant before a long trip abroad. [L1000.017]

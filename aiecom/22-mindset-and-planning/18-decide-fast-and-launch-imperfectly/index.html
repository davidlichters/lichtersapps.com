22.18 Decide fast and launch imperfectly

Overthinking and analysis paralysis

  • Overthinking paralyses execution. Think less and do more, rely on coaches, and solve problems as they arise during the work instead of planning everything perfectly upfront. [L0037.001]
  • Analysis paralysis is best broken by taking action, even with an imperfect first version, because things can be corrected once they are live. (Older, 2022.) [L1092.106]
  • Progress comes from moving forward and correcting mistakes along the way, which works better than waiting for a perfect first attempt. Making mistakes is normal in business. (Older, 2022.) [L1092.105]
  • Business decisions are made from current knowledge plus instinct. Waiting for 100% confidence before acting is a common trap because that confidence never arrives. Move forward with what you know and be ready to pivot when results show a different path. (Older, 2020.) [L0358.013]
  • Overthinking the niche choice holds many beginners back. Pick a niche that passes the criteria and start the work rather than waiting for certainty. (Older, 2021.) [L0962.138]
  • Overthinking a product costs money and loses opportunities. Choose products with proof of sales, run ads, and let the Facebook data decide instead of your own assumptions. [L1161.045]
  • Overanalysing the niche decision makes people go in circles and doubt their own reasoning. For a first business, this lack of progress can keep people stuck for months. (Older, 2021-22.) [L1059.005]
  • Searching for the perfect niche, product or customer leads to endless second-guessing, sometimes lasting weeks or months. This delay causes frustration and often ends with the person giving up. (Older, 2021-22.) [L1040.003]
  • Do not keep cycling between unsure ideas without deciding. Endless re-evaluation delays building the business. Once research is complete, either confirm a market or get an outside check. (Older, 2021-22.) [L1058.007]
  • Use a niche verification tool for external validation, get the green light, then move forward. Overthinking prevents progress. (Older, 2020.) [L0268.060]
  • Research-heavy work takes time, and taking longer to choose a niche is acceptable if it avoids a blind launch. Do not let indecision stop you indefinitely. Once an idea is validated, act on it. (Older, 2019.) [L1002.072]
  • The longer you delay a decision, the less likely you are to act on it at all. Choose a viable niche, start the store and learn from real results. (Older, 2018.) [L1274.014]
  • Founders often keep many ideas open out of fear of missing other opportunities. Committing to one anchor category and a few concrete steps is usually what makes progress start. [L1016.004]

Perfectionism and waiting for the perfect launch

  • Perfectionism kills progress in business. [L1040.004]
  • Waiting for a perfect store, perfect creatives or perfect timing before launching is usually resistance disguised as perfectionism. A launch produces real market data that no preparation can replace, so the sooner a basic version is live and measurable, the sooner the decisions that matter can be made. [L0625.001]
  • Delaying a launch indefinitely while polishing details keeps the business at zero results and hides the real bottleneck, which is usually the lack of live feedback. Treat the feeling of not being ready as a signal to define a minimum launch version and set a fixed go-live date. [L0625.002]
  • Perfectionism delays the collection of real customer data. A store with its core elements working should go live. Launch day is only the starting point, and the store is then improved continuously from its own data. [L1446.001]
  • Waiting for every supplier approval and a perfect performance score before going live postpones the only thing a launch is meant to deliver: data from real visitors. The goal at launch is learning, not a large first-day sales spike. [L1446.002]
  • The main reason to launch early is to learn what customers search for, how they compare the store with competitors and what they think of it. These insights come only from real visits and cannot be fully replaced by theoretical optimisation. [L1446.003]
  • Time cannot be recovered. Waiting months to pick the perfect niche, start ads or call a supplier costs more than launching and refining. Launch the store early instead of over-planning. [L1515.003]
  • Overplanning can stop action. A minimum viable product that goes live and is refined from real customer feedback is often better than waiting for perfection. Balance speed with quality by launching early and improving over time. [L0552.046]
  • Over-complicating the brand document delays the launch. Write down as much information as possible quickly, using what you already know about the product and customer. [L1044.033]
  • Testing in dropshipping means the brand is launched early and refined later. Waiting for a complete brand before going live slows learning. [L1044.034]
  • Test every element, and do not wait for a perfect product or relationship before launching. If a test fails, learn from it, apologise where needed and move on. (Older, 2020s; undated in source.) [L0790.086]
  • Templates let beginners reach the market faster. Start with ready-made templates, such as a landing page or ad template, and learn design and psychology while ads run. Analysing why a live ad works builds skills in parallel. Waiting until all learning is finished before launching is not sustainable. [L1348.028]
  • Pursuing a perfect niche is a common trap. Choose a niche that passes all tests, start building and improve through real traffic and sales. (Older, 2020.) [L0967.095]
  • Do not chase the perfect niche before building. Even imperfect stores often beat expectations, and each store builds experience for the next one. (Older, 2021.) [L1112.075]
  • A perfect market does not exist. Every market idea has downsides as well as a reasonable number of upsides. The goal of niche selection is to find a good market, not an ideal one. (Older, 2021-22.) [L1040.002]
  • When selecting a market, the aim is to identify good ideas and avoid bad ones, not to find perfect ideas. Keep this standard in mind throughout the selection process. (Older, 2021-22.) [L1040.005]
  • It is hard to predict whether a niche will become a very large store or a modest one. Screen niches efficiently and start instead of researching endlessly. (Older, 2025.) [L1116.046]

Time limits on niche choice

  • Niche selection must stay within a strict time limit because perfectionism kills progress. It should normally take no more than one week, and often only one to two days. (Older, 2021-22.) [L1040.004]
  • Market and product selection should be completed within a matter of days, and some people finish it the same day. Do not allow it to stretch into months. (Older, 2021-22.) [L1059.006]
  • Set a fixed deadline for niche research, such as three days or one week, and choose a niche on that date no matter what. This prevents endless research that never leads to a launch. (Older, 2018.) [L1085.096]
  • Use the forum to explore niche selection before choosing, but do not stay in research mode for long. Balance learning with a firm decision date. (Older, 2018.) [L1085.099]
  • Moving through market selection quickly matters because the goal is to launch and start earning as early as possible. Getting stuck at this stage is the main risk to avoid. (Older, 2021-22.) [L1040.008]
  • Market selection is about choosing a good-enough niche quickly so you can move on to building the business. (Older, 2021-22.) [L1040.009]
  • Many niches and products can work, so a choice that meets the criteria is better than waiting for a better option. (Older, 2021-22.) [L1059.007]
  • If niche selection stalls for a long time, narrow the options to about five serious candidates and commit to one. Continued research without a decision delays the store more than a reasonable choice would. (Older, 2018.) [L1109.068]
  • Do not spend time on brainstorming to the point of stalling, but also do not stop at a few ideas. See the research depth section below for list sizes. [L1056.022]
  • Ad launch on a single product: taking two weeks or more is too slow. Research properly and launch within about two days, then make decisions from data. (Source D 2026.) [L1038.003]

Break a niche decision block (procedure)

  1. Take your top two niches from the research list.
  2. Set a specific deadline for choosing, such as a date only a few days away.
  3. Submit the top two for niche verification if you need outside input.
  4. Choose one and start building the store.

[L0941.098]

Niche research: depth and sources

  • Study how to identify profitable niches before filling in the niche list, so the list starts from a viable position rather than from unchecked ideas. [L0591.008]
  • Complete the niche research steps yourself before submitting ideas for verification, so the verification has good ideas to work with. The team can then help you proceed or pivot. (Older, 2020.) [L0551.062]
  • Do not rush niche research. Thorough research gives the confidence to push through the early work and spend on ads. (Older, 2022.) [L0803.090]
  • Research suppliers and niches thoroughly before launching; extra upfront research is good practice. (Older, 2021.) [L0834.109]
  • Build enough confidence in a niche before launching, by checking competition and brand data first. Going in with a try-it-and-see mindset makes every bump look like proof that the niche does not work. (Older, 2019.) [L0467.026]
  • Market research can take several months. If a niche has many failures, keep pivoting from the closest candidate rather than starting from zero. (Older, 2023.) [L0855.173]
  • Niche selection is a repeated process of brainstorming ideas and then testing each against the criteria. Retest ideas before committing, since the logic often takes several passes to click. (Older, 2023.) [L0970.159]
  • Do not rely on a single niche idea when competition is high. Be sure of the niche before committing by using verification and competitor checks. (Older, 2022.) [L0969.107]
  • Market finding is an open, exploratory phase. Generate many ideas without strict limits; evaluate them against criteria afterwards. (Older, 2021-22.) [L1058.005]
  • Do not stop the brainstorm at a few ideas, such as 10. The choice of niche can decide whether the business succeeds, so a larger list gives more options. (Older, 2020s; undated in source.) [L1056.022]
  • Build a list of 50 or more researched niches. It supports future stores, because a second or third store can be chosen from the list without starting the search from zero. [L1056.023]
  • Some ideas are better considered for later stores. A first store should be simple and use the proven process, while more complex brand or seasonal ideas can wait for stores two to ten. (Older, 2020.) [L0967.096]
  • An outside review of a niche is optional. A niche that clearly passes all four criteria can go ahead without it, while a niche with uncertain data benefits from a second set of eyes before you invest in building a store. [L1080.009]
  • If you are unsure whether a niche fits, get an outside review of your criteria and reasoning instead of looping on it alone. (Older, 2021-22.) [L1059.007]
  • Get an outside review of the shortlist before setup begins, since this can reduce uncertainty. (Older, 2024.) [L0360.003]
  • Finding the market and the right products is usually the hardest early step in starting a high-ticket dropshipping business. Once that is settled, most remaining work is setup and execution. (Older, 2021-22.) [L0912.007]
  • The market selection process must be done personally, because the ability to find and evaluate a market alone is needed later when growing a business without guidance. Someone who needs step-by-step instructions for this first stage will likely struggle with growth. (Older, 2021-22.) [L0996.014]
  • Before choosing, study the criteria and identify the largest possible set of candidates. Because the top criteria are discussed in the next section, use them to filter the list. [L0591.008]
  • Product research in dropshipping is no longer reliable when based on random trending products and luck. Success depends on a deliberate process: good selection criteria, differentiation from existing sellers and a deep understanding of the target audience. [L1158.001]
  • Testing a wide range of interests is useful, since the right interest cannot be known in advance. Choose interests from what buyers do in their lives, not just from the product itself. [L1439.048]

Niche criteria and tiebreakers

  • Competition occurs at the product level within a niche, not the niche level. Multiple stores can succeed in the same niche if they differ in product selection and pricing. [L0255.068]

  • To judge whether a niche is too competitive:

    1. Go to lesson 2.9, "How much competition is too much competition".
    2. Apply the framework to the top-selling products in that niche.
    3. Check whether the top competitors have healthy profit margins.
    4. Decide based on product-level competition, not overall niche saturation.

    [L0255.069]

  • At the competition research stage, the only question is whether a niche is too crowded or too empty. Working out how to stand out comes later, once the niche has passed the research criteria. (Older, 2021-22.) [L0896.027]

  • Do not choose or avoid a niche purely because of size or weight. Calculate shipping costs properly using Freight Center. Large, heavy items are viable if margins support them. (Older, 2022.) [L0249.085]

  • Judge a niche by the criteria and by whether you can see margin, not by fear of big or heavy products. A good niche with high-ticket items can be profitable even when the product is large. (Older, 2020.) [L0664.083]

  • A basic low-margin, low-price product such as a plain t-shirt cannot support paid advertising. Higher-priced products leave room for ads and profit. Replace low-priced niches with higher-priced ones rather than trying to fix them. (Older, 2020.) [L0468.182]

  • Sell only what you can handle. If you cannot take on the customer service load of expensive items, start in a range you can manage and move up later. (Older, 2020.) [L0468.184]

  • If a niche has few products but meets the other criteria, it is not an automatic no-go. Start it only if you are confident you can get enough supplier approvals to make money. (Older, 2018.) [L1085.098]

  • Choose a niche that passes all criteria and whose products keep selling for years, so the store has no natural end date. You can sell the store later if you stop running it. (Older, 2023.) [L0934.189]

  • Choose a recent product with broad market appeal rather than a heavily saturated one, because saturated products need a strong marketing angle to stand out. Start with low investment and optimise the funnel before spending on branding. [L1377.048]

  • Pick products based on market data and proof, not personal taste, intuition or a product that merely looks appealing. Beginners who choose by feeling take on the full risk of an unproven item. [L1174.001]

  • Personal passion for a product category is one of the weakest criteria for choosing what to sell. It should rank far below market opportunity and be treated as a minor factor, not a deciding one. [L0924.001]

  • Choosing a product only because it matches a hobby or long-standing interest leads to pursuing low-potential opportunities. Effort, time and capital then go into a niche that will not grow. [L0924.006]

  • You do not need to be an expert in a niche to start selling it. You learn the product details by answering real customer questions and checking the answers with the supplier. (Older, 2025.) [L0972.001]

  • Niche expertise is not required. Many successful stores were started without experience in the product area, and learning happens while building. (Older, 2022.) [L0665.118]

  • You do not need to be an expert in the niche you eventually choose, and you do not need to be passionate about the product. The goal is to become interested in the business, and knowledge of the niche grows over time. [L1056.021]

  • A store can be launched while the owner is still learning the product, because customer questions teach what they need to know. Being willing to learn as you go is what matters. (Older, 2019.) [L0356.056]

  • Once the data checks are done, personal excitement about selling the products day to day through the website is the guide for choosing between approved niches. (Older, 2021-22.) [L0490.028]

Conflict on passion: Passion is a minor factor overall [L0924.001] and a hobby is a mistake to choose on [L0924.006]. Excitement breaks ties only between niches that have already passed the data checks [L0490.028].

Committing to one niche

  • When several niches are approved, the expected chance of success is roughly equal. Committing to one approved niche matters more than finding a slightly better one. (Older, 2021-22.) [L0490.027]
  • Make the niche decision in one sitting if possible, or sleep on it for one night at most. Then move on without spending more time on the choice. (Older, 2021-22.) [L0490.029]
  • Second-guessing the chosen niche after committing wastes time. Once the decision is made, lock it in and move to the next phase. (Older, 2021-22.) [L0490.030]
  • A niche can be changed later. It is reasonable to narrow the choice to the top two or three candidates and then commit to the strongest one. (Older, 2024.) [L0360.003]
  • Be sure of the niche before committing. Use verification and competitor checks, and do not keep relying on a single niche idea when competition is high. (Older, 2022.) [L0969.107]
  • Avoid shiny-object syndrome. Focus on one niche where you can build a reputation, rather than chasing many unrelated niches. [L0789.051]
  • Founders who keep many ideas open should commit to one anchor category and a few concrete steps. [L1016.004]
  • Build one store and make it profitable before starting another. Fixing one mistake once is far cheaper than repeating it across several stores. (Older, 2020.) [L0852.011]
  • Once the first niche is chosen, build its store before starting another. Later stores in other proven-winner niches can be added to create several income streams without shutting the first store down. (Older, 2020s; undated in source.) [L1079.006]
  • Do not abandon a half-built store to chase a new business model that sounds appealing. Reach the current goal first, then consider the next model. [L0826.015]
  • A first store should be simple and use the proven process. More complex brand or seasonal ideas can wait for stores two to ten. (Older, 2020.) [L0967.096]
  • Continuous improvement is better than endless research. If a first store's niche is not perfect, it can still be profitable and provide a learning experience. While it generates profit, build a second store with better niche research. (Older, 2020.) [L0268.059]

When to persist and when to pivot

  • A niche that passes all criteria deserves persistence. Improve the store before abandoning it, and only sell or rebuild once it has had a fair chance. (Older, 2021.) [L0247.017]
  • When many failures appear in one niche, keep pivoting from the closest candidate rather than starting from zero. (Older, 2023.) [L0855.173]
  • Use both rules together: persist with a niche that passes the criteria and improve it; pivot to the next candidate when the niche shows repeated failures. [L0247.017, L0855.173]
  • Working on the store through improvements before abandoning, and pivoting only when the closest candidate keeps failing, are both needed to avoid two traps: quitting too early and researching forever. [L0247.017, L0855.173, L0268.059]
  • Niche research can be repeated, and a candidate that fails should be retested before it is dropped. (Older, 2023.) [L0970.159]

Launch before you know everything

  • Build and validate the store with at least one paying customer before investing time in advanced scaling strategies and optimisation content. Foundational setup, launch and first sale take priority over growth tactics. Return to advanced material once core operations work. (Older, 2015.) [L0065.001]
  • Use the first sale as the checkpoint: once the core operations are functional, growth tactics are the next priority. [L0065.001]
  • Start with the basic store and proven niche approach rather than many products at once. A structured sequence of setup, supplier approval and then traffic is the reliable path. (Older, 2023.) [L1283.103]
  • Do not spend time on paperwork or heavy setup before the store has anything to show. Start with what is needed to sell and add structures later. (Older, 2022.) [L0963.150]
  • Build a store before wholesale prices are known, since calculations for each supplier can be done once the costs are confirmed. Starting without the calculation gives you no data to decide what to sell. (Older, 2022.) [L0854.110]
  • Do not rush the store to completion either. Complete niche selection, supplier approvals and the store build before the launch month, so traffic can start as soon as the store opens. Work on the store in small daily steps rather than leaving it to the final weeks. (Older, 2025.) [L0667.088]
  • Test and iterate: the brand is refined after launch, not before. [L1044.034]

Cash flow and lean launch

  • Cash flow matters more than perfection. Focus on finding small profit margins quickly rather than building the perfect brand before launching. [L0307.027]
  • Product research follows the same rule: do not wait for a winning product. Launch fast, test offers on real audiences and iterate based on data. For example, test three offer variations, keep the one with the highest CTR and conversion rate, and scale it. Speed beats perfection. [L0307.027]
  • Launch the store as cheaply as possible and reinvest as it earns. Stores started with too much money behind them tended to perform worse than bootstrapped ones. (Older, 2021.) [L1279.086]
  • Build the business in stages, with each stage tied to profitability. Even a store with modest results can already generate profit that supports the next stage. (Older, 2025.) [L1446.029]
  • Finish niche research and verification before building a store, because a store in a weak niche wastes money. A few extra weeks of research are better than a live store that cannot sell. (Older, 2021.) [L1279.087]
  • Choosing a launch timeline depends on available time and experience. See the time budget table above for the 21-day and seven-week options. [L1279.088]
  • Start with low investment and optimise the funnel before spending on branding. [L1377.048]

Store setup and launch steps

First high-ticket store sequence (procedure, older, 2021-22)

  1. Set up the business (store and supplier arrangements).
  2. Launch the store.
  3. Get the first sales.
  4. Then shift focus to growth: marketing systems and scaling the business.

[L0438.002]

Store launch checklist (procedure, older, 2022)

  1. Secure approval from at least two suppliers.
  2. Upload their products to your Shopify store.
  3. Remove all demo products.
  4. Test the checkout process.
  5. Turn on Google Shopping ads.

Open for business and let customers find you. Continue adding suppliers and optimising in the background. [L0272.050]

Supplier approval and launch readiness

  • The checklist requires at least two supplier approvals before launch. A launch that waits for every supplier approval is delayed, so the minimum is two approvals and the rest can follow. [L0272.050, L1446.002]
  • Get enough supplier approvals to make money before starting a niche with few products. [L1085.098]
  • When supplier outreach gets no response, change the call timing or approach first. Most brands can still be reached this way, so widen the niche only as a last resort. (Older, 2023.) [L0970.158]
  • A new seller should not delay contacting suppliers out of fear of rejection. Waiting too long often leads to abandoning the niche before any outreach. (Older, 2023.) [L1094.115]
  • Overthinking the call or feeling awkward about calling customers stops the programme from happening. These calls are positive, and customers who have bought generally like to hear from the business. (Older, 2021-22.) [L1011.016]

Launch from the Source D self-onboarding form

Setting: Work in your own copy of the self-onboarding Google Doc: File > Make a Copy. Fill in the copy so the original template stays clean. [L1268.002]

Complete the form in this order:

  1. Answer what your business differentiator is and how you stand out from the rest, covering your products and mission.
  2. List two to three things you wish every customer knew about your products.
  3. Name your best-selling product. If you are unsure, check store sales by product in Shopify and paste that product's URL. If you have just started, skip this question.
  4. Describe how you want your brand to be perceived by visitors, for example luxury, premium, wholesome or witty.
  5. List what stops customers from buying: complaints, comments and feedback from people who viewed the website but did not purchase.
  6. List influencers or authorities who work with or have used your products, including social media handles. Check Pinterest or Google to find them. Include social media influencers, actors, artists, celebrities and doctors.
  7. Describe any seasonal buying patterns: the seasons when customers buy, or whether sales are year-round.

[L1268.003]

  • Answer all seven self-assessment questions fully before moving on to scaling, ad production or any next stage. Skipping questions leaves gaps that later decisions depend on. [L1268.004]
  • The self-onboarding questions clarify your niche, your goals and whether you are ready before you go deeper into a course or spend on ads. [L1268.011]

Product chain and product choice

  • Plan the full chain from product idea to test-ready product, including research, recipes, covers, images and PDF, to take about three to four days. [L1131.056]
  • Choose products with proof of sales, run ads, and let Facebook data decide. [L1161.045]

Finishing steps and staying on one store

  • Do not rush through setup just to look finished. Work through each step in order, using the checklist above. [L0272.050]
  • Do not spend time polishing a step once its core output works; move to the next one. [L1446.001]
  • Start with ready-made templates and move forward while learning in parallel. [L1348.028]

Deadlines, time budgets and signs of being stuck

  • Assign a realistic time budget to each launch step. If a step clearly takes longer than its budget, treat it as a sign of being stuck and ask a mentor, community or peers for help instead of continuing alone. [L0555.005]
  • Set a launch deadline for the first store, for example 30 to 60 days, to avoid delaying the launch indefinitely. (Older, 2023.) [L0964.144]
  • Set a fixed deadline for niche research, such as three days or one week, and choose on that date. (Older, 2018.) [L1085.096]
  • Use the time budgets in the table above as the reference points for each stage. [L1040.004, L1038.003, L1131.056, L0438.005, L1279.088]

Asking for help when stuck

  • Market selection is about choosing a good-enough niche quickly, and seeking help along the way is encouraged, not a sign of weakness. (Older, 2021-22.) [L1040.009]
  • Rely on coaches and solve problems as they arise instead of trying to plan everything alone. [L0037.001]
  • Use the verification or outside review when you are stuck between two niches, but do not use it to delay the decision further. [L0941.098, L1080.009, L1059.007]
  • Once research is complete, either confirm a market or get an outside check rather than cycling alone. (Older, 2021-22.) [L1058.007]

Focus on high-leverage work

  • Page speed work is not a high-leverage activity for a business owner. Once a site is near the 5-second mark, do not spend days or weeks tuning it. Move your time to core conversion work. (Older, 2021-22.) [L1522.040]
  • Time spent on low-leverage work is time not spent on launching and learning from real customers. [L1515.003, L1446.003]

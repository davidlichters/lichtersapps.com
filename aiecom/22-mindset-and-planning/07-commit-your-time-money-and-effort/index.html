22.7 Commit your time, money and effort

Written commitment and accountability

  • Before you start a training program, write a concrete action plan. It states how many hours per day and how many days per week you will study and act, rather than just deciding to finish the material quickly. [L0444.001]
  • Your results depend on the actions you actually take. Without a written plan, the outcome is left to chance. Decide in advance which actions you will take and how often, so you shape your results instead of accepting whatever happens. [L0444.020]
  • Write a personal pledge in a notebook. Use this wording as the template:
I will put in X hours each day and work on e-commerce X days every week.
I will keep this up until I have completed the course and tested at least 12 products.
Until I have tested 12 products, I will not doubt the method.
Regardless of circumstances, I will go through the entire course and test 12 products.
Date: [today's date]
Signature: [your signature]

[L0444.018]

  • Make the commitment official and accountable:
    1. Photograph or scan the handwritten, signed commitment. [L0444.019]
    2. If you have a business partner, each of you signs a commitment and shows it to the other. [L0444.019]
    3. If you work alone, send the signed commitment to the program's support contact by email so it is recorded and checked. [L0444.019]
  • Outside support such as coaching, mentoring or a course adds value only when you take consistent action. Results come from daily execution, so accountability is your responsibility, not the mentor's. [L1503.008]

Realistic time minimums

  • Put at least one hour per day into your business. Consistent daily effort produces measurable growth and results after one month, six months and one year. [L0280.035]
  • Avoid analysis paralysis by taking one concrete action every day toward your business, even while still learning. Combining action with learning is far more valuable than consuming information without acting. [L0280.036]
  • Set a realistic weekly time commitment for the program: a minimum of 2 hours per day on 6 days per week, which totals 12 hours per week. If you have fewer study days, compress the same 12 hours into fewer days, but do not go below 12 hours per week. [L0444.002]
  • Set a minimum effort that is realistic enough to produce results within the expected time. A very low, unrealistic minimum will probably not produce results, and a person who quits before the reasonable point never sees the outcome. [L0444.017]
  • Do not set an overly ambitious daily study target, such as 12 hours per day alongside a day job. Missing an unrealistic target leads to frustration, demotivation and the false belief that the training does not work. [L0444.003]
  • Consistency matters more than hours. Even 15 minutes per day adds up over time. [L0671.144, L0671.279]
  • Consistency of daily effort matters more than the number of hours. Even one lesson plus its action item per day, for someone with only an evening free, can lead to a launched business within about a month, though it may take longer. [L1496.003]
  • Squeezing study into very small, scattered time slots while pushing yourself hard is demotivating and raises the risk of dropping out. A daily time commitment you can sustain produces more progress. [L1496.013]
  • Speed of progress does not determine long-run success. Anyone working with less time per day can be just as successful as someone working more, as long as they do the right things. [L0438.004] (Source B, 2021-22: older advice.)

  • A store can be built around the time the owner has, so launch timing should match the owner's real weekly hours instead of an ideal plan. Staying realistic prevents frustration and wasted effort. [L1068.079] (Source A, 2019: older advice.)
  • The pace of the roadmap depends on the hours per week you can dedicate. Some operators finish the early phases much faster than the standard weekly plan. [L0914.018]
  • Owners who work long days on a store alongside a full-time job should lower ad budgets instead of pausing campaigns to protect their energy. Protect your health and pace the work. [L0469.111] (Source A, 2021: older advice.)

Time for the day job

  • Dropshipping can be run as a side business alongside a day job. Set aside time outside work hours to launch the store, then use outsourcing to reduce time spent once sales are coming in. [L0771.058] (Source B, 2021-22: older advice.)
  • A person with a full-time job can still build a business by committing a fixed daily block, for example half an hour at the same time every day. Use the same discipline you would apply in a workplace. [L0832.006]
  • Scarce money can be offset by investing personal time. Working on a business before or after a day job and into the evening is a valid way to start with no capital. [L1041.011]
  • Daily time after work can be used productively: keep testing new products, create content and build the store's traffic, and study marketing and design skills. [L1077.045]
  • If you still have a job, a mortgage or dependants, build the online business on the side. Leave the job only once the business produces steady income. Replacing the salary too early exposes the household to unnecessary risk. [L1014.003] (Source B, 2021-22: older advice.)
  • If you hold a full-time job while building a store, you cannot answer every call, email or chat live. Delayed replies are a normal limit of a side business, not a failure. [L0909.003] (Source B, 2021-22: older advice.)
  • Working on a business alongside a job lowers financial pressure but makes it easier to find excuses for skipping work. A stronger commitment comes from a clear personal reason and a fixed daily work schedule, whichever way the business is started. [L0826.027]
  • Founders with no steady paycheck often push harder to make the business work. Commitment and drive matter more than free time when launching a store. [L0471.051] (Source A, 2024.)
  • Spreading time across too many projects at once is harmful. Start new ventures conservatively with a small share of time while keeping the established activity running. [L0805.052]

Fixed routine and workplace

  • Decide when in your week you will work on the business and keep that time. The business takes time to build before it produces revenue. [L0671.143, L0671.278]
  • Set a fixed office location and fixed working hours, for example 9 a.m. to 5 p.m., and treat that time as work time. A kitchen table can work, but only if it is used for business at set hours and kept quiet. [L0832.004]
  • An office does not have to be a room. A coffee shop works when traveling, as long as you sit down and set working hours. The office gives a defined space and start and end times for each goal. [L0832.008]
  • Working from home alone without deadlines makes focus hard. Set self-imposed structure and discipline to keep working, especially when money is already coming in. [L0690.036]
  • Dropshipping can support location independence, but remote workers still need a fixed routine to stay productive. Working Monday to Friday with weekends fully off was the most effective rhythm for a remote operator. [L1441.001]
  • The biggest reason beginners fail is not doing the work. Many people enroll but never log in, or log in only once. Commit to a regular schedule to make progress. [L0934.109, L0934.186]
  • Do not stop partway through the setup. Without finishing the steps, no store can make a sale. [L0671.145]

Starting capital and testing budget

  • Use your own saved money, such as income from a job, savings or an inheritance, as the beginner testing budget. Avoid leveraged money such as bank loans. [L1394.007]
  • Do not fund beginner testing with credit cards. Using them at this stage is a major red flag. [L1394.008]
  • A large budget is not required to start. A small budget can push a beginner to work harder. Budget is rarely the reason a store fails. [L0934.111, L0934.188]
  • Before launch, the main commitment in a dropshipping setup is time rather than money. Financial outlay is concentrated at the marketing stage that follows launch. [L0438.008] (Source B, 2021-22: older advice.)
  • Business costs for a new store can be a few hundred dollars if the owner does the setup work personally. Costs rise mainly when work such as logos, site build or uploads is outsourced. [L0801.167] (Source A, 2021: older advice.)

Working with limited capital

  • When capital is limited, use other levers: skills, time and resources. If one lever is short, pull another, for example by learning Photoshop or Canva and filming products yourself. [L0561.045]
  • Assess your position on three levers: time, money and skill.
    1. If you have money but little time, outsource tasks. [L1041.010]
    2. If you have time but little money, do the work yourself. [L1041.010]
    3. Build skills in every case. When stuck, analyze your current situation to find a lever you can pull. [L1041.010]
  • Starting capital example: about 300 pounds was enough to build a store and run a first Facebook ad of 45 pounds. Ads can be tested from as little as five pounds per day. [L0561.046]

Staffing and outsourcing in the early phase

  • A store with a few suppliers still needs daily time for more approvals and uploads, even after launch. Expect this. [L0836.058] (Source A, 2024.)
  • When time is scarce, outsource product uploads and store build, and oversee the outsourced work. Consistent part-time work can keep a store moving. [L0471.050] (Source A, 2024.)
  • Without budget for staff, the founder does customer service, supplier contact and fulfilment personally at first. Calling suppliers before they close for the day, even very early in the morning, can be necessary when time zones are far apart. This phase ends when the store is profitable enough to hire help. [L0910.005]

Buffers for the unknown and timelines

  • Beginners underestimate the hurdles of a first business. Include a buffer for unknown difficulties in every time estimate, and plan slower than feels necessary to avoid burnout. [L0444.005]
  • Building a sustainable, profitable store can take months. The pace depends on how fast the owner works through tasks. A slower path still beats quitting, because stopping guarantees no results. Treat the work as delayed gratification: effort now produces returns later. [L1110.001] (Source A, 2019: older advice.)
  • A lead generation machine needs time in three parts:
    • 10 to 20 hours to write or outsource the lead magnet. [L1076.022]
    • 1 to 2 hours for the lead page. [L1076.022]
    • 5 to 10 hours to build the flow. [L1076.022]
    • Maximum total: about 20 to 30 hours. Let it run for one to two months before judging the results. [L1076.022]
  • Stay with the commitment until the outcome is visible. Stay focused only on tasks that move the business forward, then commit to your goals and make a personal commitment to do whatever it takes to reach them. [L0419.008] (Source B, 2021-22: older advice.)
  • Apply what you learn every day, or whenever your schedule allows, even with a full-time job. Small steps count as long as you keep taking them consistently. [L0419.007] (Source B, 2021-22: older advice.)
  • The most common reason beginners fail is not doing the work. Many people enroll but never log in, or log in only once. A large budget is not needed to start, and a small budget can push a beginner to work harder. [L0934.109, L0934.186, L0934.111, L0934.188]

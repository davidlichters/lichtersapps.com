22.14 Audit your time and remove distractions

Measuring where your time goes

  • Before any optimization, the owner needs a baseline: a detailed record of where time actually goes, not a general sense of what was done. Without this measured starting point, later changes cannot be judged. (older, 2021-22) [L0442.001]
  • Time is the one resource an e-commerce owner cannot increase. The day has a fixed 24 hours, and customers only shop for limited hours. The only lever is using existing hours better: choosing what to work on, setting priorities, and reducing procrastination. (older, 2021-22) [L0442.002]
  • Owners tend to drift into short-term tasks that feel productive but do not drive medium- to long-term growth. Concentrate on the tasks only you can do, and stay focused while doing them. (older, 2021-22) [L0442.003]
  • The more focused the owner is on the business, the greater the chance it grows and succeeds. Distractions reduce that chance directly, so protecting focus time is part of the plan. [L1043.010]
  • Constant urgency is often perception rather than reality. Protecting focus time is more valuable than responding to every request immediately, since attention is one of the most valuable resources an entrepreneur has. [L1181.006]
  • Do not blame yourself for struggling with time management. Most business owners find it hard. Gradual, consistent improvement in how time is used reduces stress and pressure and improves confidence in the business. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.017]

Running the 30-day baseline

Procedure (about four calendar weeks):

  1. For the next four weeks, record every task performed in or on the business. (older, 2021-22) [L0442.004]
  2. Record the estimated time spent on each task. (older, 2021-22) [L0442.004]
  3. Break the record down day by day. (older, 2021-22) [L0442.004]
  4. Include every distraction, for example a 30-minute detour into Facebook during a work block. (older, 2021-22) [L0442.004]
  5. Add a procrastination line each day with the total estimated time lost to procrastination. Details of what was done are optional. (older, 2021-22) [L0442.004]
  6. Enter the entries at the end of each day, as you go, or as a running log. Choose what works, but the entries must accurately reflect what was actually done. (older, 2021-22) [L0442.004]
  7. Review the 30 days afterward to identify patterns and decide what to change. (older, 2021-22) [L0442.004]

Steps that make the baseline work:

  • Set a daily reminder in the phone or calendar so that the time log is filled in every day for the full 30 days. (older, 2021-22) [L0442.012]
  • Start the time log immediately, on the day you first set it up, instead of postponing it until other planning or learning is finished. Setup takes only a couple of minutes, so there is no reason to delay the 30-day baseline. (older, 2021-22) [L0442.014]
  • Tracking does not have to run continuously. It can be done periodically, and a one-month tracking run is enough to refresh your view of how time is being spent. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.019]

Tools for time tracking

  • Toggl Track (toggl.com/track) is an app and online service that automatically times activities on a computer and, optionally, on a phone. You do not have to remember to start and stop each task. It can show how much time goes to apps such as Instagram. (older, 2021-22) [L0442.008] The review unit names it "Toggle." [L1514.001]
  • Manual day-by-day spreadsheet tracker, available in Excel and Numbers versions. Each day is a column. Each row is a task with the time spent, and a procrastination row is included. Add a column for each further day to extend the tracker beyond the template's sample days. (older, 2021-22) [L0442.009]

Example entries for one Monday:

Task Time
Customer service phone calls 1.5 hours
Writing product descriptions for the website 1 hour
Updating business social media pages 30 minutes

(older, 2021-22) [L0442.010]

Logging honestly

  • Time tracking only works if the owner is honest with themselves. Recording distractions and procrastination can feel uncomfortable, but skipping them makes the baseline useless. (older, 2021-22) [L0442.005]

  • The tracking records are private and are not shared with a coach or anyone else. Their purpose is to reveal patterns in your own working time. (older, 2021-22) [L0442.006]

  • Do not analyze or change working habits during the 30 days of tracking. First collect the data. Patterns and corrective actions come after the tracking period ends. (older, 2021-22) [L0442.007]

  • Totaling the day at the end relies on memory, which makes accurate tracking harder for people who struggle to recall their day. Logging each task as it happens is more reliable. (older, 2021-22) [L0442.011]

  • A time log only shows the real picture if it includes distractions and detours, not just planned work blocks. Record examples such as:

    • switching to another website or social media during a work block
    • checking the phone instead of working
    • deciding to leave for a break, such as an hour away

    Log each one with its estimated duration. (older, 2021-22) [L0442.013]

Reviewing the log after 30 days

Procedure: Review the time-tracking log after at least 30 days (a practical minimum, a month of tracking). Find four categories: distractions, rapid task switching, long stretches on unimportant tasks, and tasks that could be delegated. Use an app such as Toggl Track or a spreadsheet to pull the data. Then check each category in order: (older, 2021-22) [L1514.001]

  1. Distractions: note interruptions in the middle of a task and off-topic breaks, and count how often each one recurs. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.001]
  2. Task switching: look for many short blocks of 15 minutes or less on the same kind of work. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.001]
  3. Unimportant tasks: find activities that take long stretches of time, and classify them with a prioritization matrix (an important/urgent grid). (older, 2021-22) [L1514.001]
  4. Delegation: find tasks taking more than one hour per day, especially recurring ones over 30 days, and ask whether only the owner can do them. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.001]

Deciding what counts as a priority:

  • Treat a distraction as a priority problem only when it recurs often over the tracking period. A distraction seen once in 30 days may not matter. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.002]
  • Classify each recurring distraction in two ways: as business-related or not, and as something you control or not. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.002]
  • The worst distractions are the ones that keep appearing at the same point in the daily schedule. A single occurrence in a 30-day tracking period is usually not worth acting on. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.020]

Distraction types and how to handle them

Habit-based distractions

  • Distractions that come from habits are addictive behaviors that persist until you deliberately break the habit with a specific plan. Examples include checking email, social media, or the Shopify app repeatedly, such as 57 times a day. Such habits do not speed up sales or business growth. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.003]

Business-related distractions: scheduling

  1. Decide whether the distraction can be moved to a later time. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.004]
  2. Add it to the daily planner at a fixed time instead of dropping it entirely. For example, 30 minutes of email at 1 p.m. after important work is finished. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.004]
  3. Enforce the schedule through self-discipline, since no tool can enforce it for you. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.004]

Uncontrollable interruptions

  • Interruptions you cannot control need a different solution than habit-based distractions. Examples include a business phone ringing before a customer service agent exists, or a family member entering unexpectedly. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.005]
  • Address the phone-ringing case through staffing or outsourcing rather than willpower. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.005]

Task switching

  • Each interruption to a task costs about 15 minutes to return to the flow state, so every switch loses at least 15 minutes of focused work. (older, 2021-22) [L0679.019]
  • Task switching is inefficient when you work on the same task in blocks of 15 minutes or less. The brain needs at least 10 minutes to reach peak efficiency on a task, so short repeated blocks lose a large share of productive time. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.006]
  • Example: A blog post written in four 20-minute blocks on different days took 80 minutes of time across the sessions. When the same post was written in one 80-minute block, it took about 60 minutes, because focus was sustained and work quality rose. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.007]
  • Example, same case within one day: A blog post written in four separate 20-minute blocks within one day took 80 minutes in total. When the same post was written in one continuous 80-minute block, it took about 60 minutes, because the writer kept full focus and the quality was higher. Combining the fragments into one block of the same task saves time. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.021]
  • Combine short fragments of the same task into one longer block, around 60 to 80 minutes, to finish faster. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.007]

Procedure to reduce task switching:

  1. Identify tasks you switch between often in blocks of 15 minutes or less. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.008]
  2. If you are not using a daily to-do list, start one and build a habit around it. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.008]
  3. If you already have one, enforce it with self-discipline and reward yourself for sticking to it. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.008]
  4. Schedule the task in one larger block. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.008]

Comfort tasks and ad account over-checking

  • Spending more than one hour per day on tasks that are unimportant for the business is a warning sign. Review these tasks with a prioritization matrix. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.009]
  • People often spend too long on unimportant tasks they feel comfortable with, such as hours in the Google Ads account. They avoid important tasks they feel less confident about, such as SEO or difficult supplier or customer conversations. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.010]
  • For a high-ticket dropshipping business, SEO has far greater potential than Google Ads. Spending hours on ads while neglecting SEO means the time is misallocated. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.010]
  • Optimizing Google Ads has diminishing returns, and you can easily spend hours on it. Spending five hours a week to raise CTR by 0.3% rarely produces revenue or profit worth that time, and those hours could go to other work. (older, 2021-22) [L0879.036]

Ad account time limits:

  • Ads account work needs hours daily only in rare cases. Setting up a new account, building new campaigns, or making major changes justifies extra time. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.011]
  • Routine data review and small tweaks that take hours are over-analysis. Limit them, for example to 30 minutes instead of two hours. (older, 2021-22) [L1514.011]

Over-optimizing by hand:

  • Refusing automation and optimizing every bid by hand in spreadsheets takes very many hours. One keyword example was estimated at 20 hours. That time is then missing for conversion rate optimization, store branding, and other growth work. [L0454.012]

Protecting focus blocks

Blocked work sessions:

  1. Use Do Not Disturb or focus mode during a blocked work session. [L1181.005]
  2. Silence phone notifications and close apps. [L1181.005]
  3. Handle urgent calls or messages only if they are repeated or clearly an emergency. [L1181.005]

Unfocused time:

  • Two hours of checking email, searching, and messaging without a defined task does not count as productive work. Recognize unfocused time as lost time, not as progress. [L1039.015]

Supplier outreach:

  • A common failure point is avoiding supplier outreach, especially phone calls. Postponing calls to a later weekend or vacation stops progress. Schedule the calls and make them. [L0934.110, L0934.187] (Source A, 2023)

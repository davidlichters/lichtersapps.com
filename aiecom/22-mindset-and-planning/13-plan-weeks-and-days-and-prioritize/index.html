22.13 Plan weeks and days and prioritize

Calendar as the foundation

  • A task or event that is not blocked in the calendar will not happen. Block fixed time for deep work, especially for building web pages or ad creatives, which cannot be done in short gaps [L1181.001].
  • Every scheduled task must go into a real calendar or diary with a fixed date, not only into memory. This is a basic part of time management for any business owner [L0384.009].
  • Use an electronic calendar such as iCal on Mac or Google Calendar, or any reminder system you already use. Set a fixed time every working day to review the daily to-do list, either early in the morning or in the evening [L0441.005].
  • Only schedule daily to-do reviews on days you work on the business. If you work five days a week, prepare Monday's list on Friday afternoon and leave the weekend free. If you work every day, set a fixed time at the end of each week to plan the coming week [L0441.006].
  • Schedule planning as recurring calendar blocks: 5 minutes daily for the daily list, 15 minutes weekly for the weekly list, and a longer session each quarter for rocks. Place each block at a time of day that is usually free, so other things cannot take it [L0679.028].
  • Work that needs deep focus belongs in blocks of one to two hours. Lighter or ad hoc tasks such as booking calls or catch-up work go into the afternoon [L1181.002].
  • Schedule tasks at the time of day when you do your best work. Morning or evening can both work if rest is sufficient [L1181.015].
  • Add contingency time to every calendar block, because estimates usually run long. For tasks such as ads, plan considerably more time than expected and leave a buffer between back-to-back calls [L1181.004].
  • A rigid weekly schedule tends to break because unplanned events keep appearing. Plan at the level of the day, with time blocks for fixed work and open slots for unpredictable requests [L1181.033].
  • Give each task a start and an end time. One speaker estimated that a task could take 20 hours without a time limit but about one hour with a set slot [L0832.007].
  • Give each work session a fixed length and a clear output. Without a time cap, tasks such as writing, testing or product research take far longer than needed [L1343.038].
  • Use supplier research time, for example while a store is being built, to extract supplier information and fill in a supplier tracking sheet before contacting suppliers [L0934.191].
  • Reserve two or three days each week to research new products and check how the market is moving, separate from running existing stores. Use these sessions to build a list of candidate products [L1377.058].

Weekly planning

  • The weekly to-do list must be derived from the rocks. The single most important task needed to reach the current rocks goes at the top of both the weekly and the daily list [L0441.004].
  • Treat to-do lists as a habit built with persistence. They act as an anchor that prevents distraction and keeps effort on the right tasks. Without them, progress on important work tends to be lower [L0679.007].
  • Weekly planning, in this order (older advice, 2021-22) [L0679.008]:
    1. Once a week, usually on a Friday or a Saturday, block a recurring 15-minute slot in the calendar with phone notifications so it cannot be booked over.
    2. In that slot, review the current 90-day rocks and check what is needed for the coming week.
    3. Write the weekly to-do list for the coming week with no more than 5 items. These are the big items that must be achieved, and they must come from the rocks.
    4. Split each rock into a sequence of tasks. Put only some of them on the weekly list, adding a few more each week, because a complex task such as a hire cannot be completed within one week.
    5. Keep the list and check it against the rocks during the week.
  • The weekly list can be written at the end of the previous week or at the start of the new week. Either timing is acceptable, as long as it is written in one of these recurring slots [L0679.030].
  • A weekly to-do list must never have more than 5 items [L0679.013].

Daily planning and the evening routine

  • Daily planning, in this order (older advice, 2021-22) [L0679.009]:
    1. At the end of each workday, review how the current day's to-do list went.
    2. Write the to-do list for the next day, with a maximum of 3 must-achieve items, numbered by priority. Number 1 is the highest-value activity.
    3. Place the list where it is always visible, such as on paper next to the computer or on a sticky note on the screen.
    4. The next day, start with item 1 and work through the items in sequence. Do not start non-urgent tasks, such as checking email or social media, until the list is complete.
    5. Create a new list every day.
  • Daily to-do lists should be prioritized, giving the day a structured approach to its work [L0223.008].
  • Write the to-do list for the next day the night before. The brain settles and starts ordering tasks, so the workday starts without deciding what to do first [L0679.010, L0705.012, L0705.014].
  • Evening planning routine [L0705.012]:
    1. Every evening before sleep, write a list of the tasks for the next day.
    2. Use Notion, a piece of paper, a whiteboard or any calendar to organize and manage the tasks.
    3. On waking, review the list so you know exactly what to work on and do not spend the morning deciding.
  • A whiteboard can be used to write the next day's to-do list in the evening [L0705.013].
  • Place the planning list or whiteboard where it is the first thing you see on waking, so you review the next day's tasks before the morning starts. This removes the time lost to deciding [L0705.016].
  • Writing the list in the morning is still better than having no list, but the evening is preferred [L0679.010].
  • Plan the day or week the night before. Do not work on the to-do list until the time-critical morning tasks are finished: orders, customer messages, finances and ad checks [L1181.008].
  • The daily to-do list is not the whole workday. It holds the must-achieve tasks that drive weekly goals, rocks and yearly targets. Non-urgent activities wait until the first daily item is done, but personal time can be scheduled before or after business work [L0679.017].
  • Organize your time with a calendar and a to-do list. Without them the daily workload becomes unmanageable. Block focused work time in the calendar before opening the task list, and do not let open tasks overwhelm the day [L1041.015].
  • Calendar and task structure reduce anxiety. Once the day's tasks are known in advance, the mental load of remembering open items drops and focus improves [L1181.016].
  • Daily focus routine (Source A) [L1039.014]:
    1. Know your core reason and your dreamline so you know what the work serves.
    2. Plan the day's tasks the night before and work from that list when you sit down.
    3. Focus on one priority task at a time and do not start work without a plan.
    4. Plan tomorrow's tasks before you stop today.

  • A rigid list with no open slots fails. Keep some time for unplanned work [L1181.033].

Prioritizing: the Eisenhower Matrix

  • The Eisenhower Matrix sorts all tasks by two dimensions, urgency and importance. Each quadrant gets a different action: immediate execution, strategic scheduling, delegation or deletion [L0265.001].
  • Urgency means the task is overdue, has a deadline approaching very soon, demands immediate attention, or would have immediate consequences if not completed [L0265.007].
  • Importance means the task affects many people or projects, contributes substantial value to the business, has other tasks dependent on its completion, or offers high reward relative to effort [L0265.006].
  • Urgency and importance are independent. Something can be urgent without contributing value, and something can drive large business value without time pressure. Confusing the two makes people work on low-value tasks instead of high-impact ones [L0265.008].

  • Business owners should spend personal time on important tasks, especially those that are both important and urgent or important but not urgent. Time on urgent but unimportant tasks prevents high-impact work [L0265.017].
  • Examples (older advice, 2021-22):
    • Scaling organic traffic from 1,000 to 10,000 monthly visitors is important (a 10x increase typically produces 10x revenue and may improve margins by lowering cost per acquisition) but not urgent. It should sit at the top of the priority list and get daily focus until complete, despite no pressing deadline [L0265.009].
    • Responding to an angry customer is urgent but not important. Delegate it to a customer service virtual assistant [L0265.012].
    • Writing a blog post for SEO growth is important if it serves the organic traffic goal, but not urgent. It should sit high on the list if you handle it personally [L0265.013].
    • Checking email daily is neither important nor urgent in most cases and should never be at the top of the list. Ideally, delegate the email system to a virtual assistant who screens messages and sends a daily summary of items needing your attention [L0265.014].
    • Posting a Black Friday promotion is both urgent and important: high reward with minimal time, potentially thousands of dollars in extra revenue. Execute it immediately [L0265.015].

Eat the Frog and task order

  • The Frog-Eating Method, attributed to Brian Tracy, says that completing your most unpleasant required task first makes everything else feel easier, raises the chance of finishing the whole list, and builds daily momentum [L0265.010] (older advice, 2021-22 for the Source B source).
  • Eat the frog: start with the most difficult or unpleasant task so it is done before the rest of the day [L1181.010].
  • Schedule the most important but most difficult or uncomfortable task first thing in the day, using a daily to-do list. Complete it before the less important tasks, which then get smaller time slots in the rest of the day (older advice, 2021-22) [L1514.012].
  • Frog-eating procedure (older advice, 2021-22) [L0265.011]:
    1. Sort all tasks into four groups: things you don't want to do but need to do; things you want to do and need to do; things you want to do but don't need to do; things you don't want to do and don't need to do.
    2. Complete group 1 first. These often involve difficult conversations or financial discomfort.
    3. Complete group 2 second.
    4. Complete group 3 last.
    5. Outsource or delete group 4 entirely.
  • Conflicting view: Source A (2025) found that starting the workday with the simplest task builds momentum, and that moving to harder tasks once in work mode works better for him. Starting with the hardest task can also work, but it often delays the start [L0972.092]. The same source notes that the classic Eat That Frog advice is to do the most avoided task first, and that starting with an easy task worked better for daily momentum [L0972.093].
  • Which to use: Use the frog method (hardest task first) when the hard task is the one that blocks the day and you can start it right away. Use the simple-first method (warm-up, then harder work) when starting the hard task tends to delay the start. Both sources agree that the daily list must be set in advance [L0972.092, L0972.093, L0265.010, L1181.010].

Staying on the plan during the day

  • Once daily priorities are set with the Eisenhower Matrix or the frog method, do not second-guess or change them mid-day. Working the list without deviation increases the sense of progress and accomplishment (older advice, 2021-22) [L0265.018].
  • Focus on one task at a time. Holding several unfinished jobs in mind creates tension and lowers the quality of each one [L1206.019].
  • Plan the day around the top priorities, not the whole backlog [L1181.007].
  • Work does not need to be completed in full every day. Missing some items is acceptable if the top priorities are done [L1181.007].

Handling distracting ideas

  • Distractions that pop up during a task, such as a new post idea, should not interrupt the current task. Write them on the back of the daily list to record them without breaking focus (older advice, 2021-22) [L0679.018].
  • Keep the phone away during work time to protect focus. Use paper and pen for daily task lists instead of digital tools (older advice, 2021-22) [L0223.007].
  • Handling distracting ideas, in this order (older advice, 2021-22) [L0679.020]:
    1. When a new idea comes up during a task, write it on the back of the daily to-do sheet and keep working on the current task.
    2. At the end of the day, when writing the next day's list, review everything written on the back of the sheet.
    3. For each idea, decide whether to add it to the next day's list (if important enough), keep it as a record for later, or turn it into a future rock.
  • Ideas are not a substitute for execution. Staying in the visionary role only, with many ideas and no execution, produces no results. Schedule fixed execution time so the idea phase never takes over the whole day [L0519.007].
  • Many entrepreneurs have more ideas than time, so the real skill is choosing what to work on. Focus on the highest-impact task for the current stage of the store (2018, older) [L1274.080].
  • Everyone has the same number of hours per day, so the question is where to invest them. Time on compounding work matters more than filler time that feels productive (2019, older) [L1275.059].

Decision limits and the value of time

  • A solo owner can make only a limited number of high-quality decisions per day and per week. Quality drops once the limit is exceeded. Lack of focus is a major barrier for small and medium-sized businesses, which will not reach their potential if the owner is spread across too many opportunities [L0679.029].
  • Each person can make only a limited number of high-quality decisions per day or week. Planning the to-do list in advance saves decision capacity for business decisions that come up during the day. Routines such as a fixed breakfast or similar clothing serve the same purpose (older advice, 2021-22) [L0679.011].
  • For a small to medium e-commerce business run by one person or a small team, the owner's ability to perform, manage time, focus and set priorities largely determines how fast it grows (older advice, 2021-22) [L0802.001].
  • Start each new calendar year with a dedicated block of owner-focused preparation: time management, focus, prioritization and delegation. Do this before executing the year's marketing plans. The block needs no marketing tactics (older advice, 2021-22) [L0802.011].
  • Judge the value of current time by the business you expect in about two years, not by the hourly income you earn today. Intense early phases often pay off later, like a snowball effect [L1359.012].
  • Organization is necessary for growth. Missed deadlines, calls and commitments have real consequences, and structured time management is essential when one person handles many roles [L1181.025].
  • Avoid over-engineering the task system. Keep it simple, and add complexity such as custom fields or extra views only when a specific process needs it [L1181.017].

Tools and task systems

  • Paper is the preferred tool for weekly and daily lists because it stays visible and feels real. A single sheet is enough without fancy templates (older advice, 2021-22) [L0679.012].
  • Weekly and daily to-do lists do not need software. They can be written by hand. A workable routine is to write the daily list the night before, so the next day starts with the top priority already set (older advice, 2021-22) [L0441.014].
  • Notion, paper, a whiteboard or any calendar can organize and manage daily tasks [L0705.013].
  • Notion can hold calendars, to-do lists, projects and sprints in one place, which makes it the preferred organization tool for running a dropshipping business. The operator moved from Slack to Trello before settling on Notion [L0519.008].
  • Deep Work by Cal Newport helps build a focused work environment, which is especially useful for the fabric-worker role. Getting Things Done by David Allen helps keep tasks from piling up as stress [L0519.010].

Three-hat method

  • The three-hat method splits work into three roles. The fabric worker does the tasks. The manager plans, delegates and checks the work. The visionary generates new ideas and products. Each role needs its own fixed time block so that ideas never crowd out execution [L0519.005].
  • Build a color-coded daily schedule for the three roles. Example from the lesson: fabric worker black, manager green, visionary beige (Source C) [L0519.006]:
    1. Morning: read for about one hour to collect ideas and inspiration.
    2. From 10 a.m.: work in 25-minute shifts, taking one task at a time and finishing it.
    3. Afternoon: fixed slot for client or student calls.
    4. After the calls: manager hat. Check the morning's finished tasks and prepare the next day's task list.
    5. Then use the time for skill learning, for example language lessons.
    6. Stop work, exercise, and start the next day on the same schedule.
    7. Evening: keep a slot for free thinking, because new ideas often come during other activities.

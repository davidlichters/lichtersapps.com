7.6 Pricing products from suppliers without MAP

Core floor: 2x wholesale for non-MAP products

  • Without a MAP, the selling price should be at least 2x wholesale. Competitors should also sell at a minimum of 2x wholesale. For example, a 250 wholesale price implies a competitor price of at least 500. [L0247.038]
  • The rule for non-MAP pricing is to set prices at least 2x wholesale. Research competitor prices. If competitors sell above 2x, match or exceed that level rather than undercutting. [L0270.013]
  • Without a MAP or MSRP, the formula is: selling price = wholesale × 2. Example: wholesale 500 gives a selling price of 1,000. [L969.002]
  • Minimum advertised price for suppliers without a stated MAP: MAP = 2 × wholesale cost. Example: wholesale $500 means the selling price should be at least $1,000, which leaves roughly 20 to 30% after expenses. [L1281.030]
  • MAP means Minimum Advertised Price. [L0270.013]
  • The 2x test: the average competitor selling price should be at least double the wholesale cost. If it is below double, treat it as a red flag and do not upload the supplier's product, since margins will not be sustainable. [L1051.006]
  • Example with $500 wholesale: an average competitor price of $700 fails the 2x test, so the product should not be listed. An average competitor price of $2,000 passes, leaving about $1,500 per sale before fees. [L1051.007]

Alternative markup and where the rule is recorded

  • Where a manufacturer sets a minimum advertised price, follow the MAP price list. Where none exists, apply a fixed markup such as 20% to each product and compare it with competitor prices. [L1396.038] (Older advice, 2021-22. This 20% fixed markup differs from the 2x floor above. Use the MAP list where one exists, and use the 2x floor for non-MAP products as described above. [L1396.038, L1051.006])
  • Put the pricing rule on the upload card so the person uploading does not have to guess. [L1396.038]

Setting up price fields

  • The compare-at price is the supplier's MSRP, and the sale price is the MAP. [L1281.031]
  • Example: wholesale $500, MAP $1,000, MSRP $1,200. Set the compare-at price to $1,200 and the sale price to $1,000. [L1281.031]

Pricing against the market

Procedure: price a non-MAP product by market (2025)

  1. Search Google for the product from the supplier's brand to see the prices competitors list. [L966.049]
  2. If competitors list it at a price that gives enough margin, list your product at that higher price. [L966.049]
  3. If competitors list it too low for a sufficient margin, keep the product on your store at a markup you accept. [L966.049]
  4. Do not include that product in your paid ad spend. Sales from free traffic still earn money without wasting ad budget. [L966.049]

Procedure: start at 2x and adjust to the market (2020, older)

  1. Set the starting selling price at about double the wholesale cost. [L967.065]
  2. Search for the same brand's products online to see what other retailers charge. [L967.065]
  3. If other retailers sell at a higher multiple of wholesale, match that multiple instead of doubling automatically. [L967.065]

Procedure: check the market before doubling (2021, older)

  1. Search for the product online. [L853.035]
  2. Note what other sellers charge. [L853.035]
  3. If most sellers charge about 2x or more, the higher price can work. [L853.035]
  4. If sellers charge only about 1.2x, the markup is not worth pursuing. [L853.035]

Procedure: price a non-MAP product, 2019 version (older)

  1. Search Google for the supplier's product names and note the lowest prices from competitor listings on Google Shopping. [L1069.044]
  2. If competitors sell at a much higher price (for example $1,000 against a $200 wholesale cost), price the product at the market level and run Google Shopping ads for it. [L1069.044]
  3. If competitors sell close to the wholesale cost (for example $300 against a $200 wholesale cost), do not run Google ads for that product, because no one will buy at a loss. List it only if needed for catalog completeness. [L1069.044]
  4. If the market gives no usable price, set the price at three times the wholesale cost, provided shipping does not consume the margin. [L1069.044, L1069.045]
  5. Exclude non-MAP brands from the price match policy. [L1069.044]

Three-times formula (2019, older)

  • Selling price = wholesale cost × 3, as long as shipping cost does not wipe out the margin. Examples: $200 wholesale gives $600, and $500 wholesale gives $1,500. [L1069.045]
  • This 3x rule sits alongside the 2x floor. The 2x floor is the newer and more common guidance. Use 3x only when there is no usable market price. [L1069.045, L969.002, L1051.006]

What to do when competitors sell below 2x

The sources disagree on this case. Each applies to a different situation.

Source Action when competitors are below 2x
L969.002 (2022, older) Still list at a minimum of 2x. Rely on free traffic instead of paid ads.
L1051.006 (undated) Treat it as a red flag and do not upload the product.
L1069.044 step 3 (2019, older) Do not run Google ads for the product. List it only if needed for catalog completeness.
L855.046 (2023) List it at about double wholesale without ads. This keeps the store looking established.
L966.049 step 3 and step 4 (2025) Keep it at a markup you accept and exclude it from paid ads.
  • Choose the option that fits your margin. If the product cannot reach an acceptable margin at the market price, the sources mainly differ on whether to list it at all. [L1051.006, L969.002, L855.046, L966.049]

Avoiding price wars

  • Do not win non-MAP sales by undercutting competitors. Price cuts start a race to the bottom, where competitors undercut again until no margin is left. Match the average market price when margins allow it, and win customers through conversion optimization instead. [L1051.008]
  • Sell at MAP and do not manipulate feed prices. For non-MAP suppliers, match a low competitor price only when profit remains, because price wars leave everyone with less profit. [L469.071]
  • Pricing far below the market, for example selling a $1,000 market product at $800, starts a price war and gives away profit. Price with the market instead. [L1069.046]
  • Using the three-times rule without checking the market can leave money on the table, because the actual market price may be much higher than three times wholesale. Always check competitor prices first. [L1069.047]

Selling above MAP with trust

  • With a strong review history, a store can sell above MAP and still win sales. A small discount, such as $50 off a multi-thousand-dollar order, can close the sale when buyers want peace of mind. [L806.055]

Price-match protection for non-MAP suppliers

Procedure: protect prices when selling a supplier with no MAP policy (2019, older)

  1. Exclude that supplier's brands from your price match policy by adding a note with an asterisk and an exclusions list naming the brands on the price match page. [L357.039]
  2. Hide or remove the price match icon or guarantee from that supplier's product pages. [L357.039]
  3. Check the supplier's products on Google Shopping before contacting them. [L357.039]

Rules

  • Exclude suppliers without a MAP shown on the product page from the price match guarantee. Their products should not be covered by that promise. [L247.043]
  • If a supplier has no MAP policy, exclude its brand from the store's price match policy and check competitor prices before running ads on those products. [L1069.043]
  • Do not offer a price-match policy for suppliers with no MAP. Check Google Shopping before setting your price. [L468.099]
  • In markets without MAP policies, such as Australia or the UK, do not use price match guarantees. Set the sale price at least at double the wholesale cost. [L967.067]
  • Price-match guarantees must never cover items you sell at a loss because of a supplier with no MAP policy. Otherwise, a customer can later find the product cheaper and demand a refund of the difference. [L357.041]

Deciding whether to sell a product

  • Sell no-MAP products only when few sellers offer them. If many companies sell the same product, the margin will be hard to achieve. [L357.040]
  • Before contacting a no-MAP supplier, check how many companies sell its popular products on Google Shopping. [L357.040]

Deciding whether to advertise a product

  • If a supplier has no MAP and no margin, do not run ads to those products. List them at about double wholesale without ads, which keeps the store looking established. [L855.046]
  • Run paid ads only on products where the market price gives enough margin. Otherwise, list the product without ads. [L1069.044, L966.049]

Escaping restrictions with your own brand

  • Creating your own brand from a no-name supplier product lets you avoid pricing and channel restrictions. Once you have a private brand with no restrictions, you can consider other sales channels, but only after the main store is consistently strong. [L357.043] (Older advice, 2019.)

Escaping restrictions with bundles

  • If a supplier does not enforce MAP, combine multiple units into bundles and advertise them as a combo product. Build a separate product page for each bundle. [L852.054] (Older advice, 2020.)

Pattern: bundle differentiation (2021-22, older)

  • Combine the best-rated components from several suppliers, each already sold separately by its maker, into one starter kit under your own brand. Offer a recurring consumables subscription on top. [L1155.011]
  • Pattern: take the top-liked part from brand A, the kit from brand B and the consumable from brand C. White-label each, then sell them as one bundle plus a subscription. [L1155.011]
  • Example 1: a home brewing store white-labels a fermenter from one maker, a kit from another and a yeast refill from a third. It sells a starter set with monthly yeast delivery. [L1155.011]
  • Example 2: a coffee gear store white-labels a grinder, a brewing dripper and a filter paper pack into one starter bundle, with a monthly filter subscription. [L1155.011]

Case: 3D printer store (2021-22, older)

  • A store selling 3D printers ran for about one year. Customer comments showed that buyers liked the extruder from one maker, the kit from another and the filament from a third. [L1155.017]
  • The owner planned to ask each maker to white-label its part, combine the parts into one starter bundle under the store's own brand, and add a filament subscription. [L1155.017]
  • The owner sold the store before this plan was carried out. [L1155.017]

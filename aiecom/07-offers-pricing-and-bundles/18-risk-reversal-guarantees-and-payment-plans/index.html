7.18 Risk reversal, guarantees and payment plans

Risk reversal: the principle and the decision rule

  • Risk reversal moves the risk of a purchase from the customer to the seller. Buyers are risk-averse, so leaving all the risk with them lowers conversion. Risk reversal can take the form of a returns policy, a price-match guarantee, another satisfaction guarantee, or a worry-free installation or pre-assembled delivery for products that need setup. [L0411.015]
  • A money-back guarantee is the strongest offer element because perceived risk is the main purchase objection. It makes any offer more attractive, provided the supplier is reliable and product quality is good. [L0683.010]
  • Treat risk reversal as a must-have for making a lot of money in high-ticket dropshipping, because the customer must be protected if something goes wrong. [L0414.019]
  • Place the guarantee after the value stack, so it reinforces a strong offer. Stack the value first, then remove the risk with a money-back guarantee or another risk reversal, such as a statement that it costs nothing if the buyer is not satisfied. [L1365.039]
  • Make the guarantee the main headline when the guarantee is strong and the offer is clear. A guarantee boosts response, and the better the guarantee, the better the response. Strong guarantees outperform weak ones, even though some marketers claim guarantees are outdated. [L1370.077]
  • Guarantees must have no caveats if you make a big claim. A lowest-price guarantee should only exclude things like secondhand products. Every extra rule or state-specific exception makes the whole offer look weak. [L0411.019]
  • A guarantee is more convincing when it comes from someone the buyer knows and trusts, such as a local dealer who signs a written pledge to refund. A distant, unknown company's guarantee is less persuasive. [L1261.098]
  • Guarantees are not limited to products. They can apply to service levels such as lowest fare, maximum downtime or a fixed meal service time. [L1370.080]

Money-back and trial guarantees

Basic structure

  • Offer clear refund guarantees and money-back policies within a specified timeframe. If customers are unsatisfied, they receive a full refund. Guarantees eliminate purchase risk and build trust, directly increasing perceived value. [L0233.008]
  • Basic money-back guarantee: refund for any reason if the buyer is not fully satisfied. Use livelier wording such as "delighted" or "thrilled". If it is true, say why no competitor offers the same guarantee. [L1370.078]
  • Refund and keep the premium: the buyer can cancel, get a full refund and keep a free bonus gift. This makes the guarantee feel generous and certain. Redundancy also helps, so state the refund promise two or three times in different words, such as "a full refund of every penny paid". [L1370.079]
  • A 30-day money-back guarantee reverses the buyer's risk. Loss aversion makes this guarantee strong, because the shopper does not feel the purchase can go wrong. [L1207.037]
  • Pattern: "[number] day money back guarantee, [condition or reassurance]". A clear time limit turns a risky purchase into a reversible one. Examples: "60 day money back guarantee, no questions asked" and "14 day money back guarantee on every order". [L1207.038]
  • Pattern: a money-back guarantee tied to a specific short time and effort, such as "results in X minutes a day or your money back". It cuts the perceived time to results, reduces the effort required and lowers buying risk, which raises the value of the offer. Use it only after the product has been tested and its quality is confirmed.
    • Example 1: "Ease your morning stiffness in 5 minutes a day or your money back."
    • Example 2: "See a cleaner kitchen in 2 weeks of 10 minutes a day, or get a full refund." [L0871.042]

Trial periods and free examination

  • A trial period with a refund, such as three to four days or one week, lowers the buyer's risk and raises perceived value. Offer it only when the business can afford the refunds, and design the terms to keep the refund rate as low as possible. [L0502.010]
  • Offer a free examination period of 6 days, one week, 10 days or 30 days before the buyer pays. The seller pays shipping both ways, so returning the product costs nothing. Make clear that the buyer owes nothing unless he keeps the product. [L1369.009]
  • A free trial with a return window, such as returning the product for a full refund within 90 days, turns the guarantee into a no-risk trial. [L1370.080]
  • A 100-day free trial or lifetime warranty can work for furniture-type brands. Avoid a free-trial offer for a low-cost product that breaks often or for a stingy customer base, because it causes problems. [L1404.027]
  • Offer a service the buyer can test before paying, such as samples, a week of use or approval shipments, instead of asking for a purchase up front. Let the customer prove the product's claims at no cost or risk. [L1261.086]
    • Case: a sewing motor was offered for a one-week home trial with a technician who showed how to use it. About nine in ten trials led to a sale. The offer removed the risk of buying and let the product sell itself. [L1261.087]
    • A "try, then pay" offer converts far better than a "try, then return if not satisfied" offer. The customer's decision to keep is already made once they have used the product. Where possible, ship first and bill after use. [L1261.088]

Results-based and no-cost guarantees

  • Guarantee a result with a no-saving-no-cost offer. Ship a full trial batch at your own expense and charge only if the buyer's measured saving meets the stated level, such as 25 percent. If the saving falls short, the buyer keeps the trial at no cost. [L1369.008]
  • Just-pay-shipping offers (product free at checkout, customer pays shipping) can work, although they look scammy and need careful brand positioning. A variant requires a minimum-spend subscription after the free product, which produces recurring revenue and encourages use. [L1404.028]

Refund-on-request

  • Offer a refund only when a buyer contacts you and says the content is not valuable. Do not heavily advertise the guarantee. [L0513.021]

Guarantee wording and proof

  • Vague guarantees such as "satisfaction guaranteed" fail. They set no customer expectation and seem risk-free but are not credible. Specific guarantees such as "30-night money-back" or "7-day results or your money back" work because they communicate confidence and clarity. [L0313.010]
  • Example: the Posture Pro Corrector offered a 30-night money-back guarantee at a $39.99 price point. The ad claimed relief from back and neck pain in as little as 7 days. This removed purchase friction by offering a full refund if the benefit timeline was not met. The lower price ($39.99) than the discounted version ($49.99 regular, 20% off) suggests the offer is positioned as a trial price. [L0313.015]
  • Timeframe logic for Posture Pro: the 30-night money-back period is set against a claimed benefit of "as little as 7 days". This lets the customer verify the benefit well before the refund deadline. [L0313.020]
  • A 90-day money-back guarantee with no questions asked was shown on the landing page and used in the ads as a risk reversal. The guarantee is a USP that sits next to the mechanism and the price. [L0374.027]
  • Competitor pages can differ in guarantee length. One page advertised a 90-day 100% money-back guarantee and another a 365-day money-back guarantee. Guarantee length is an offer element you can test against conversion rate. [L1164.055]
  • A guarantee tied to a recognized seal or certification should state a clear refund or replacement window, such as two years. A concrete time limit reads as more credible than an open-ended promise. [L0340.082]
  • Case: a seller mailed about 2,000 packages with a guarantee to pay $25, or donate $50 to charity, if the recipient read the whole letter, watched the video, listened to the audio and still felt it was a waste of time. The guarantee cost roughly $100,000 at most if every claim was paid. The mailing produced a return of 10 to 15 appointments per 100 letters, and 2 to 3 of those converted into clients with about $10,000 first-year value each. [L1370.081]
  • Case: a seminar letter guaranteed the content. The author paid out less than $200 in claims from mailing to nearly 4,000 prospects and earned over $100,000 in profit. Split tests showed that adding a letter guarantee increases readership and response. [L1370.082]

Landing page reassurance

  • Lower perceived risk on the landing page by stating visible reassurance, such as a free trial period, easy cancellation, a money-back guarantee, and clear answers about returns, refunds, help and what happens if the product is not wanted. Place these near the hero and the offer. [L0918.007]
  • For subscriptions, use an "easy cancel" message that states there is no contract and that the customer keeps control. Flexible subscription plans lower the fear of being trapped. [L0919.028]

Returns and restocking fees

Matching return policies to suppliers

  • Return policy length should match each supplier's return policy. A 30-day return window can be offered on products priced from $1,000 to $2,500 only when the supplier accepts returns. (2020) [L0664.057]
  • Cap the return window rather than offering unlimited returns. Match the return policy to the supplier's policy, for example 30 days when the supplier allows 30 days. (2019, older) [L1069.053]
  • Your return policy does not have to match a competitor's policy. If the supplier does not accept returns for a product, set that product as not eligible for returns. (2018, older) [L1085.071]

Restocking fees

  • Set the restocking fee 10 to 15 percent above the supplier's restocking charge, to cover return shipping costs. Check competitors first, since not charging a fee may affect conversions. (2023) [L1094.080]
  • Return shipping fees can become the customer's responsibility if the return policy says so. Check how top competitors word their return policies first. (2023) [L1094.081]
  • Before matching competitors' restocking fees, which can reach 20 percent, or their upgraded delivery options, calculate your margins. Match those offers only if the margin still works after the cost. (2021, older) [L0801.045]
  • Mistake: advertising a 30-day return or free returns while hiding a 25% restocking fee destroys trust. On a $1,000 product that fee is $250. Any return conditions that make the promise conditional must be disclosed plainly, or the promise should not be made. (2021-22, older) [L0411.018]
  • Case: a seller changed the returns policy with little else changing, and monthly revenue rose from about $40,000 to about $80,000. The source notes that this scale of increase is not typical and that other businesses showed much smaller gains. (2021-22, older) [L0411.016]

Return fee options: Checkout Plus

Fee levels

  • Average US return fees are around $8 per return. The speaker estimated Australian fees at about $5 AUD but said this needed double-checking, so treat that figure as unverified. [L0955.006]
  • A common Checkout Plus baseline is a pre-purchase fee of $2.98, against a later return fee of $10. These two figures are a starting point that suits many brands, not a fixed rule. [L0955.007]
  • In the demo, the unlock fee was $1.98 on a $600 snowboard (total $601.98). Without it, a return triggered a $12.00 handling fee. The store can exclude certain items from the service. [L0955.018]
  • The right return fee depends on shipping rates, the product vertical and product weight, and on the brand's current return rate. Heavy or bulky products such as appliances or furniture can carry higher fees, and so can higher-AOV brands. [L0955.008]
  • Test fee levels before choosing one, for example $2.99 against $3.99, and measure uptake. The vendor recommends an A/B test first if a brand is skeptical about adding a cart fee. [L0955.016]

How it works

  • Checkout Plus is a pre-purchase option in the cart and checkout. The shopper pays a small fee to get a free return, avoiding a larger return fee later. Shoppers who do not opt in pay the normal return fee if they return. [L0955.009]
  • Checkout Plus can be framed as peace of mind rather than insurance. The shopper pays a small fee now to make sure a later return does not cost $10 to $15. [L0955.013]
  • Checkout Plus can be configured as a flat or a dynamic fee, with many customisable options. Package protection can be bundled with it, and the bundled fee is higher to cover both protection and free returns. [L0955.014]
  • Package protection is priced by cart value. The speaker's understanding is that it is about 2% of cart value, and the price rises when it is bundled with Checkout Plus. [L0955.015]
  • The Checkout Plus option is never added to the cart automatically. If the shopper does not opt in, it is not added at checkout. This keeps the shopper's decision intact. [L0955.019]
  • In the demo, the cart showed Checkout Plus at the bottom with the price and a details modal. [L0955.018]

Procedure:

  1. The shopper clicks the Checkout Plus button in the cart. [L0955.010]
  2. At checkout the option appears already selected or not, based on the cart choice, and the shopper can change it. [L0955.010]
  3. If the shopper later returns and opted in, the return is covered and no handling fee applies. [L0955.010]
  4. If the shopper did not opt in, the option still appears at checkout unselected, and a return triggers the normal return fee. [L0955.010]

Economics

  • Example economics: with a 20% return rate and 50% of shoppers opting in, the pre-purchase fees can fully cover return software and return shipping costs. The vendor says it absorbs part of this cost too. [L0955.011]
  • Pre-purchase revenue is roughly the share of shoppers who opt in, multiplied by the fee, compared against the share who actually return, multiplied by the return cost. Vendor data cited about 70% opt-in against typical return rates of 20-30%. The gap between opt-ins and returns covers return operations and often leaves a surplus. [L0955.012]
  • Shoppers who pay the fee are not necessarily the ones who return. The opted-in cohort's return rate was slightly higher, and the non-opted cohort's was slightly lower, so the overall return rate stayed stable. [L0955.017]

Price match and price guarantees

The MAP rule

  • Suppliers that enforce minimum advertised price (MAP) forbid resellers from selling below a set price. Because of this, a price guarantee is low-risk, since competitors cannot legally undercut you and rarely offer a lower price. [L1130.004]
  • Most online shoppers do not know that dropship suppliers enforce MAP, which requires all authorized retailers to advertise at the same price. Because customers are unaware of this constraint, they comparison-shop across competitors looking for discounts that legally cannot exist. This ignorance is why a price guarantee is such a powerful conversion tool: it appears to offer pricing assurance (which is genuinely true due to MAP) while creating almost no refund liability. [L0145.013]
  • Price matching works with MAP products because all sellers keep the same price. With non-MAP products, a competitor can cut prices by a large amount at any time, and you would be forced to match a price where no margin is left. [L1051.011]
  • A non-MAP competitor can cut its price by as much as 30% within a week. A price-match promise can force you to sell with no margin. Use price-match guarantees only when all sellers are bound by the same MAP, which keeps prices equal everywhere. [L1051.014]
  • A price-match promise only makes sense in MAP markets, where retailers sell at an enforced common price, so matching costs little. Outside MAP, the same promise forces you to match competitors' prices on products you would sell at a loss. [L1146.014]

Which suppliers can be covered

  • If your suppliers enforce MAP, offer a price-match policy and show it as a benefit on the site. Competitors often do not have one. [L0894.013]
  • Price-match guarantees only apply to MAP suppliers. Do not offer one for suppliers without MAP. [L0963.064]
  • Do not offer a price-match guarantee on non-MAP supplier products. Price-match guarantees only make sense with MAP pricing enforced. [L0962.101]
  • Products from non-MAP suppliers should be excluded from any price-match guarantee you offer. [L0671.009, L0671.160]
  • Products from non-MAP suppliers should be excluded from the price-match guarantee. A price-match guarantee on products without MAP can create conflicts with other sellers. [L0967.066]
  • Exclude suppliers that do not enforce MAP from any price-match guarantee. Otherwise customers can force you to match a price that does not make money. (2022, older) [L1281.033]
  • Do not offer a price-match policy on your website in markets without MAP. Customers could ask you to match prices on products where you would lose money, so the policy can only hurt you. [L1146.010]
  • Suppliers without MAP can be excluded from the policy, and those product pages should not show it. [L0970.107]

When to offer, and when to drop it

  • A price-match guarantee is optional. Competitors offering a six-month price guarantee can influence buyers, so a missing guarantee can cost sales. (2021, older) [L0671.051, L0671.190]
  • A price-match policy can be left off, especially at launch, since customers who find a slightly lower price elsewhere may ask for a refund. (2023) [L0970.107]
  • Remove the price-match policy when most of your suppliers do not enforce MAP. A few brand exclusions are acceptable, but if half or more suppliers are non-MAP, do not list the policy at all, because it causes confusion. (2023) [L0835.053]

Price-guarantee mechanics

  • The price guarantee applies to the price shown on the website before any coupon code is applied. If a competitor is cheaper, the store matches that price, so the guarantee should state this clearly. [L1130.005]
  • If you promise a price match, state it in one short line on the page and link to the full policy. The promise works as a trust element for resellers selling products that are also sold elsewhere. (2021-22, older) [L0409.046]
  • A price-match policy, if offered, should be published on the product page so buyers can see it without leaving. [L1346.023]
  • A price-match guarantee page promises that the store aims for the best price and will match a lower price a customer finds at another online store. It is one of the trust pages set up before launch, and the promise has to be one the store can actually keep. Write its conditions, such as the matching rules, only after the store has decided how it will handle them. (2021-22, older) [L1264.022]

Warranties

  • Contact your suppliers to ask about extended warranty options and their pricing. Extended warranties typically cost approximately $25 per product for one year of coverage. If your customers value warranty protection, include the extended warranty free with purchases to differentiate from competitors. [L0314.014]
  • Offer a warranty for expensive items as a single checkbox at checkout. If the customer does not tick it, send an automatic email shortly after the purchase offering the warranty with a discount. [L0334.020]
  • Extend.com is a Shopify app for selling extended warranties on high-ticket products. You earn a commission on each warranty sold. Extend.com handles all warranty fulfillment, claims processing and customer service. Source A members recommend it as a way to add revenue without additional operational complexity. (2023) [L0251.062]

Discounts and payment methods

  • Discounts and promo codes can be applied with every payment method, including installment providers such as Klarna. Do not exclude installment payments from the discount. (2021, older) [L1279.092]

Financing options: Shop Pay, Klarna, Affirm, PayPal Credit

Shop Pay Installments (Shopify rules)

  • Shop Pay Installments is available for orders from $35 up to $30,000. Orders below $35 do not qualify, and the financing limit for a single purchase is $30,000. [L1295.007]
  • Conflicting activation threshold: one source states that Shop Pay requires a minimum customer order value of $40-50 to activate as a payment option, and that products under $30 do not qualify. The Shopify tier above lists $35. Check the threshold in your own store's settings before setting any product price or messaging. [L0324.018, L1295.007]
  • For orders from $35 to $50, customers can pay in two interest-free bi-weekly installments of 50% of the total each. Customers can also simply pay the full amount at checkout. [L1295.008]
  • For orders from $50 to $999, customers can pay in four interest-free bi-weekly installments. For example, an $800 order would be paid over about two months with no extra cost. [L1295.009]
  • For orders from $150 up to $30,000, customers can pay in monthly installments. Interest rates depend on each customer's credit approval with Shopify. The merchant does not set or see these rates. [L1295.010]
  • For orders above $25,000, the customer pays $5,000 upfront to Shopify, and the remaining balance is split into equal monthly installments that carry interest. This case is rare and mainly applies to large B2B orders. [L1295.011]
  • Example: a hoodie priced at $44.50 falls in the $35 to $50 tier, so the product page shows an option to pay two interest-free installments of $22.25 each. Shoppers can ignore this and buy at full price, or choose the installment option at checkout. [L1295.012]
  • The installment tiers and interest terms are preset by Shopify and cannot be changed by the merchant. The merchant decides only whether the option is switched on for the store. [L1295.013]

Shop Pay financing limit (older figures)

  • Shop Pay financing limit varies by store. It was originally $500 and is now higher on many stores, but some stores still have lower limits. Check the financing limit before assuming it covers your average order value. If the limit is below your average order, keep Klarna as a backup. (2023) [L0274.044]
  • Conflicting limit: the $30,000 per-purchase figure above applies to the Shopify installment programme as currently described. The $500 original limit (2023) applies only where the store's own limit has not been raised. Use the limit shown in your store settings as the governing value. [L0274.044, L1295.007]

Deciding whether to add Klarna

  • When deciding whether to offer Klarna alongside Shop Pay: first check whether your average order value fits within Shop Pay's financing limit. If Shop Pay's maximum financing covers your typical order size, Klarna is not necessary. Add Klarna only if Shop Pay's limit is too low for your average order value and you want to offer more customers financing options. (2024) [L0253.050]
  • Buy-now-pay-later services (Afterpay, Klarna) increase customer acquisition, especially across the top five countries. The default risk falls entirely on the customer's credit. You receive full payment upfront from the BNPL provider, which eliminates merchant chargeback exposure. [L0324.042]
  • Offer payment options like Shopify Pay installments, Klarna or similar financing, so customers can spread payments, especially in weaker economic conditions. (2022, older) [L1113.075]
  • A financing app allows customers to pay in installments. It directly increases conversion rate by removing upfront payment barriers for price-sensitive buyers. [L0040.006]

Affirm and PayPal Credit

  • Offer PayPal Credit for customers. It gives six months with zero payments and 0% interest. Affirm integrates directly with Shopify, and both options can be offered together. (2019, older) [L1110.013]
  • Payment plan via PayPal Credit: a visitor with a PayPal business account can offer this option on purchases above $99, with six months of no interest and no payments. The seller receives the full amount right away, and the buyer pays PayPal. (2018, older) [L1085.067]
  • To accept PayPal Credit, use a PayPal business account and enable the option in the account settings. If the option is missing, contact PayPal through live chat to set it up. (2018, older) [L1085.068]
  • PayPal Credit is enough for a typical order value. Stores with an average order value near $10,000 use more customised financing options. (2018, older) [L1085.069]

Installment framing and payment plans

  • Concealed price through payments: present the cost as a small monthly or instalment payment rather than the full price. In one test, "$39.95", "2 payments of $19.95" and "3 payments of $19.95" produced no difference in sales. Use payment framing carefully and test it, since it can lift the price a buyer accepts. [L1370.073]
  • Case: a $5,000 business equipment item was sold as four payments of $1,250. A premium version at $7,000 was offered as eight payments of $875, which is a lower monthly payment than the cheaper version. 72 percent of buyers picked the higher-priced premium version. [L1370.074]
  • For higher-priced items, split payment into a small first installment and monthly payments. Examples: $5 down with the balance over nine months, $1 a month for 12 months, or $2 a month for a large set. A low monthly amount makes the decision feel small. [L1369.010]
  • An illustrative $1,000 product could be offered on a 12-month payment plan with roughly $100 due at the first payment. Showing that smaller first amount next to the full price can make a high price feel affordable to shoppers who would otherwise hesitate. [L1295.019]
  • Buyers who use installments carry the payment obligation, not the merchant. Keep the installment terms visible at the price point and the checkout so that the frame is not a surprise. [L1295.012, L1295.013]

Testing and decision steps

Testing a payment option

Procedure:

  1. Run the Shop Pay vs. Klarna test for one month. [L0274.043]
  2. Review the last three months of sales and note the Klarna transaction count. [L0274.043]
  3. Remove Klarna from the site by disabling the payment method. [L0274.043]
  4. Enable Shop Pay installments. [L0274.043]
  5. Run for exactly one month. [L0274.043]
  6. After the month, compare: did Shop Pay transactions exceed, match or fall below the Klarna baseline? [L0274.043]
  7. Decide whether to keep both, switch, or revert. [L0274.043]

Testing a fee or price level

  • Test fee levels before choosing one, and measure uptake at each level. [L0955.016]
  • Test guarantee length against conversion rate, since guarantee length is an offer element that can be measured. [L1164.055]

7.27 Handling pricing exceptions and cost shocks

Price Changes and Supplier Cost Increases

Tariffs and Wholesale Increases

  • Tariffs can raise both the wholesale cost and the MAP. The margin per sale can stay the same, but a sudden large price jump can make customers stop buying (sticker shock). [L0626.017]
  • MAP is usually a multiple of the supplier's wholesale cost. When wholesale rises, MAP rises, and every seller of that product raises prices. [L0972.039]
  • Rising supplier wholesale prices also raise MAP. A wider spread between wholesale and MAP helps cover extra costs, so cost increases should be passed on to every supplier's products at once. (Older: Source A, 2022.) [L1092.057]
  • When supplier prices rise, MAP rises too, which widens the margin. Update your prices right away when a supplier changes them, push the ad return, add paid upsells such as expedited or white glove shipping, and put more ad spend behind the high-margin products. (Older: Source A, 2022.) [L1113.076]
  • As an alternative to showing tariffs separately, add a percentage of the expected tariff cost into the sale price. [L1116.035]
  • When a supplier publishes a new MAP policy with updated prices, such as a tariff-related price change, update the listed prices to match the new MAP. [L0857.022]

Adjusting Only the Affected Suppliers

  1. Before raising prices, identify the suppliers where the gap between your price and wholesale price is too small to cover shipping. [L0801.041]
  2. Adjust only those suppliers' prices instead of every product on the site, since most suppliers leave enough margin. [L0801.041]

What Not to Do Under Shipping Cost Pressure

  • Do not respond to shipping cost pressure by raising prices above MAP, adding a large shipping fee when competitors do not, or dropping the brand first. Doing so can make the products unsellable, and money spent on their ads is then wasted. [L0852.041]

Worked Example: Free Shipping and Margin

  • Free shipping means higher carrier rates are paid from the product margin, so margins shrink even when the selling price stays the same. Waiting for suppliers to raise their wholesale prices only restores the gap in part. [L0942.017]

Supplier Dependence

Concentration Risk

  • A store that made about 80 percent of its sales from one main supplier faced a supplier that raised sale prices for shipping cost increases, cut the store's margin from roughly 20 percent to about 10 percent, and started selling directly. [L1456.016]
  • The response discussed was:
    1. Find additional suppliers over time.
    2. Lower Google Ads bids as an interim measure, while still aiming for at least break-even sales.
    3. Build bundles on low-margin products. [L1456.016]

Stock Shortages and Substitution

  • Substitute an out-of-stock item with a similar, cheaper item that is always in stock when the original is on backorder, and tell the customer about the lower price. This can raise margin when the substitute brand is less well known but customers accept it. [L1076.051]
  • Example: A supplier often had backorders on one item, so the store offered a similar, always-in-stock item that cost less, around 700 dollars instead of about 1,000 dollars. Customers who wanted the original got the price difference refunded, roughly 200 to 300 dollars, and the substitute shipped in about three days. Many customers accepted the substitute, and the margin on it was much higher because the brand was less known. [L1076.052]

Mispriced Orders and Supplier Outreach

Handling a Mispriced Order

  1. Check your supplier's current stock and pricing, and confirm whether the model is discontinued or the stock is a phantom unit that the inventory system wrongly shows. [L1472.022]
  2. Email the customer promptly, explaining the stock or price problem with a clear, polite apology. [L1472.022]
  3. Offer either the corrected price or a cancellation with a full refund, based on what your policy allows. [L1472.022]
  4. Ask the supplier to renegotiate the price, using the dealer cost and the mismatch as the reason. [L1472.022]
  5. Keep the full email thread saved, and check the status again until the order is closed. [L1472.022]

Customer Email Template (Corrected Price)

Hi {customer_name},

Thank you for your order of {product_name}. We have just discovered that {reason, for example: this model has been discontinued by the manufacturer or the item is no longer in stock}, and the price shown at checkout was entered incorrectly. The correct price is {corrected_price}.

You can either keep the order at this corrected price, or we can cancel it and refund you in full. Please let us know your choice by {deadline_date}.

We apologize for the mistake and appreciate your understanding.

{your_name}
{store_name}

[L1472.023]

Supplier Email Template (Renegotiation)

Hi {supplier_rep_name},

We have received order {order_number} for {product_name} at {price_paid}. The current price on your list is {list_price}, which means we are losing money on this order. Could you work with us on a revised price of {target_price}?

If we are not able to agree on a new price, we will have to cancel the order, but we would much prefer to keep working with you.

Thank you for your help.

{your_name}
{store_name}

[L1472.024]

Accepting Losses

  • Taking a loss on a mispriced unit is sometimes unavoidable. Occasional losses on a discontinued brand can be negative by a small amount, such as about $50. Accept that these cases are part of selling, but try to negotiate before you take the loss. [L1472.028]

Shipping Delays and Customer Retention

Gifts Instead of Discounts for Delayed Shipments

  • Never offer discount codes for delayed shipments. Instead, include a free product with an actual cost of $1-2 but a perceived value of $15-20 in the parcel, which generates gratitude and repeat purchases. [L0324.016]
  • Offering a 10% discount for late orders fails because customers question why they would reorder from a late supplier. Free gifts create emotional value and willingness to buy again. [L0324.017]

Concessions for Unhappy Customers

  • Offer a discount or a credit to keep an order when a customer is unhappy, as long as the remaining margin stays comfortable. A small concession such as an extra $50 off or an additional 3% can be better than losing the sale entirely. [L1472.038]
  • Offering a discount to retain a customer is better than making no money at all. Compare the cost of the concession with the loss of a cancelled sale, and choose a concession that still leaves a healthy profit margin. [L1472.040]

Escalating Resolution for a Delayed or Unhappy Order

  1. Offer a first, smaller credit, such as $25, to keep the order.
  2. If the customer still wants to cancel, offer a larger credit, such as $50.
  3. Keep the total concession within a margin you can afford, so the order still makes a profit. [L1472.039]

VAT and Market Entry

Testing a Market Before Registering for VAT

  • Consider the VAT question before selling into a country such as the UK. With VAT added, customers may be surprised by an extra cost at delivery and return the product. Test demand first, then decide whether VAT registration is worth it. [L0950.069]

Test a market with VAT in this order:

  1. Test demand in the market first without VAT changes.
  2. Calculate break-even including the extra VAT (for example 20%).
  3. Run ads only to that market separately.
  4. If the result does not exceed the higher break-even, stop the ads for that market. [L0950.070]

Pricing Checks

Free Products From Missing Prices

  • Shopify allows a product with no price to be sold for free. Check every product price against the supplier's pricing, because customers may believe a free order is real and dispute it. Pricing errors also need checking. (Source: Source B, 2021-22, older.) [L1145.033]

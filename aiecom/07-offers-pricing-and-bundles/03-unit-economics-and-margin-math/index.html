7.3 Unit economics and margin math

The full cost stack comes before any price

  • Before you set a price, list every cost per sale and per month: the total supplier price including product and shipping, payment fees, customer acquisition cost, fixed costs, and a reserve for returns. Only then do you know the break-even point and the margin each sale needs. [L0436.001]
  • A price set without payment fees, acquisition cost, fixed costs and refunds can turn a sale that looks profitable into a loss. Check the full cost stack before you trust a sticker price. [L0436.002]
  • Acquisition cost matters only for paid traffic. If most customers come through word of mouth, acquisition cost has little effect on pricing. If you buy traffic with paid ads, every price must cover acquisition cost on top of product cost and fees. [L0436.006]
  • Example: a product that costs $10 from the supplier and sells for $15 leaves too little room to cover acquisition, fees, returns and profit. With paid ads, a gap that narrow is very hard to make profitable. [L0436.007]
  • Use a profit calculator before creating any offer to calculate profit margins, set new ROAS and CPA targets, and verify that the offer stays profitable at scale. [L0232.002]
  • Reduce unit economics aggressively (product cost, operating expenses, fulfilment cost per order). Every dollar saved per unit becomes budget for advertising, which is the core lever for scaling. [L0041.008]
  • Wholesale means the cost of goods sold: only the product price paid to the supplier. Shipping is a separate cost and is not included in wholesale. [L0966.041]

Profit per sale formulas

  • Net profit per sale = MAP − COGS − ad budget per sale − shipping cost. Keep COGS at or below 50% of MAP and the ad budget per sale at or below 10% of MAP. [L0359.068]
  • Profit per sale = selling price (at or above MAP) − wholesale cost − shipping cost (if shipping is free) − advertising cost per sale. Keep advertising cost within about 10% of the selling price. [L0667.068]
  • Profit per sale = selling price − wholesale price − shipping cost − payment processing fee − advertising cost per sale. Heavy items with high shipping costs need this check before the product is listed, because margins can be very thin. [L0468.100]
  • Profit per order = selling price − supplier wholesale cost − card processing fees (about 2.9%) − advertising cost per sale − shipping cost, if you offer free shipping. Older advice (2021). [L0671.262]
  • Net profit per sale = MAP − wholesale − average shipping cost − ad cost per sale. Example: MAP $1,000, wholesale $500, shipping $150, ad cost $100 gives $250 profit, a 25 percent net margin. [L0966.044]
  • Profit before shipping per sale = MAP − wholesale − ad spend (up to 10% of MAP) − payment fee (2.9% of MAP). Example: MAP $1,000 and wholesale $500 leave about $371 before shipping, after $100 of ad spend and a $29 fee. [L0970.104]
  • Profit before ads = sale price − wholesale cost − shipping cost − Amazon fees (including the referral fee). Example: $19.97 − $3.00 wholesale − $1.50 shipping − $6.40 Amazon fees = $9.07 profit before ads. The Amazon referral fee in this example is 15 percent of the sale price. [L1153.026]
  • Net margin = (sale price − wholesale − shipping − ad cost per sale) ÷ sale price. Example: $1,000 − $500 wholesale − $150 shipping − $100 traffic leaves $250, which is 25%, below the 30% target the lesson sets. Older advice (2019). [L0663.055]
  • Break-even price for a bulky product sold with free shipping: minimum selling price = COGS + average shipping cost + other per-order costs (customer acquisition, card processing fees, traffic and the seller's time). If the price only equals COGS plus shipping, net profit is $0 before those other costs, so every sale loses money once they are added. [L1180.014]

Worked example at 30% off MAP with free shipping. A $600 product costs $420 from the supplier (600 × 0.70), leaving a $180 margin (600 − 420). The ad budget is up to $60 (10% of MAP), leaving $120 (180 − 60). Subtract the card fee of 2.9% of the sale and other order fees from that $120. Older advice (2020). [L0967.069, L0967.070]

Landed cost and shipping

  • Before you list a dropship product, add the supplier product price to the shipping cost for the destination country to get the landed cost. Example: a cup washer costs $5.95 for the chosen variant and totals $18.85 with shipping to a US address (shipping $12.90). Compare this with the planned selling price of $39.99. List the product only when the gap leaves enough room for ads, fees and profit. [L0635.032]
  • Test every product first against this baseline: the minimum profit margin should be at least two to three times the cost of shipping and fulfilling the item. [L0706.005]
  • Heavy and bulky items need the shipping check before listing, because the freight cost depends on size and weight, not on the selling price. [L0468.100, L0855.041]
  • Doubling the wholesale cost is not enough on its own. With an $80 product at a 2x markup, only $80 is available to cover shipping, ads, fees and profit. Older advice (2023 is not flagged; this statement is from 2023). [L0855.040]
  • Example: a $180 futon with $90 wholesale and $250 freight loses $160 per order. A $1,800 leather sectional with $900 wholesale and the same $250 freight keeps $650 per order. [L0855.042]
  • Example: a bulky home gym sells for $2,500 and the wholesale cost is $1,250, giving $1,250 gross. Shipping costs about $400, leaving $850. After the order processing fee, about $800 remains. Ads to acquire the customer cost about $100, leaving about $700 net. The lesson: bulky or heavy products only work when the selling price is high enough to carry the shipping costs. A $600 item that costs over half its price to ship is not worth selling. [L0551.056]
  • Freight and order costs for big items: with free shipping, a $500 item costing $250 leaves $250 of margin, while a $3,000 item costing $1,500 leaves $1,500. If free shipping costs about $300 on a large item, the cheap item drops to about $50, while the expensive one keeps about $1,200. Older advice (2021). [L0671.263]
  • Shipping cost depends on distance, so orders to far-away regions can cost much more than nearby ones. Calculate the average shipping cost per product and raise prices or charge shipping where profit is too thin. Older advice (2021-22 course). [L1201.024]
  • Example of a thin-margin order: a customer paid $500 for two units of the same product, with no discount, paid via PayPal, and no sales tax. The supplier total was $475 ($250 for products, $225 for shipping). The PayPal fee was $14.80 and fraud insurance was $5. Result: gross profit $25, net profit $5.20, net margin 1.04%. Here shipping almost equals product cost, which signals that the price should go up or shipping should be charged. Older advice (2021-22 course). [L1201.011]
  • Batch size must match product margin. Small batches cost more to ship per unit, which is affordable only if each unit earns enough margin. On a $400 product, small-batch shipping is a minor issue. On a $50 product, high shipping costs can wipe out profit. [L1508.031]
  • Example of spread versus shipping, stand-up desks (average package 5 ft × 6 in × 24 in, average weight 100 lb, average shipping $200 per order). Older advice (2022).

  • Spread = MAP price − wholesale cost. Net after shipping = spread − average shipping cost per package, because free shipping is paid from the margin. Older advice (2022). [L0854.026]
  • A budget desk with MAP $400 and wholesale $200 leaves a $200 spread. After $200 average shipping, nothing is left, and the 2.9% transaction fee then makes the sale a loss. Older advice (2022). [L0854.028]
  • Ad costs and merchant fees must come out of the spread after shipping. A remaining $150 can look fine but may not cover them, so check whether the product is still worth advertising before you scale it. Older advice (2022). [L0854.029]

Spread between MAP and wholesale

  • Margin before expenses = MAP − wholesale cost. With a MAP at 2x wholesale, a $500 wholesale cost leaves $500 to cover ads, fees and shipping. [L0942.016]
  • The starting point for profit is the spread between MAP and wholesale. Net profit should aim for 30%; the other levers are customer acquisition cost and shipping cost. [L0965.039]
  • Profit margin is determined by the spread between wholesale cost and MAP. After negotiating supplier pricing, the biggest remaining costs are shipping and ad spend. Older advice (2023 course is not flagged). [L1094.082]
  • At a selling price of 2x wholesale, gross margin = (selling price − wholesale cost) ÷ selling price. Example: (1,000 − 500) ÷ 1,000 = 50% before card fees, shipping and ad costs. These remaining costs must come out of this 50% to reach a net profit of about 30%. [L0969.114]
  • Margins below the 50% spread are not a standard target. A lower margin can be acceptable only when each sale still brings in large absolute profit, such as a very expensive item or a large bulk order. [L0970.105]
  • A high product price alone does not justify a low margin. An item priced around $4,000 with a 10% margin is still not acceptable. [L0970.106]
  • A 25% profit margin does not mean paying 75% of the sale price for the product. After cost of goods sold, the remaining money must cover shipping, ad costs, processing fees, team costs and net profit. Older advice (2018). [L1109.019]
  • On a $1,000 sale, wholesale should be $500 or less, leaving about $500 for shipping, ads, fees and profit. The minimum target in this example is $250 net profit, which is 25% of the sale price. Older advice (2018). [L1109.021]
  • With careful control of shipping, ads and fees, net profit margins can reach about 40%. Margins below that depend on the same cost control. Older advice (2018). [L1109.022]
  • Doubling the wholesale cost is not enough; see the spread-and-freight case above. [L0855.040, L0855.041]

Payment fees

  • Card processing fee of about 2.9% of the sale is used in several older worked examples. [L0671.262, L0970.104, L0854.028]
  • Calculator gateway rule: the standard Shopify Payments example is 5.5% of the price plus $0.30. Use your own Shopify Payments rate if it differs. [L0986.001, L0986.002]
  • Worked gateway figures in the calculator: a $40 price on site gives a gateway fee of $2.50 (5.5% × 40 + 0.30), and an $80 price on site gives $4.70 (5.5% × 80 + 0.30). [L0986.008, L0986.009]
  • In the PayPal example above, the fee of $14.80 on $500 is about 2.96%, and fraud insurance of $5 was also paid. [L1201.011]
  • Payment fees are paid from the margin along with shipping and ads. Their total must be subtracted before any net-profit check. [L0671.262, L0962.098]

Ad cost per sale, break-even ROAS and break-even CPA

Break-even ROAS and CPA

  • Break-even ad spend per day = number of sales needed × average order value. Equivalently, sales needed per day = daily ad spend ÷ average order value. [L0378.032]
  • Break-even cost per purchase = selling price − (product cost + shipping cost). Example: a $39.90 product with a $17 fulfilment cost yields a margin of about $22. Check: $39.90 − $17 = $22.90, so the source's $22 is rounded down; with $22.90 the break-even ROAS is 1.74, not 1.77. [L0440.037]
  • Break-even ROAS = selling price ÷ margin before ads. Example: $39.90 ÷ $22 gives about 1.81 in the source's own $22 margin; $39.90 ÷ $22.90 gives 1.74. The source states 1.77. Ad campaigns must keep ROAS above break-even to make money; below it means a loss. [L0440.038, L0440.037]
  • Break-even ROAS is the return on ad spend at which ad revenue only covers product cost and fees. Example: product cost $18 and selling price $39.99 gives a break-even ROAS of 1.85 in the Dropship Toolkit. Product cost alone gives $39.99 ÷ $21.99 = 1.82, so the 1.85 depends on the tool's fee assumptions. If actual ROAS is above break-even, the product makes money. If it is below, raise the price or choose a product with a lower cost of goods. [L0661.025]
  • Break-even ROAS in the calculator = total revenue ÷ margin $. Buy 1 gives $43 ÷ $32.50 = 1.32; Buy 2 get 1 free gives $83 ÷ $60.30 = 1.38. [L0986.007, L0986.008, L0986.009]
  • Target cost per acquisition (CPA) must be below break-even cost per purchase. For a product sold at $30 that costs $10, acquire each customer for under $20, because that difference is the margin available for advertising. [L0440.039]
  • At the start, the margin of $20 is calculated without advertising expenses. Other costs such as apps, Shopify and hiring are considered later. Once a product has run for more than a month, reserve about 5 to 10 percent of product plus shipping cost for these. [L0440.040]
  • For a store with several price points, calculate break-even ROAS from the lowest-priced product at the start. After about 100 orders, switch to the actual average order value and the average cost of product plus shipping. [L0645.044]
  • Work out break-even and selling price before setting the campaign structure. For a $40 product, the maximum CPA is around $15-$18. [L1195.044]
  • Ad budget ceilings: keep ad cost per sale at or below 10% of MAP. The 10% rule is a budget ceiling for net-profit planning; break-even CPA is the point where a sale stops making money. These answer different questions, so check both. [L0359.068, L0667.068, L0970.104, L1195.044]
  • Margin cushion: do not run a store with margins so thin that one day of ad account neglect could lose money. Keep enough room in each product's margin to absorb mistakes and ad fluctuations. Older advice (2021). [L0962.099]

ROAS targets for paid launches

  • When launching a product on paid ads, start at a medium price point rather than a very cheap one. The minimum acceptable starting point is a 3.5x return on ad spend (ROAS), with a long-term target of around 8x. [L1215.006]
  • Raising average order value (AOV) can turn an unprofitable item into a profitable one, because the extra revenue covers the margin gap. Measure profitability per order after each AOV change. [L0920.019]

Gross versus net margin

Gross margin

  • Markup to gross margin: 43% markup on wholesale ≈ 30% gross profit; 54% markup on wholesale ≈ 35% gross profit. [L0255.004]
  • Gross margin per unit = selling price − supplier cost, before transaction fees, shipping and advertising. Example: a product sold at $38.99 with a supplier cost of about $20 leaves roughly $19 per unit before those other costs. [L0372.028]
  • Compare products by profit per order and by how many orders the same total profit requires, not by margin percentage alone. A $50 product with $20 profit has a 40% margin, but a $3,000 product with $900 profit has only a 30% margin and still earns 45 times more per sale. Older advice (2021-22 course). [L0771.069]
  • A typical dropship net margin is about 30% of total sales revenue. [L0011.002]
  • Business margins: about 23% after negotiating volume discounts with suppliers. [L0226.036]
  • A margin of 25% or higher is the target for gross margin on products. Products with thinner margins often cannot carry the cost of paid ads. Older advice (2021). [L0853.022]
  • Gross margin in the calculator: Margin $ = Total Paid − Total Cost For Us, where Total Cost For Us = COGS + Pick N Pack + Shipping + Gateway. Margin % = Margin $ ÷ Total Paid. The margin is therefore after product, fulfilment, shipping and gateway fees, and before ad spend. [L0986.007, L0986.001]
  • The lesson's 70% profit margin threshold for slight price cuts below competitors does not say whether it is gross or net. Calculate it only after COGS and ad spend are subtracted, so the number reflects what is actually kept. [L1149.013]

Net margin

  • Net profit margin after all costs must be at least 20% and aim for 30%. Costs include cost of goods, shipping, ads, Shopify fees, merchant fees and other expenses. Older advice (2021). [L0962.098]
  • Target an average net profit margin of about 30% across the business after all costs. Do not treat the MAP-to-wholesale spread alone as the margin, because shipping and ads still need to be paid from it. [L0966.045]
  • Net profit should aim for 30% of the sale. A supplier that gives only about 15% margin leaves very little room for ad spend, so reaching a 30% net profit target usually requires very low customer acquisition costs. Check this before you build a catalogue around that supplier. [L0965.039, L0965.087]
  • Target profit after shipping is about 25 to 30 percent of the selling price, measured after freight. Older advice (2023 course is not flagged). [L0855.045]

Dollar and ratio tests

  • A typical target margin is about $20 per product. The final price should be set after you decide on bundles, bundle deals and volume discounts, since these change the margin. [L1219.011]
  • Choose products with a minimum margin of about $20. Smaller margins make it harder to get sales and lead to more returns, complaints and chargebacks as order volume grows. When competitors charge close to the same price, increase average order value through bundles to improve profit. [L0440.035]
  • Ratio example: a sourcing cost of about $20 and a selling price of about $40 was described as a very good ratio for a product. [L0530.025]
  • The wholesale cost should be about 50 percent of the selling price, which is the typical target. Lower margins make it difficult to pay for shipping and ads. Older advice (2020). [L0551.054]
  • A product with a 30% gross margin is thin, so its pricing should be reviewed before scaling. Thin-margin products leave little room for chargeback and refund costs. [L0351.045]
  • A product is only a winner if its profit margin is at least 18 to 25 percent. A product that loses money does not count as a winner, even if it sells. [L0661.024]
  • Margin below 15%: reject the supplier, because ad costs, shipping and payment processing fees must still be covered. A healthy margin is needed on top of these costs. Older advice (2019). [L0960.034]
  • If wholesale includes shipping, or shipping is charged separately, the total landed cost still needs a margin around 30%, not 15%. Ad costs and processing fees must be paid from that margin. Older advice (2019). [L0960.035]
  • Do not launch a store if most products have average margins of about 10 percent before ad costs. Negotiate better prices or drop low-margin products from the store. Older advice (2021). [L0962.097]
  • Example: a store offering free shipping, a free gift and a price-match guarantee had margins too thin on about half its products. Removing the price-match guarantee first restored margins while keeping free shipping and the free gift. Older advice (2021). [L0962.100]
  • Returns: use a return rate of about 1% to 2% at most for drop-shipped products. If a supplier has no bad reviews, returns do not need to be built into product costs. Older advice (2021). [L0853.041]
  • Reserve for returns: list a reserve for returns among the costs per sale before setting any price. [L0436.001]
  • Thin margins and low-margin suppliers are a problem on every scale: the margin rule applies to all products. [L0962.099, L0962.097]
  • Margin rule for shipping: the minimum margin should be at least two to three times the shipping and fulfilment cost. [L0706.005]
  • Small-margin products cannot absorb mistakes at scale; keep margin for returns, complaints and chargebacks. [L0440.035, L0351.045]

Pricing and minimum-margin sanity checks

  • With a MAP at 2x wholesale, the margin before expenses equals the wholesale cost (see spread section). [L0942.016]
  • A product should leave a clear margin before you run any offer; do not discount below the threshold. [L1148.022, L1204.069]

Pricing from cost: setting the selling price

  • Selling price = total cost per order ÷ (1 − target margin). Total cost per order includes product cost, credit card fees, merchant account fees, shipping and fulfilment cost per unit. For a 70% margin, price = total cost ÷ 0.30. [L1182.005]
  • Example: for a product costing $8 with a 70% margin target, raise the price until margin reaches 70% after fees, shipping and fulfilment are added. The sheet shows the resulting selling price and margin, which you check against the 67-70% range before using the price. [L1182.006]
  • Set the selling price so that gross margin reaches 70% of the price, the main pricing target for every product. If 70% is not reachable, 69%, 68% or 67% is the acceptable minimum. [L1182.001]
  • Gross margin on the calculator target applies to offers; use the product-level benchmarks above when screening products. The two measure different bases, and a product with product cost at 50% of price will not reach 70% after fees. [L0986.010, L1182.001, L0551.054]
  • Price the product at about three times the wholesale cost when it is not MAP-protected and still worth listing. Older advice (2019). [L1069.091]

Procedure: fix a product that shows no profit at MAP with flat-rate shipping (older advice, 2019)

  1. Calculate the profit per sale as the selling price minus the wholesale cost minus the flat-rate shipping cost. [L1069.091]
  2. If the profit is zero or negative, ask the supplier's sales rep whether a different price list applies to your account. [L1069.091]
  3. If no different price list exists and competitors sell at break-even, do not list the product. [L1069.091]
  4. If the product is still worth listing and is not MAP-protected, price it at about three times the wholesale cost, do not run Google ads on it, and rely on organic sales. [L1069.091]

Offers, bundles and discounts

  • Every offer must make financial sense. The free item or gift must be affordable, and the offer must still produce profit after ad spend and product costs. [L0650.035]
  • An offer can never reduce the price to zero. Each discount, free item or bonus must still cover product cost, shipping and ad spend, so calculate the margin before running it. A free gift only makes sense when its cost is low compared with the order value it is tied to. [L1204.069]
  • Example of a cheap novelty product: sold at $13 with a supplier cost of $2 to $3 from AliExpress, leaving roughly $10 gross margin per unit before fees. The presenter judged this healthy for a low-return, low-risk item. [L0655.018]
  • Product cost examples for a bundle: a necklace costs about $4-5 to make, a jewellery box about $1, and a three-item bundle about $13, giving a margin of about $17 on a $29.90 price. [L0502.021]
  • Bundles for buy-X-get-Y: enter the total supplier cost of all units in the bundle and the total price the customer pays, not per-unit figures. Mixing per-unit cost with bundle price distorts margin and break-even ROAS. [L0986.011]
  • For a buy-X-get-Y offer, Price Before Discounts must be the full price of every unit in the bundle, including the free units. With a single unit at $40, Buy 2 get 1 free uses $120 here. An $80 Price On Site then shows a $40 saving and a Savings % of 33.33%. [L0986.020]
  • Enter Price Before Discounts as the compare-at (strike-through) price the store displays next to the sale price, because the sheet calculates Savings and Savings % from it. The savings then match the discount shoppers see on the product page. [L0986.021]
  • To compare a bundle with a single unit, divide the bundle's Price On Site and its COGS by all units in the bundle, including free ones. For Buy 2 get 1 free at $80 price on site and $18 COGS across 3 units, this gives about $26.67 revenue and $6.00 cost per unit, against $40 for a single unit. [L0986.022]
  • Discount test example: Product B, with $450 supplier shipping and free customer shipping, drops to $300 gross profit (12% gross margin) and $202.20 net profit (8.09% net margin) after fees. With a 5% discount, gross profit is $175; with 10%, $50; with 15%, −$75. Older advice (2021-22 course). [L1148.022]
  • New stores often do not sell at a profit at first and may only break even. The list price, supplier cost, negotiated supplier cost, and whether shipping is charged to the customer are all adjustable levers that take time to tune. Older advice (2021-22 course). [L1148.024]
  • Test different price points and offer structures in the calculator before launching them, because each change moves margin, break-even ROAS and target CPA. Comparing scenarios on paper shows which offer can still pay for ads. [L0986.013]
  • Bundles raise average order value, which improves profit per order. [L0440.035, L0920.019]
  • Discount-level spreadsheet: the build-phase product pricing sheet (Product / Supplier Pricing) takes each product's list price, supplier cost and shipping paid by the customer. It shows gross profit and gross margin at 5%, 10% and 15% off. Check it before any discount to see how much margin each level leaves. Older advice (2021-22 course). [L1246.016]

Procedure: setting an offer for a new store with no sales history (older advice, 2018)

  1. Compare the MAP price of each product with its wholesale cost. [L1085.070]
  2. Ask the supplier for sample shipping costs. [L1085.070]
  3. Calculate the expected profit per sale from these numbers. [L1085.070]
  4. Base the offer on that profit. Do not choose a discount without these figures. [L1085.070]

Margin calculators and sheets

Calculator procedure (Margins & Offers sheet)

  1. Make your own copy of the shared margin calculator sheet. [L0986.001]
  2. List every offer you run or plan to test, such as Buy 1, Buy 2 get 1 free or Buy 3 get 2 free; the list depends on your product. [L0986.001]
  3. For each offer, enter the COGS as the total supplier cost of all units the customer receives in that offer. [L0986.001]
  4. Enter the Pick N Pack cost and the Shipping cost you pay; if shipping is already included in COGS, leave Shipping at 0. [L0986.001]
  5. Check that the Gateway column uses your Shopify Payments rate, the standard example being 5.5% of the price plus $0.30. [L0986.001]
  6. Enter Price On Site as the total the customer pays for the offer, for example $80 for Buy 2 get 1 free. [L0986.001]
  7. If you charge shipping, enter the Shipping Price the customer pays. [L0986.001]
  8. Read Total Paid / Total Rev, Margin $, Margin % and BE ROAS for the offer. [L0986.001]
  9. Enter Price Before Discounts to see Savings and Savings %. [L0986.001]
  10. Read Target CPA for 2+ ROAS and for 2.5+ ROAS. [L0986.001]
  11. Confirm that Margin % is at least 70%. [L0986.001]
  12. Fill every cell for each offer you run before you use its numbers. [L0986.001]

  • Aim for a gross margin of at least 70% on every offer before you run ads for it. The 70% figure is the minimum target in this approach, and an offer below it has little room for ad costs. [L0986.010]

Filtering products by net profit per sale

  • Start from the spread: MAP minus wholesale, then take out shipping, fees and ads. A product that does not leave enough after these is not a candidate. [L0966.044, L0854.026, L0970.104]
  • Keep a product only if its net profit per sale is above roughly 10%. Products below this are not worth listing. Older advice (2019). [L1069.049]
  • Net profit per sale check (target 30% net, 20% floor): use the net-profit formula for each candidate, then compare against the threshold. [L0962.098, L0966.045]
  • Compare candidates by profit per order and by how many orders the same total profit requires, not by margin % alone. [L0771.069]
  • For a catalogue with hundreds of products, do not calculate every item. Focus on the best sellers, which are often fewer than 20 out of 500 products and give the highest leverage. For large catalogues, group products into price buckets, such as $2,000, $1,700, $1,500 and $1,300, and use averaged targets per bucket. Older advice (2021-22 course). [L0446.014]
  • Shipping-heavy items with high shipping costs need the net check before listing, because margins can be very thin. [L0468.100]
  • Amazon net check: profit before ads = sale price − wholesale − shipping − Amazon fees. [L1153.026]
  • Products whose net profit is below the threshold and that show no profit at MAP with flat-rate shipping: see the procedure above. [L1069.091]
  • Do not launch a store on a supplier list where most products are around 10% margin before ads. [L0962.097]

7.4 Setting the base selling price

Placeholder prices during the build

  • Enter a fixed placeholder price such as $69 in the Price field during the build, not an empty field, so the preview shows a realistic layout. Replace it once the final margin and price are decided. [L1331.013]
  • Demo product prices should be realistic but need not be exact, because they are placeholders that get updated when real supplier products are added to the store. [L0088.009]
  • Price demo products in line with the niche's actual market prices. Example: selling price $799, compare-at price $999, cost per item $300. [L0036.011]

Inputs to collect before setting a price

  • Price every product for the USA market by default, because most customers are there, and use US costs and US fees when filling in the sheet. [L1182.002]
  • Set the price from several inputs together: supplier cost, the margin needed after all per-order costs, competitor prices, and the value the customer perceives. Product cost only sets the minimum and does not determine the final price on its own. [L1182.019]
  • Include ad spend and COGS (cost of goods sold) in the calculation before fixing the final price. Do not decide the price from product cost alone. [L860.009, L1149.002]
  • Base prices on healthy margins that cover payment processing fees, shipping costs, ad spend and profit. Do not base pricing solely on competitors' prices, because low-margin competitors usually give poor service and fail within years, so long-term success needs sustainable margins. [L0274.018]
  • Calculate the total dropshipping price for the chosen variant and destination country from the supplier platform, then add payment fees, the estimated customer acquisition cost, fixed costs per sale and a return allowance. [L0436.012]
  • Payment fees are not in the 3x split (see Markup rules). At a $29.99 price, a 1.5% to 3% gateway fee costs about $0.45 to $0.90, which comes out of the $10 margin, so subtract it before accepting the price. [L0436.026]

Full pricing procedure

  1. Calculate the total dropshipping price for the chosen variant and destination country from the supplier platform. [L0436.012]
  2. Add payment fees, an estimated customer acquisition cost, fixed costs per sale and a return allowance. [L0436.012]
  3. Research competitor prices for the same or a similar product. [L0436.012]
  4. Set a starting price and check it with the 3x rule. [L0436.012]
  5. Adjust the price against the market and pick a discount strategy. [L0436.012]
  6. Launch with ads and test prices and offers. [L0436.012]

Markup rules

2x: the minimum floor

  • The simplest starting rule is to sell at no less than 2x wholesale, using the supplier's MAP (minimum advertised price) as the reference. This leaves room for shipping, ads and fees. [L0854.031] (older, 2022)
  • A rough check: wholesale cost should be about half the selling price (MAP). The spread must still leave money after expenses. [L0855.039]
  • Minimum sale price = 2 x wholesale cost. A product with a wholesale cost of $500 should sell for at least $1,000. In a MAP market, the sale price column is set by MAP instead. [L1146.006]
  • Example: a stand-up desk with a wholesale cost of $500 and an MSRP of $1,200 has a strong margin built in. If competitors sell it for $900, do not list it at $900, because the minimum is $1,000 (2x wholesale). [L1146.007]
  • If competitors already sell above the 2x minimum, price at or near their level instead of at 2x, and do not undercut the market. Example: with a $500 wholesale cost and competitors at $1,200 on Google Shopping, sell above the $1,000 minimum. [L1146.008]
  • The 2x calculation works in any currency, because only the numbers change. A wholesale cost of 500 in any currency gives a minimum price of 1,000 in that same currency. [L1146.017]
  • Conflict with a blanket doubling: do not arbitrarily double wholesale prices. Validate the price against market rates. (older, 2022) [L0249.034]
  • Resolution: the 2x figure is a floor to check against the market, not a default to apply to every product. [L0249.034, L0854.031, L1146.008]
  • Never set the sale price by copying what competitors charge at the moment. Base it on wholesale cost using the 2x rule and the competitor check. [L1146.005]

2.5x: testing and strong-brand starts

  • Start at least at 2.5x if you are new. [L0645.019]
  • A markup of about 2.5x is a reasonable starting price when a product has high perceived value and several marketing angles or benefits. Lower competitor prices do not rule this out if branding is strong. [L1217.060]
  • For a first test, start at 2.5x to 3x and raise prices as branding is added. [L0335.062, L0335.028]

3x: the standard target

  • Aim for a markup of at least 3x, for example buying at $10 and selling at $30 or more. [L0335.028]
  • Selling price at about three times the total dropshipping cost, then check whether the market accepts it. $10 total cost gives a $29.99 price: $10 for the product, $10 for customer acquisition and $10 as margin. [L0436.019]
  • The same formula, stated as: selling price = 3 times supplier cost, covering supplier cost, customer acquisition cost and profit margin. Example: supplier costs $10, price $30. [L1172.030]
  • Round the 3x result down to a.99 ending. A $10 total dropshipping cost gives 3x = $30, which becomes $29.99. [L0436.027]
  • Set the selling price at a minimum of 3 times product cost, for example a $10 product sold at $30. Treat a $20 minimum selling price as the bare minimum. [L0440.036]
  • Aim for at least 3x the cost of product plus shipping. If product and shipping cost $10, the price should be at least $30, otherwise the product is hard to scale profitably. [L0708.005]
  • Aim for 3x the cost of product plus shipping, and start at 2.5x if you are new. Keep a minimum margin of $20 when the product price is low. [L0645.019]

Organic versus paid traffic

  • Organic sales can usually be priced at two to three times cost, which leaves room to cover taxes and possibly shipping. Paid ad sales need a higher price than organic, because ad costs come on top of the product cost. [L0706.006]

$10 $20 Organic Workable, leaves room for taxes and shipping
$10 $30 Organic Better margin

4x: branded and visual products

  • Fashion products can usually carry a higher markup of around 4x once the store has custom images or videos and branding, because buyers pay for lifestyle and visuals. [L0335.062]
  • In a fall sock example, one pack was sourced for about $5 and sold for $20, a 4x markup. The lesson describes this as a standard single-item markup. [L0347.015]

10x: high perceived value

  • For products with high perceived value, such as jewelry, the selling price can be about 10 times the sourcing cost when buyers believe the item is worth it. A product bought for about $5 can be sold at roughly $50. Perceived value sets the ceiling, not the cost. [L0693.060]

Fees, low-priced items and currency gaps

  • Build the currency difference into the price when buying from US suppliers in US dollars and selling in Canadian dollars. Otherwise the exchange rate gap can erase the margin. (older, 2021-22) [L0905.017]
  • To offset eBay commission and payment processing fees on low-priced items, apply a markup rule to the product feed: any product below a set price is increased by a fixed percentage. For example, a fee of about 0.30 GBP per order can make cheap products unprofitable without a markup. [L1441.040]
  • A cheap product bought at about $10 or less on AliExpress was sold at $29.99, matching competitors. The margin was about 60%, so price matching made it workable for a low-cost product. [L1444.052]

Perceived value and premium positioning

  • Premium positioning allows higher prices when differentiation exists. Selling supplements at 50% to 100% above competitors is possible when a unique formula or packaging justifies the premium. Perceived value drives willingness to pay. [L0227.025]
  • A product with high perceived value can be priced well above its supplier cost, for example a $23 to $25 supply cost with a potential selling price near $50. Check the supplier cost of the exact variant before assuming the margin. [L0372.029]
  • A security camera that works like a light bulb went viral (video of about 6.7 million views, with a second video still growing). It sold for $38.99, with a supplier cost estimated at about $20. A similar product on Amazon was listed around $69. The estimated margin was considered good, and the perceived value suggested a price up to around $50, although TikTok buyers might not accept that. [L0372.027]
  • A product that started at $49.99 was later sold at $89.99 without changing the product itself. The increase came from custom branding, custom packaging, custom content, good images and videos, and a branded website. [L0363.015]
  • Strong product images and branding can raise perceived value enough to support a higher price in niches such as jewelry. Example: a necklace that once sold at $12.99 can be sold at $19.99 with good images, then offered as BOGO. [L1345.014]
  • A case with a buy-cost of about $20 and a break-even of about $52 leaves room for a launch price of $49 or $59. [L1194.036]
  • When a supplier sells versions of one product that differ in a single feature, such as a hood or open feet, the version with the extra feature can be priced higher. The price gap should match a feature the buyer can see on the product page. [L1194.057]
  • When a viral product's selling price feels too high for its perceived value, look for a cheaper variation or another supplier that brings the price into the target range before running ads. Test only once buyers can see clear value at that price. [L0530.036]

Platform differences

  • The same product can carry a different price on a different platform, because each platform's audience accepts a different price. Example: a store running ads only on Pinterest sold the same product at about $38. [L0871.092]

Competitor and Google Shopping benchmarking

Where to look

  • If no MSRP exists, search Google for competitor prices and match them. (older, 2022) [L0249.034]
  • Compare competitor prices using the item price shown for the same product on Google Shopping. This is the price to benchmark against. (2024) [L0971.095]
  • Do not base the selling price on Amazon prices, which are usually lower. Benchmark against competitors selling the same product on Facebook, since that is the platform used. [L0693.022]
  • Ignore very low prices from competitor stores that look like low-effort tests, such as a product crossed out at 40 and listed at 20. These prices are not a reliable benchmark. [L0693.023]
  • Compare your prices with competitors to decide whether to match, undercut or charge more. A higher price is defensible only when the customer perceives more value in your store. [L0436.013]
  • A common starting benchmark is a retail price of about double the product cost. Verify this against competitor prices on Facebook before setting the final price. [L0693.024]
  • Use the Pricing Research columns of the sheet: MSRP or RRP, MAP and competitor prices, plus whether competitors charge for shipping. Use them to compare your price against the market while moving the list price toward the best balance of sales and profit. (older, 2021-22) [L1148.025]

Shipping in competitor averages

  • When estimating a competitor average price, include shipping if it appears on most Google Shopping listings for that product. Otherwise average only the product prices. (older, 2021) [L0968.076]
  • Do not inflate the sale price to include shipping. Instead, set the MAP high enough that the markup covers shipping, customer acquisition and profit margin. (older, 2020) [L0268.023]

Competitor research procedure

  1. Find several competitor listings for the same or a very similar product. [L1167.011]
  2. Note the one-time purchase price and the subscription price for each listing. [L1167.011]
  3. Note the spread between the lowest and highest price, and any subscription discount shown. [L1167.011]
  4. Choose your price inside that range and set it in the Pricing section of the product. [L1167.011]
  • Example: for a similar toothpaste tablet product, competitor listings ranged from $32 for a one-time purchase up to $48 depending on the purchase option. One listing offered a subscription at $28.80 (10% off a one-time $32 price), and another advertised Save 33% on a 4-month supply. [L1167.012]
  • A demo price of $35 was chosen inside the $32 to $48 range. The demo was only an example: the final price should come from your own margin calculation, not from the competitor range alone. [L1167.013]
  • A single-book example: the standard price in the Buy 1 tier is $41.95, and the lowest acceptable price for that tier is $39.99. [L1149.003]

Positioning against competitors

  • Set the base price close to competitors' prices. If the profit margin is at least 70%, you can price slightly below them to undercut. If margins are strong but tighter, price slightly above them to protect profitability. [L0860.008]
  • Price a product at roughly the same level as direct competitors, using the net margin after ad spend and COGS as the deciding factor. If that margin is at least 70%, price slightly below competitors to undercut them. If the margin is strong but tighter, price slightly above them to protect profitability. [L1149.001]
  • The lesson gives no numeric boundary between "at least 70%" and "strong but tighter". Below 70%, only the direction is given (price slightly higher to protect profitability). Set the exact adjustment from your own margin calculation. [L1149.014]
  • Matching a competitor's price, especially when they offer free shipping, without first factoring in ad spend and COGS skips the profit check. Price decisions need the full cost picture. [L0860.010]
  • Premium positioning of 50% to 100% above competitors is possible with differentiation. [L0227.025]
  • Check the price after rounding: the final price must still fall within the target margin range (see Psychological price endings). [L1182.011]

Avoiding price wars

  • Do not start a price war with competitors, because it rarely works in this kind of business. Check competitor pricing for similar products before setting your own price. [L1215.004]
  • Do not undercut the market. Undercutting starts a price race to the bottom. [L1146.008]
  • Matching a competitor's price can leave only about 5% to 7% profit. That is a red flag for paid traffic and a reason to negotiate supplier pricing before advertising. [L0966.047]

Psychological price endings

  • Psychological pricing means ending prices just below a round number, such as.99 or.95, which buyers perceive as lower. This makes the same product look more attractive than a whole-dollar price. [L0436.015]
  • The 3x rounding step uses a.99 ending: round the result down, so $30 becomes $29.99. [L0436.027]
  • Final prices are rounded to a.95 ending, such as $45.95 or $64.95. After rounding, check that the margin still falls within the 67% to 70% range. [L1182.011]
  • Both endings are in use. The Source C rule uses.99 and the Source D rule uses.95. The sources do not say which one to pick for a given product, so test per market. [L0436.027, L1182.011, L1316.004]
  • Price-ending tests matter: a price like 49.90 instead of 50 in the local currency can lift conversion. Test this per market rather than assuming one rounding rule works everywhere. [L1316.004]
  • In a new market such as the UK, do not use an automatic currency conversion price. Use a price ending like 24.90 or 26.90 pounds instead of 27 pounds, which the mentor described as unattractive. [L1161.031]

Anchoring

Reference points before the price

  • Anchoring: set a reference point before the reader judges a price. For example, compare the product with hours of cleaning each month or with the much higher cost of an alternative, so the real price looks small. Stacking bonuses with individual values and a total value, then showing a much lower price, uses the same effect. [L0516.029]
  • Example copy: compared to the hours spent scrubbing each month, this brush is a small one-time cost. [L0516.029]

Price anchors for thin margins

  • When profit margins are tight and large discounts would erode profitability, create a price anchor: duplicate the product, raise its listed price by 15% to 30%, then discount from that higher price. Customers cannot easily calculate the true savings because they lack a reference point for the actual product cost, which creates perceived value. [L0291.005]
  • Example: original product cost $100. Duplicate the product and raise the price 20% to $120. Offer 30% off this higher price: $120 × 0.70 = $84 final price. The customer perceives $36 in savings, but the actual discount from original cost is only $16 (16%), which preserves margin. [L0291.006]
  • Apply the price anchor strategy only when margins are low. With strong margins, offer straight discounts without the duplicate-and-inflate technique. [L0291.007]

Buy-one-get-two-free anchor

  • Set a deliberately high price for one unit, then offer buy one get two free. Buyers compare the offer with the inflated single price, feel they receive the extra units free, and can picture giving them to relatives or friends. [L1219.010]
  • Example 1: a $25 phone stand shown at $75 with buy one get two free. Example 2: an $18 pet brush shown at $54 under the same offer. [L1219.010]
  • Raise the product price, then offer buy-one-get-one-free on top, so the offer still looks large while the margin stays intact. [L1436.046]

Compare-at and strikethrough pricing

Shopify fields

  • In Shopify, the Price and Compare at price fields use the store's currency, shown as a prefix in the field (for example A$ in an Australian store). Enter all prices in that currency. The compare-at price must be higher than the price to show a sale. (older, 2021-22) [L0452.031]
  • Enter the selling price in the Price field and keep "Charge tax on this product" ticked by default. Cost per item is visible only to you and is used for the margin and profit display. (older, 2021-22) [L1166.014]

Core rules

  • Do not use a compare-at price as the sale price. The actual selling price must match the product feed. Compare-at is only an additional display. (older, 2020) [L0468.103]
  • Do not show every product as a discount. Use a strikethrough price only when the original price is legitimately higher. This maintains credibility. (older, 2022) [L0249.040]
  • Only display a compare-at price that the product has really sold at. An inflated reference price is a deceptive pricing claim. Competitor pages show a sale price next to a struck-through original and a percentage saving, for example 59.99 against 119.99 with 50% saved. [L0600.038]
  • Display a sale price next to a higher compare-at price with a percentage savings label, for example $19.99 next to a struck-through $39.99 with a SAVE 50% badge. This makes the saving visible on the product page. [L0347.019]
  • Show a higher regular price crossed out next to a lower sale price, so the discount is visible at a glance. The higher reference price makes the sale price look like a real deal. [L1325.018]
  • Examples in the same pattern: a regular price of $30 crossed out next to a sale price of $19.99, and a regular price of $12 crossed out next to a sale price of $7.49. The lesson's example was a regular price of $4.10 reduced to $1.49. [L1325.018]

MSRP and MAP as strikethrough and sale price

  • List the MSRP as the strikethrough price and sell the product at MAP. Example: wholesale $500, MAP $1,000 and MSRP $1,250 gives $1,250 struck through and a sale at $1,000. Competitor sites can be checked for the strikethrough price, and it is the MSRP about 95% of the time. [L0941.050]
  • If a supplier does not send an MSRP, create an everyday price to use as the strikethrough price. A missing MSRP happens roughly 10% to 20% of the time and is not unusual. [L0941.051]
  • Show MAP as the main price on the product page and MSRP as the crossed-out compare-at price, which matches typical retail presentation. (older, 2021) [L0968.079]
  • List products on the store at MAP, the minimum advertised price set by the supplier. Use MSRP as the compare-at price with a strikethrough so the discount is visible. (2024) [L1115.065]
  • When a supplier also publishes an MSRP, set the MAP as the sale price and the MSRP as the compare-at price shown with a strikethrough. Example: MAP 1000 and MSRP 1200 gives a 1200 compare-at price. (2023) [L1094.076]
  • When MSRP equals MAP, either set the compare-at price about 20% above MAP or leave it blank. Match the approach used by the top competitors. (older, 2020) [L1111.088]

Volume discounts

  • For a 50% off volume discount, set the compare-at price to about double the sale price. Example: compare-at $79.99 and sale price $39.99. [L1345.016, L1260.019]
  • For volume discounts: set the compare-at price to about double the sale price (for example, compare-at 79.99 and sale price 39.99), show the sale price in a larger font, add 50% off, and optionally a time-limited or event-based scarcity message. Use a theme or page builder such as PageFly or GemPages to highlight the compare-at price. [L1260.019, L1345.016]

Multi-offer display

  • Example structure of a Posture Pro Corrector offer: regular price $49.99, a 20% off offer (implied price $39.99), a risk-free trial also at $39.99, and a free gift (Posture Fix Guide) shown as a $19.99 value. [L0313.019]

Multi-currency and multi-market pricing

  • Each extra market needs its own prices in its currency. With many markets, prices must be converted by hand, which is easy to forget and can leave products underpriced. Reducing to a few free markets was seen as simpler, while showing local currency at checkout is worth doing for the UK and similar markets. [L0694.026]
  • A store serving a specific country should display prices in that country's currency, such as CHF for Switzerland, rather than USD. Local currency pricing makes the offer easier to understand and fits local payment methods. [L0492.068]
  • Convert price ranges to local currency and keep lower-priced niches around $200 from dropping too low, such as to 150 pounds in the UK. Higher-priced niches can be less strict. (older, 2021) [L0469.075]
  • Set prices per country based on local landed cost and shipping cost, not one global price. Example: dropshipping cost was $16 for Australia and $19 for the UK. The UK price was set higher (54.90 AUD) to protect margin, while Australia stayed lower because its shipping was cheaper. [L1316.005]
  • Test price endings per market (see Psychological price endings). [L1316.004]
  • In new markets, avoid automatic conversion prices (see Psychological price endings). [L1161.031]
  • The 2x wholesale minimum applies in any currency (see Markup rules). [L1146.017]

Further details

  • Before you fix the final price, factor in ad spend and COGS (cost of goods sold). Do not decide the price from the product cost alone. [L0860.009]

2.5 Price point, margin and shipping economics

Minimum Price: The $200 Floor

  • Products in a store should average at least $200, and this minimum has been used since the program began, so do not sell items priced below it (2025). [L0667.070]
  • High-ticket products start at $200 or higher (2025). [L0278.001]
  • Focus only on categories where most products are priced at $200 or more, so each sale leaves enough profit for a sustainable model. [L0012.001]
  • Only consider products priced above $200, ideally higher, because lower-priced items usually leave too little profit to cover ads and fees (2020). [L0961.089]
  • The minimum product price to sell is about $200. Within a niche, carve out a higher tier rather than selling cheap versions, for example studio lighting sets at $400 or more instead of $100 sets (2021). [L0801.010]
  • Do not list products under $200 unless they are add-ons or accessories, because low-priced products rarely produce enough margin to justify ad spend (2025). [L0837.015]
  • Sort a supplier's catalog by price and list only products above the set threshold. Products under $200 are usually not worth listing because their margins are too low (2024). [L0965.021]
  • Core products should cost at least $200, and ideally more. The lesson's stores average above $1,000. Products below $200 can still be sold, but they should not be the products that paid traffic is bought for. [L1147.009]
  • Cheap, very common products such as phone cases look easy to sell but face huge competition and many variations, so they usually earn little per order. Easy-looking demand is not a reason to choose a product. [L1147.011]
  • Inexpensive items such as decorative pillows, ornaments and regular pillows are almost never a good niche for dropshipping. [L0766.019]
  • Avoid the cheapest commodity segment of a niche (example: basic ceiling fans at $20 to $60) and focus on the premium segment (example: $300 and up). The premium segment has large demand and enough margin, and cheap items are not worth the time (2018). [L1085.025]
  • Minimum price for a niche product is about $200, with no maximum. Higher-priced products are acceptable if supplier quality and pricing are right (2018). [L1109.012]
  • Minimum price used as a filter is $200 or more. This floor must be combined with the target demographic and not used on its own. [L1518.012]
  • A niche passes the price point test only when its average selling price is above $200. Niches below $200 move to the bottom of the research list. [L1150.001]
  • The average price point of a niche's products should be at least $200. A niche with a lower average fails the price check even when demand and seasonality pass. [L0451.009, L0451.013]
  • Half or more of the first-page search results for a product should be priced above $200. [L1092.030] (older: 2022)
  • If all candidate products in a list are under $100, look for higher-priced products within the niche, or niche up to a broader category that contains a higher price range (2019). [L1110.020]
  • Products with an average retail price under about $200 are not recommended, even with search volume above 200,000, because the margin cannot cover shipping, fees, fixed costs and ad spend (2020). The lesson framed this as a room-for-profit issue, not a niche problem. [L0358.020]
  • For small, light products shipped with standard parcel carriers such as UPS or FedEx, a selling price of at least about $200 is the minimum threshold. [L1179.010]

Conflicting thresholds for niche ideas (when each applies):

  • For generating niche ideas, the average sale price must be $500 or higher to qualify as viable for dropshipping. [L0024.002]
  • For a niche to count as high-ticket, target an average product price of $500 or more. This attracts affluent buyers with more purchasing power and creates viable margins. [L0159.005]
  • The $200 floor remains the price check applied to a listed niche. [L0451.009, L0451.013, L1150.001]

Average Price: What to Measure and How

  • Calculate average prices from new products only, excluding used items. Leave out shipping costs when shipping is not included in the listed price. [L1279.009]
  • Ignore auctions and auction-based prices. Include only products at fixed prices that are new and currently for sale; eBay fixed-price new sales count. (2024) [L0276.069]
  • Use the products on the first page of Google Shopping results for the niche term, since these are the most popular products and their prices give a usable average. [L0941.009]
  • Use the first page of Google Shopping results. A few cheap, lower-quality competitors are acceptable because you sell the better, higher-priced products (2021). [L0469.012]
  • Look at paid results first and weigh them more heavily than organic results, though organic prices also help. [L0671.029, L0671.168] (older: 2021, identical text)
  • Organic first-page results should show an average of at least $200. There is no maximum price, but very high average order values bring more phone orders (2024). [L0806.002]
  • Do not filter Google Shopping results by a price floor such as $200 when estimating a niche's average price. Default results show the most popular products, and a price filter distorts demand research (2021). [L0853.020]
  • Find average prices from the default search results, not from price filters, because filters change which products appear as the most popular ones (2021). [L1112.066]
  • Do not apply a price filter such as "over 500" when researching on Google Shopping. It changes which products show as most popular. Keep the unfiltered first page for research (2021). [L1279.010]
  • When one product type spans a huge price range (example: sofas from $200 to $20,000), do not mix price segments. Change the search term (example: leather sofa or three-seater sofa) instead of using the sidebar price filter (2024). [L0806.006]
  • When a niche has many products with very wide price ranges, add a qualifying keyword to the search to narrow results to the desired range rather than relying on sidebar price filters (2023). [L0855.092]
  • Price variation inside a niche is normal. Sofas may range from about $100 to $20,000, so keep the selected items in the range where the brand and product mix is strongest (2020). [L0967.006]
  • When a category has two price tiers, such as standard $1,500 sofas and $30,000 custom Italian leather sofas, pick one tier for the store. Choose the lower tier for simpler customer service, or the higher tier if you accept more service work (2021). [L0469.019]
  • Estimate prices from several retailer sites and categories, not from a single product page. A rough estimate that reflects the range you see is enough for the decision. [L1329.015]
  • Judge a niche by the typical average price across several retailers and categories, not by a single product. Individual products can sit above or below the target. [L1329.021]
  • To find retailers for price research, use the sponsored ads and scroll down the main Google results for more store sites. One website does not show the full price range of a niche. [L1329.022]
  • Keep a few high-priced outlier competitors in the average if their products still fit the niche. Deluxe versions that cost much more do not disqualify a niche (2023). [L0970.020]
  • Include outliers only when they are relevant products you would list. If an outlier is a pricing error, exclude it. [L1283.066]
  • Exclude price outliers that are obvious data errors, such as a $23,746 dresser caused by a misplaced decimal or a multiplication bug. Real products with genuine prices stay in the average (2021). [L0834.005]
  • A supplier's shipping cost from offshore sources can distort average price. Fix this by refining the keyword, not by adding shipping to the average. [L1283.067]
  • Products priced well above the calculated range can still be sold in the store, and suppliers offering higher-priced items raise profit margin. The average price remains the main pass criterion for a niche. [L1150.006]
  • Average prices are estimated from what you see. Exact calculation is not needed at this stage. [L1329.009]
  • Price research for a broad niche: 1) search the main keyword and look at the most popular products on the first page of Google results; 2) if you plan to sell only a subtype, price research that subtype; 3) if you plan to sell all types, average the prices across them (2021). [L0968.088]

Procedure: Average price from Google Shopping (Source A, 2020, older)

  1. Search Google for the main keyword a potential customer would use for the product. [L0358.018]
  2. Look at the first page of the Google Shopping results. [L0358.018]
  3. Calculate the average price across those results. This average approximates what most buyers are willing to spend, because top results reflect the most popular products. [L0358.018]

Procedure: Source B's Shopping-tab check (2021-22, older)

  1. Open Google, search the product type, and click the Shopping tab. [L0458.017]
  2. Read the listed prices and scroll through the results pages. [L0458.017]
  3. Use the price filter with the ranges Up to $500, $500 to $1,000, $1,000 to $3,000 and Over $3,000 to see how many listings sit in the high-ticket range. [L0458.017]
  4. Calculate the average price of the listings and check it is at least $800. The lesson's generator example averaged about $1,000 or more. [L0458.017]
  5. Mark the price criterion as yes only if the high-ticket range is well covered. [L0458.017]

Procedure: Estimating average price with Google Shopping (Source B, 2021-22, older)

  1. Search the broad market idea or a specific product type in Google. [L1329.009]
  2. Make sure results come from your target selling country. If you live elsewhere, use a VPN and set the location to that country. [L1329.009]
  3. Review the Google Shopping ads (product image, title, price), which appear at the top or side of results, and open the Shopping tab for a dedicated page. [L1329.009]
  4. Use the Shopping filters to narrow prices to your target range. [L1329.009]
  5. Note the products that fall within your target price range, and note the product types you see (for example drawers or turbos) for later research. [L1329.009]
  6. Open retailer websites from the ads and shopping results, browse their categories, and note products above and below your target range. [L1329.009]
  7. Skip low-ticket categories that do not fit your price target. [L1329.009]
  8. Estimate one average price per idea from the prices you saw. Exact calculation is not needed. [L1329.009]
  9. Repeat for each of the five ideas. This takes about 10 to 20 minutes for all five. [L1329.009]

Procedure: Price test for a high-ticket product (Source A, 2022, older)

  1. Search the product name on Google Shopping. [L1281.021]
  2. Check the price spread of the results. [L1281.021]
  3. Pass the product only if at least half of the results are priced at $200 or more. Products with mostly $20 to $50 results fail, even if they sell. [L1281.021]

Price filter brackets (conflicting sources, when each applies)

  • Source B's Shopping-tab check uses the brackets Up to $500, $500 to $1,000, $1,000 to $3,000 and Over $3,000 to check the high-ticket range. [L0458.017]
  • Source B's general price check uses the Shopping preset brackets Up to $100, $100 to $250, $250 to $600 and Over $600 to see whether results reach the target range. [L1329.011, L1329.019]

Tool-based price analysis (Source A, no year)

  • Before automated research tools, average prices were gathered manually: search the niche keyword on Google, open the Shopping tab and read the listed prices. [L1150.002]
  • The research tool's average uses the interquartile range, a statistical method that removes outliers such as very expensive items and accessories in keyword searches. The displayed range is therefore narrower than the full set of prices found. [L1150.003]
  • Example: chandeliers had an average of $305.49 from 33 products, with a range of $44.48 to $999 after outliers were removed. Raw listings such as $1,800, $2,730, $4,400 and $4,999 were excluded. [L1150.004]
  • Judging a niche by individual high or low listings instead of the filtered average gives a misleading picture. Accessories and extreme outliers distort the impression of what a niche sells for. [L1150.005]
  • The price panel at the bottom of the research tool's results shows three figures: the average price, the number of products the average is based on, and the price range after outliers are removed. For example, chandeliers showed an average of $305.49 from 33 products, with a range of $44.48 to $999. [L1150.015]
  • The price analysis in the research tool checks a niche's average price against the price-point criteria taught in the Blueprint testing module. [L1075.007]
  • AI tools such as ChatGPT often give pricing data that does not match live Google Shopping prices. Verify a few products manually before using AI for pricing, and drop a niche that fails at the rough filter stage (2024). [L0471.053]
  • To estimate a product's selling price, check what similar items sell for on eBay and in Google Shopping results. Use those observed prices as the planning sale price before running margin calculations, for example a cheap phone case at about $10 and a paddleboard at about $1,000. [L1147.025]
  • Judge a Shopping price against local retail as a ceiling check. Example: a vacuum canister sourced from AliExpress was priced around 37 pounds (about 45 dollars) in UK retail, compared with 20 to 40 dollars on Amazon. [L1186.043]

Shipping: Size, Weight and COGS

  • Profitability is determined by the relationship between COGS and shipping costs, not by product size or weight. [L0015.001]
  • Disqualifying products during niche research based only on size or weight, without analyzing COGS and shipping, is a mistake. [L0015.002]
  • Size and weight alone do not disqualify a niche. The decisive question is whether the sale price leaves enough net profit after COGS and shipping. Disqualify a product when the margin left over is too small. [L1180.009]
  • Large and heavy products should not be ruled out automatically. Viability depends on the arithmetic of selling price, COGS and shipping, plus common sense about whether the seller can afford the shipping. [L1180.001]
  • Most products in a category cost roughly the same to ship, so a fixed shipping burden hits low-priced items much harder than high-priced ones. The selling price decides whether a niche can absorb shipping. [L1179.008]
  • For large and heavy products, shipping costs remain similar across price points. A $500 item and a $1,500 item may cost nearly the same to ship, making the higher-priced version far more profitable. [L0016.004]
  • A low-priced bulky or heavy product can lose its entire gross margin to shipping, leaving zero net profit. Avoid such niches even if the product sells well. [L1179.009]
  • Avoid niches dominated by inexpensive, large or heavy products, because shipping eliminates profit margins and leaves minimal or zero net earnings. [L0016.008]
  • Heavy or bulky products are workable only when the selling price is above $1,000. Below that level, shipping costs on large, heavy items eat the profit (2019). [L0663.002]
  • Do not sell large, heavy, inexpensive items. Shipping makes them unprofitable unless the retail price leaves a large spread over wholesale (2022). [L0969.022]
  • Heavy or bulky products need a higher price point than standard items, because freight costs require a wider spread between wholesale and MAP to cover free shipping (2022). [L1092.031]
  • Heavy or bulky items are better when their selling price is high enough to absorb shipping. Higher-priced items carry heavy freight better, and free shipping is possible only if the price covers the shipping cost. [L1285.014]
  • The shipping problem comes mainly from products whose price leaves too little room between MAP and wholesale, not from size or weight alone. Large or heavy products can work if the price is high enough to cover shipping and ads. [L0359.013]
  • Product options (variants) and product weight are generally not reasons to drop a niche. Heavy products are a problem only when prices are too low to cover shipping. [L0551.011]
  • Products that are difficult to ship, such as large or heavy items, should not automatically be rejected. Shipping is harder than parcel shipping but can be learned quickly, while high customer service load is a stronger reason for caution (2020). [L0967.007]
  • Heavy and bulky items have fewer sellers because many avoid freight shipping, so competition is often lower. Their shipping costs must be built into margin math before choosing them (2020). [L1111.013]
  • Before listing a heavy B2B product, compare the item price with the wholesale cost and the shipping cost to check whether it leaves a healthy profit after free shipping (2021). [L0671.285]
  • Before choosing a heavy or bulky product, calculate the shipping cost. A single item can cost hundreds of dollars to ship across the country. Avoid products whose shipping cost would be more than half of a low selling price (2020). [L0551.009]
  • Shipping of around $2,500 on a $20,000 order is a sign that a supplier should not be listed if the customer saves that amount by ordering direct (2023). [L0964.016]
  • Keep only the products that stay at least as profitable after shipping (2023). [L0964.016]
  • Check the shipping feasibility of a product before testing. Liquids or large packages can be a problem, and shipping cost can kill the margin. [L1176.011]
  • Bulky, heavy or installation-dependent items such as sinks, cabinets, lifts, saunas, pools, hot tubs, sheds and tiny homes raise shipping, handling and return costs. Weigh these costs before picking them. [L0546.005]
  • Heavy commercial and industrial items such as Commercial Ranges and Ovens, Walk-in Refrigerators and Freezers, Forklifts, Car Lifts and Industrial Sewing Machines are often too heavy for standard parcel shipping. Plan freight or pallet delivery and a quote-based sale for them rather than a general checkout. [L0546.022]
  • Bulky seasonal products such as barbecue grills can be considered for dropshipping if shipping costs are manageable. Timing the launch with the season, such as barbecue season, is a sensible way to leverage demand. [L0660.011]
  • Products should weigh no more than 2 kg. Products of about 3 kg or more are very hard to ship and should be avoided (Source C). [L0708.010]
  • Lightweight products with high perceived value, such as jewelry, keep shipping cost low relative to the selling price. Their low unit weight and room for custom boxes or private branding make them easier to scale. [L0780.038]
  • If a bulky or heavy product fails at a low price point, check whether a higher-quality supplier version of the same type sells at a price high enough to absorb the shipping. Moving up the quality tier can turn an unprofitable item into a profitable one without changing the category. [L1180.015]
  • Large and heavy products are harder to manage but not impossible to sell. Household furniture such as a table is acceptable, while items at the scale of a car are too big and too heavy (2021-22, older). [L0994.004, L0994.005]
  • Shipping is a yes-or-no check based on common sense: are the products very large or very heavy? Detailed logistics are not needed at this stage. (2021-22, older) [L1329.016]
  • Shipping limits to check (2021-22, older): in Australia, products over 100 kg are difficult to ship and may fail the shipping check. In the US, products weighing hundreds of pounds may be a problem. Products too large to fit in a small room of a home are too big to ship. [L1329.017]
  • Examples of shippable high-ticket products (2021-22, older): canopies for pickup trucks at about $3,000 are not small but are not very large or heavy, so they pass. Airbag suspension kits at about $825 to over $1,000 come in boxes that can be sent by post, so they clearly pass. [L1329.018]
  • Products must be small enough to ship comfortably. Very large or heavy items, such as big machines shipped from overseas, count against a niche in the shipping check (2021-22, older). [L0490.011]
  • Avoid products that are too big or too heavy, because shipping them becomes difficult and costly for you and your supplier (2021-22, older). [L0994.004]
  • Big or heavy products are only worth considering if their retail price is $3,000 or more. Below that, the shipping burden usually outweighs the margin (2021-22, older). [L0994.006]
  • Sources differ on the threshold for bulky or heavy products: one mention says 300, while the summary checklist sets 3,000. Use 3,000 as the working threshold and treat anything below as a likely shipping problem (2021-22, older). [L0994.030]
  • Check product size, weight and shipping cost against expected profit before going further with a niche. Shipping can make an attractive product unprofitable (2021). [L0247.006]
  • Bulky and heavy items may be a niche to consider, but the seller should judge whether a sale is worth the freight. [L0551.009]
  • Shipping problems come mainly from prices, and high-ticket items absorb freight. [L0359.013]

Heavy items and expensive versions within a niche

  • Large and heavy products with a low sale price are generally not profitable with free shipping. If shipping eats the margin, sell only the more expensive products within the niche, for example fabric sofas instead of futons (2022, older). [L0665.006]
  • Within one category, subtypes can differ widely in price. A futon is a low-priced sofa, while a three-seater fabric sofa averages about $1,500. A generic rug can be a cheap entryway mat, while Persian rugs sit well above $500. Narrow the niche to the subtype whose price can absorb shipping. [L1179.021]
  • Avoid niches where all products are large and heavy and the selling price is around $600 or less, because there is typically no room for profit. A niche confirmed above this level can be pursued. [L1180.012]
  • A product from a high-end segment, such as a $3,500 desk where a profit of $1,750 covers shipping, can be profitable where a cheaper heavy item cannot (2022). [L0969.021]

Margin Targets and Gross Margin Rules

  • Target a net profit margin of approximately 30% on each sale. Some niches allow 25%, while others sustain higher margins, but 30% is the benchmark. [L0016.002]
  • COGS from suppliers averages approximately 50% of the retail sale price. [L0016.001]
  • A niche with a few brands ranking for the main keyword is viable if margins meet a 20% threshold and suppliers allow you to sell it (2020, older). [L0664.046]
  • For a niche with 20 to 25% margins including shipping and an average product price around $1,500 to $2,000, first try to move to better pricing tiers with suppliers, and grow free organic traffic (product feed optimization, SEO) so ad costs do not eat the margin (2024). [L0805.038]
  • Focus on gross margin percentage rather than margin dollars. High-ticket products still need enough percentage to pay for the more hands-on and expensive sales process (2019, older). [L1068.021]
  • Example (2019, older): a $4,000 sale at a 20% margin gives $800 per sale, which is strong because more margin can absorb ad and discount costs. A $5,000 item with only $250 margin is not worth selling. [L1068.022]
  • Example (2019, older): brands showing a 20 to 25% margin before ads and shipping can fall to 10 to 15% once a $100 shipping cost is included. A margin that thin is too small for paid traffic. [L1068.023]
  • In furniture (2019, older), a $200 futon has so much shipping cost that little margin is left, while a $5,000 leather sofa leaves room for margin and shipping. Avoid low-priced bulky items where shipping absorbs the profit. [L1068.020]
  • Procedure for low-margin brands in a niche (2019, older):
    1. Run Google Shopping product listing ads only for brands with healthy margins. [L1068.024]
    2. Upload low-margin products to the site but exclude them from Google Ads campaigns. [L1068.024]
    3. Let Facebook remarketing follow visitors who viewed those product pages. [L1068.024]
    4. Keep these brands for brand recognition, a larger catalog and organic sales, and do not buy cold traffic for them. [L1068.024]
  • Only pursue products that can reach a gross profit margin of at least 3x the purchase price, with a minimum profit of $20 per product (Source C). [L0895.002]
  • Technology gadgets can be sold at 3x to 6x their cost, and categories with high perceived value such as beauty, health, wellness and tech gadgets make it easier to set a high price (Source C). [L0638.024]
  • A product with a sourcing cost around $40 and a selling price around $70 to $80 was judged a mid-range ticket item with a workable $30 gross margin. A price point near $100 was seen as a downside (Source C). [L0530.007]
  • A low sourcing cost is not enough on its own. The selling price should sit where perceived value clearly exceeds the price, because an item that looks expensive for its value can kill the sale (Source C). [L0530.008]
  • Price the product so that it still earns money after paying to acquire a customer. If the margin does not cover paid acquisition, every sale loses money. [L0790.010]
  • Price tier decides how much profit each sale brings. A product priced around $300 with $60 to $70 net profit can work, and higher-ticket items add profit faster but may bring more service work (2021). [L0801.009]
  • Higher-priced products in the $300 to $600 range can generate far more profit per sale than cheap products, so a store does not need $1,000 or more products to make Google Shopping work. Every ad dollar should go toward products that return the target ROAS when they sell (2020 and no-year). [L0748.007]
  • Products selling well in this model typically retail for $20 to $40 and cost $10 to $20 through CJ dropshipping (Source C). [L0562.016]
  • Check a product's real profitability by comparing competitor prices and margins. A product with high margins should be matched to the minimum price, not undercut (2021). [L0962.092]
  • When two products have the same shipping cost, choose the more expensive one to protect the margin. Shipping is a fixed cost per item size, so higher prices keep the margin higher (2021). [L0962.088]
  • Higher-priced products are where the real money is in this model, even though cheap products feel less risky. The perceived risk of large orders is usually greater than the real risk, and fraud protection tools cover most of it (2020). [L0551.008]
  • Higher-priced products are a reasonable starting point when all other research criteria are equal, because expensive items give more revenue per order. [L0941.012]
  • Higher-priced products are generally easier to make profitable than cheap ones, unless the product is a luxury good with different buying behavior (2018). [L1085.026]
  • A product with about a 20% wholesale-to-MAP margin is not workable for paid ads. True margins only become visible after suppliers are set up and approved (2023). [L0970.026]
  • Each niche is not a fixed margin. Margins depend on each supplier and product. After approval, compare each brand's wholesale price with MAP to see the real profit per product (2020). [L1277.015]
  • Within a niche with a wide price range, check whether low-priced items can produce a real margin. If they cannot, drop them, because a niche with very cheap items may not pay for the work (2020). [L1277.016]
  • Price high-ticket items above about $79 for a high-ticket store. One store sold items between $350 and $700 with about 250 units sold in one month (Source C). [L1440.051]
  • Products priced around $25 fall into an impulse-buy range, where many shoppers buy spontaneously. Price points in this range are easier to sell cold to new audiences (Source C). [L1251.003]
  • Products in a store should be evaluated by sourcing cost and ability to price. An AliExpress bottle opener costing about $5 with shipping is a very cheap product, which makes it attractive to test (Source C). [L0656.013]

MAP and Non-MAP Suppliers

  • The gap between wholesale and MAP differs per supplier and per product, and no tool reveals it in advance. The actual margin only becomes known during the approval phase, when price sheets arrive (2023). [L0804.021]
  • In a niche where one of 20 suppliers had MAP, margins were 7 to 15%. Without MAP, margins can vary widely, so compare prices before committing (2022). [L0963.105]
  • A margin seen from only a few supplier conversations is not evidence that every supplier in the niche offers the same terms. Contact more suppliers before judging a niche's margins (2025). [L0966.015]
  • For a niche with many brands but unfamiliar products, check that the wholesale price is well below the selling price before committing. Products selling direct at a lower price on the supplier's own site are a red flag (2020). [L0551.075]
  • Search the exact product name in Google Shopping as a shopper would. If you are the only seller shown, a higher price plus a discount code can work. If competitors list it cheaper, shrink the discount or remove it (2025). [L0966.016]
  • A niche with many suppliers and a high-priced outlier is best handled by including outliers only when they are relevant products you would list. If one is a pricing error, exclude it (2023). [L1283.066]
  • A supplier's shipping cost from offshore sources can distort average price. Refine the keyword rather than adding shipping to the average (2023). [L1283.067]

Procedure: Evaluating a non-MAP supplier (Source A, 2021, older)

  1. Get the supplier's price list. [L0671.158]
  2. Search Google Shopping for several of its products. [L0671.158]
  3. Check whether anyone already advertises those products and at what markup. [L0671.158]
  4. If competitors mark them up only 10% or 15%, do not list them. This is a no-go. [L0671.158]
  5. If there is little competition, become the main seller for those products. If competitors sell them at healthy margins, list them too. [L0671.158]

Step: Large non-MAP supplier catalogs (no year)

  • For a non-MAP supplier with a large price list (example: 100 SKUs), search each product name individually on Google Shopping and record the average competitor price for each one. Plan the time for this, because the research is done product by product. [L1051.015]

Phone-Sale and Customer-Service Thresholds

  • To avoid phone sales, focus on niches where the average product price is under $2,000. [L0050.003]
  • If the business cannot handle phone calls, keep niches at product prices of $2,000 or less. Higher-priced niches (for example $2,000 and up) are only worth testing if phone sales are part of the model. [L0459.002]
  • Target average order values of about $2,000 to $3,000 only if you are willing to handle phone calls and extra emails. If you want less customer service work, stay in the normal price range (2020 and no-year). [L0359.016]
  • A niche with high technical complexity or a price point around $2,000 or more requires extra pre-sale and post-sale customer service. Such a niche is viable if the business can offer that service at first and outsource it later (2024). [L0836.019]
  • Average order value in the $500 to $1,000 range is generally the sweet spot. It leaves enough margin per order and needs the least free selling and customer interaction. Higher averages (about $1,200 to $2,000) still work but bring more pre-sale calls and emails (2021). [L0834.011]
  • Average prices above the $200 to $800 range can still work, but they bring more pre-sale and post-sale questions. Plan support capacity before choosing high-ticket products (2021). [L1279.016]
  • A niche should show an average of at least $200 across organic first-page results. There is no maximum, but very high average order values bring more phone orders (2024). [L0806.002]
  • To reach $2,000 profit per sale, the average order value must be between $6,000 and $7,000 (2025). [L0278.002]
  • When targeting $2,000 profit per sale, eliminate all products under $6,000 during price research in Module 2 (2025). [L0278.003]
  • For upper-middle-class customers, target product prices between $1,500 and $3,000 (2025). [L0278.021]
  • Choosing a product only because it meets a minimum price, without checking shipping, can lead to a loss. Free shipping must still leave profit after actual shipping cost is subtracted. [L1180.010]
  • A $200 futon earns much less per sale than a $2,000 product, but its buyers often still need support and return handling. Weigh profit per order against the workload each order creates before choosing a price point. [L1517.018]
  • When choosing a first niche from several that pass testing, pick the one with the highest average order value, ideally at least $1,000 (2024). [L1115.018]
  • Premium appliances priced around $10,000 for some models can have very good margins, but the volume is low. Plan the operation for fewer, more valuable orders. [L1472.060]

Impulse, Cold Traffic and Buyer Price Points

  • Very expensive products are hard to sell to cold traffic, because buyers research longer before purchasing. Keep the product price within a range that impulse buyers accept (Source C). [L0361.018]
  • Products around $25 fall into an impulse-buy range, which makes them easier to sell cold to new audiences (Source C). [L1251.003]
  • Dropship products for an Instagram viral approach are priced between $9.99 and $49.99 (Source C). [L1367.002]
  • For shoes sold online, the best-selling price range is roughly $20 to $40. Above about $40, a shoe stops being an impulse purchase and buyers need more convincing (Source C). [L0661.009]
  • Expensive niche products that do a narrow job, with limited capacity, rarely convert on price alone. If buyers need several units to get value, offer a quantity discount or rethink the price; otherwise most will buy just one (Source C). [L1186.012]
  • Customers do not care about the retailer's margin. They choose by the outcome or benefit they expect from the product, so margin belongs in the decision of what to stock, not in the customer's decision to buy (2021-22, older). [L1456.018]
  • Products with high perceived value, such as beauty, health, wellness and tech gadgets, make it easier to set a high price (Source C). [L0638.024]
  • Lightweight products with high perceived value keep shipping cost low relative to price (Source C). [L0780.038]
  • Phone cases and other cheap, very common products look easy to sell but compete with huge demand, so they usually earn little per order. [L1147.011]
  • If a niche has much cheaper variants, do not advertise the cheap versions when they would pull buyers away from higher-priced items. List low-price items only when the margin is there, and never spend ad money on them because they erode profit (2020). [L1088.006]

Price Tiers Within a Niche

  • When choosing between price points in the same niche, higher-priced products are generally easier to make profitable, unless the product is a luxury good with different buying behavior (2018). [L1085.026]
  • Keep a niche's products in the price range where the brand and product mix is strongest rather than mixing segments (2020). [L0967.006]
  • Product tiers within a niche: choose the tier that fits your service capacity, as stated in the two-tier rule. [L0469.019]
  • Within a niche, carve out a price tier above the cheap versions, for example studio lighting sets at $400 or more instead of $100 sets (2021). [L0801.010]
  • Price variation inside a niche is normal and should be handled by choosing the tier with the strongest mix (2020). [L0967.006]
  • Adding products above a range can still work if supplier quality and pricing are right (2018). [L1109.012]
  • Products sold per square foot or per piece: to estimate the typical bundle size, look at competitor sites for bundle or multi-pack listings and use those sizes. No reliable estimation method was given beyond this. [L0359.020]
  • Products priced well above the calculated range can still be sold, and higher-priced suppliers raise margin (Source A). [L1150.006]
  • Products whose quality tier can absorb shipping are better than the same products at a lower tier. [L1180.015]

Volume and Realism

  • For a high-ticket niche product that sells around $1,000, a realistic monthly volume for a new store is about 10 to 50 units, with 20 as a plausible middle figure. A plan built on 1,000 units of such a product is unrealistic. [L1147.024]
  • A smaller audience paying high prices can produce the same profit with far fewer orders and far less traffic than a large audience buying cheap products. Large, heavy or expensive products look harder to sell but can be profitable (no year). [L1147.012]
  • Higher-priced products earn more revenue per order, so fewer orders are needed to reach the same profit (no year). [L0941.012]
  • Plan the operation for fewer, more valuable orders when selling premium products (no year). [L1472.060]

Decision Sequence for an Operator

Work through the tests in this order. Stop at the first failure.

  1. Price test. Average of new, fixed-price products on the unfiltered first page of Google Shopping is at least $200, and at least half of results are at $200 or more. Average around $700 to $800 is the working target under Source B's framework (older). [L0667.070, L0451.009, L0451.013, L1281.021, L0458.017, L0994.003, L1329.013]
  2. Shipping test. Check size and weight, then the COGS and shipping arithmetic. Standard parcels should sell at about $200 or more; large or heavy items should ideally sell above $1,000 and at $3,000 or more under the older working threshold. [L0015.001, L1179.010, L1179.011, L0994.006]
  3. Margin test. Net margin target of about 30%, or 25% in some niches. Gross margin at least 3x purchase price with a minimum of $20 per product (Source C). [L0016.002, L0895.002]
  4. Supplier test. Confirm wholesale-to-MAP spread after approval, or use the non-MAP procedure. Reject a 10 to 15% markup. [L0804.021, L0671.158]
  5. Service test. Stay under $2,000 for phone-free operation, or plan service capacity for higher averages. [L0050.003, L0836.019]
  6. Buyer test. Keep impulse-range prices for cold traffic, and be cautious above about $40 for shoes or about $79 for high-ticket items. [L0661.009, L1440.051]
  7. Volume test. Plan around about 20 units per month for a new store at about $1,000 per product. [L1147.024]
  8. Ranking. When several niches pass, choose the one with the highest average order value, ideally at least $1,000. [L1115.018]

Further details

  • When screening niches before exact cost and shipping data exist, rough intuition about price points is an acceptable first filter. Those estimates should later be replaced with measured COGS and shipping figures. [L1179.012]

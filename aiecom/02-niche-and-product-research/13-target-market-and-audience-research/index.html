2.13 Target market and audience research

Market Definition: Start With the Customer

  • Define the market by the customer, their interests and their needs, not by the product list. Choose products afterwards to serve that customer, because research that starts from products alone leads to weak markets. (Older advice, Source B 2021-22.) [L0771.012]
  • Start from the customer rather than the product. Define the ideal customer first (their problems, where they search, who they trust and what they spend), then select products and markets that serve them. [L0414.048]
  • Write down where the business and its products sit in the market. Competitor analysis, market research and a written outline of the business structure belong together, because they decide positioning and target customers. (Older advice, Source B 2021-22.) [L0761.003]
  • Search for a winning problem in a niche, not a winning product. Find small, specific niches with large addressable markets. [L0307.012]
  • The same product sold by many competitors can succeed if it is repositioned to solve a different problem or to target a different emotion. Example: a tea brand does not sell taste but benefits such as focus, energy or libido, using a white-label manufacturer. The product is the same as competitors', but the market positioning differs. [L0307.012]
  • Research a market deeply once, then reuse that knowledge for every product in the same niche. Repeat the full research phase only when you enter a new market. [L0378.013]
  • Define one dream customer as the persona for all product research, then ask which product would solve that persona's problem. Across several years of testing, women over 40 proved to be the most responsive audience for this approach. [L0492.006]
  • Russell Brunson's Dot Com Secrets is a practical starting point for defining a clear dream customer before product research. His companion books, Traffic Secrets and Expert Secrets, cover traffic and positioning. [L0492.064]

Niche Selection Filters

  • A market should pass the standard filter: a price point suited to high-ticket selling, reliable shipping, enough suppliers and sufficient traffic volume. Check competition as a separate step, because an idea can meet all four criteria and still be crowded. (Older advice, Source B 2021-22.) [L0905.001]
  • Prefer enthusiast markets, where buyers purchase out of passion, such as horse riding, RV solar kits or boating accessories. Avoid needs-driven products such as refrigerators, because those markets are harder to sell into and usually more competitive. (Older advice, Source B 2021-22.) [L0771.015]
  • Choose a niche where you already have knowledge or passion, not complete ignorance. This speeds up problem identification, audience understanding and solution positioning. Example: in fitness, identify real gym-consistency problems (New Year motivation, lack of home workout tools) and solve those. Avoid niches like video games if you have no knowledge, because you will not identify real customer problems. [L0307.026]
  • Gym example: after New Year, members are highly motivated but fail to keep up habits. They abandon memberships within weeks because of busy schedules, lost motivation and lack of accountability. Position products around "stay consistent" and "remove distractions" rather than "get fit." Solve the real problem (motivation maintenance, not only fitness results). Relevant solutions include home workout tools, accountability coaching, habit trackers and time-management courses. [L0307.033]
  • Choose a target market whose members are proud of and passionate about their hobby, skill, background or trait, and who are easy to identify on social platforms. Passion for the mission matters more than the product itself. [L0487.018]
  • Your target market should be proud of its hobbies, skills, backgrounds or traits; passionate about its interests; easily identifiable as a group; and specific enough to count as a niche. [L0237.006]
  • Avoid markets where people have no passion or feel uncomfortable talking about the topic publicly. They will not share or tag friends even if they buy. Health-related products can fail this test even when they sell well. [L0487.019]
  • Look for niches that support recurring purchases, seasonal packages and educational content. Check whether competitors are dull or uneducational. Many long-established stores are weak at education, which leaves room for a new brand to differentiate. [L0487.021]
  • Focus on a select group of people who share the same underlying problem rather than targeting everyone. A narrow group is easier to reach, and a service that solves its shared problem is easier to sell repeatedly. [L0701.035]
  • Enthusiasm must belong to your customers, not to you as the seller. You do not have to share their passion to sell to them well. (Older advice, Source B 2021-22.) [L0994.009]

Crowded Markets

  • Even in a crowded market, a product can succeed by finding a narrower marketing angle, because a broad audience dilutes the appeal of any single message. Look for a specific group within the market rather than assuming the category is closed. [L0997.001, L0998.001]
  • In a saturated market, narrowing the audience to a specific group can reveal marketing angles that a broad product ad cannot. The same product can stand out when positioned for one defined buyer group instead of everyone in the category. [L0997.001, L0998.001]
  • In a market that is not very competitive, a focused customer segment may not be needed at the start. In a crowded market full of general stores, pick one target customer and build the whole business around that person. (Older advice, Source B 2021-22.) [L0905.007]
  • Electric bikes are a crowded high-ticket category, with up to 30 high-ticket dropshipping stores selling similar bikes and brands. Possible buyer segments include commuters, surfers who ride to the beach, hunters, retirees and people who travel with RVs. (Older example, Source B 2021-22.) [L0905.003]
  • Market size matters for long-term brand planning once sales reach 20k to 30k. A market of 10 billion USD with a competitor at 100 million USD is a reference point for deciding whether to compete. [L0895.024]

Market Validation Procedure

(Older procedure, Source B 2021-22.)

  1. Run the broad market through the standard criteria: price point, shipping, number of suppliers and traffic volume. [L0905.013]
  2. Check whether customers exist for the products and whether the product fits them. [L0905.013]
  3. If the market meets the criteria but looks too competitive, narrow the focus to a subset of customers that no competitor speaks to directly. [L0905.013]
  4. If such a subset is found, proceed with the market even if the broad market looks crowded. [L0905.013]

Learning an Unfamiliar Niche

  • Learning a new niche is feasible in about a week. Spend that week on videos, reviews, beginner guides and websites selling the product. This gives enough knowledge to talk with suppliers and customers and to build a credible site. Deeper expert knowledge comes later through practice. (Older advice, Source A 2019.) [L0357.012]
  • You do not need existing expertise in your niche. Position yourself through direct manufacturer relationships that let you answer customer questions by consulting suppliers, and keep a frequently asked questions section on your site. [L0031.001]
  • To learn an unfamiliar niche quickly (older advice, Source A 2021):
    1. Set aside about one hour a day for one week. [L0247.015]
    2. Read introductory books or watch introductory videos on the niche. [L0247.015]
    3. Aim for a conversational understanding, not expert knowledge. [L0247.015]

Target Audience Research: Sources and Methods

  • Target audience research should be enough to know the problem, the customer, and their needs, pain points and motivations. It should not become a long research project. Thirty minutes of reading Amazon reviews for recurring trends is often enough to pick an angle and start testing. Over-researching can stall a launch. [L0552.005]
  • Quick method for a product:
    1. Read Amazon reviews of comparable products and note recurring trends. [L0552.006]
    2. Note the demographics, psychographics, needs and motivations of the buyers. [L0552.006]
    3. Use those findings to pick one angle to test. [L0552.006]
    4. Refine the audience later if time allows. [L0552.006]
  • Before any market research starts, ask for the brand website or key product information. Every later step depends on it, and without that input the research cannot be grounded. [L0613.001]
  • Audience research for a physical product, in order:
    1. Start with a plain Google search for the product category. Note which product types, settings and pet or user types come up. [L0450.001]
    2. Search forums for buyer questions and recommendations about the category (for example, "best pet hair remover") and read the replies. [L0450.001]
    3. Read Amazon reviews of the top competing products and of their rivals. [L0450.001]
    4. Look up data and statistics sites (Statista, Google Trends) for hard numbers such as ownership counts and popularity. [L0450.001]
    5. Check current news for the niche to find topics and events your audience is discussing. [L0450.001]
    6. Use AI research tools as a supplement, never as a replacement for forums and Amazon. ChatGPT is useful for analysis; AgentGPT can run automated web research toward a goal such as "Research three target audiences for XYZ niche." [L0450.001]
    7. Check competitor stores and their social accounts, the Meta Ad Library and Instagram to see how they market and who follows them. [L0450.001]
    8. Find the most-followed brands in the wider category and read their audience's comments. [L0450.001]
    9. Dig into the problem behind the purchase (for example, health or allergy effects) and build the persona from what you find. [L0450.001]
  • Statista and Google Trends provide hard data and statistics for a niche, such as breed popularity or ownership numbers. News sources reveal current events that can serve as conversation topics for the target audience. [L0450.002]
  • Competitor research (sites, social accounts, customer comments) is one of the most important ways to identify the target audience and find gaps. A product's target audience must be defined before writing ads or messaging. [L0497.024]
  • Check breed or type popularity with authoritative sources to see which types of owners form a large audience. For example, the American Kennel Club publishes a yearly list of the most popular dog breeds. [L0450.010]
  • The AKC's 2022 ranking of the most popular dog breeds placed French Bulldog, Labrador Retriever, Golden Retriever and German Shepherd in the top four. This figure is dated (2022). Use the current ranking to size dog-owner segments by breed before choosing an audience. [L0450.031]
  • Among breeds on a worst-shedding list, German Shepherd, Welsh Corgi, Chow Chow and Pyrenees are named as heavy shedders. For a pet hair product, owners of these breeds form segments that can be reached through breed interests in ads. [L0450.030]
  • A heavy-shedding breed segment can be targeted on Facebook through interests such as a particular breed. [L0450.010]
  • Gift buyers can make a product sell beyond its target audience. On TikTok, a product aimed at one group may be bought by relatives or partners. Include in the ad buyer persona those who purchase for the target customer. Comments that mention buying for a parent, partner or relative signal this. [L0819.012]

Hunting for Dream Customers Before Launch

  • Find out whether a niche responds before investing in a store, by publishing content and watching how people react. Engagement is a cheap signal that tells you whether the path is right before you build products. [L0487.023]
  • Find dream customers before the store launches, in this order:
    1. Identify the target market and who these people are. [L0487.022]
    2. Define the brand persona and brand voice. [L0487.022]
    3. Create content for the target market. [L0487.022]
    4. Go to the platforms where they are active and find them there. [L0487.022]

Demographics, Psychographics, Behaviour and Motivation

The Four Pillars

  • Target audience research has four pillars: demographics, psychographics, behaviour, and goals, needs and motivations. Goals, needs and motivations sit below the surface and reveal what the buyer is trying to fulfil or escape. [L0353.018]
  • Audience segmentation rests on four pillars. Goals, needs and motivations are the most important and should be prioritised over the others. [L0497.004]
  • Needs and motivations are the most important pillar because they explain why someone buys. Demographics, psychographics and behaviours are the other three pillars. [L0995.002]
  • Segmentation rests on four components, each describing the audience from a different level, from surface facts to deep drivers. [L0997.005]
  • Four components of audience segmentation:
Component What it describes Examples Depth
Demographics Static traits of a person Age, gender, income level, education, marital status Surface level, define last
Psychographics Personality, values, beliefs, attitudes Openness, conscientiousness, organization, tendency to worry, need for control or belonging, religious views, opinions about the world Middle depth
Behavior What people do and in which context Browsing a product, using a product, the trigger that causes the need, where the problem fits into their day Observable
Goals, needs and motivations The underlying reason behind the behavior Status, freedom, security, belonging, fear of falling behind Deepest, most persuasive

[L0998.009]

  • A complete audience profile across all four pillars is not required before starting. Build the picture step by step and fill the gaps as far as possible, weighting motivations and goals above demographics. [L0497.027]
  • Segment the audience by demographics (who they are, such as dog or cat owner, age), psychographics (what they value and fear), behaviour (what they do and which solutions they already use), and needs and motivations (what they desperately want, what they fear, their biggest problem and their trigger). Keep the research simple and fill in what you can. [L0450.006]

Demographics

  • Demographics are static statistical facts about a person, including age, gender, income, geolocation, occupation and education level. [L0497.005]
  • Demographics are static attributes such as age, gender, income level, education and marital status. They are surface-level and should usually be the last layer you use when defining a target audience. [L0997.006]
  • Demographics describe who someone is, not their personality. They are useful as a filter but are the weakest basis for a message. [L0998.010]
  • Demographic data alone is a weak targeting tool. The further you move from underlying motivations toward demographics, the less important targeting becomes, although demographics still help describe a segment. [L0497.009]
  • Demographics alone tell you who a buyer is, but not what they do or why. Combining demographics, behaviour and motivation gives a clearer picture, which makes marketing messages stronger. [L0644.035]
  • Demographics can decide whether a product fits at all, for example when a product only makes sense for a certain life stage. Users outside that range are excluded, even if their other traits match. [L0997.007]
  • Example of a demographic filter deciding fit: the average age at which menstruation ends is over 50, so for a menstruation-related product, buyers above that age are generally excluded regardless of their other traits. Check life-stage fit at the demographic layer before spending time on psychological targeting. [L0997.029]
  • For a status-driven jewellery piece, buyers might span an age range of 20 to 50. Age matters much less than the underlying desire for status, so the demographic range is informative but rarely the core of the message. [L0997.008]
  • Age and other demographics matter less than the problem and goal. Refine them later if needed. [L0450.015]
  • Demographics are useful as a filter, not as a targeting engine. Check them after you have defined the problem and motivation. [L0998.010, L0497.009]
  • Demographics are the surface layer. Define them last, once the motivations and problem are clear. [L0997.006]

Psychographics

  • Psychographics describe who a person is: personality traits (for example openness, neuroticism, conscientiousness), values and beliefs (for example peace of mind, control, power, integrity), attitudes and opinions, lifestyle preferences, interests and hobbies, and emotional tendencies such as anger or feeling overwhelmed. [L0497.006]
  • Psychographics cover personality traits and how a person thinks: openness, conscientiousness, organisation, tendency to worry, core values such as control or belonging, beliefs about the world, religion and attitudes toward specific things. [L0997.009]
  • Psychographics include personality traits and attitudes toward buying, such as price consciousness or status. [L0995.002]
  • Psychographics show what the buyer values and fears, and they sit in the middle of the depth scale. [L0450.006, L0998.009]
  • Behaviours and psychographics are the surface layer you can observe, but they seldom reveal why someone acts. [L0497.010]

Behaviour

  • Behavioural segmentation asks what the person does at the moment they experience the problem, or when the product would fit into their day. Example: rushing to a subway while grabbing breakfast. [L0497.007]
  • Behaviours cover what people do and achieve, the context in which they do it, and the trigger that makes the problem appear. Define where the product fits into the person's routine or life journey. [L0997.010]
  • A vegetable slicer fits the context of someone preparing food in the kitchen. The trigger is cutting vegetables badly, which gives uneven, tough and frustrating results. [L0997.011]
  • Behavioural research checklist:
    1. What is the person doing when the problem occurs, or where does the product fit in their timeline? [L0497.008]
    2. Which event triggers the problem (for example, lack of sleep causing a late wake-up, or no time to cook breakfast)? [L0497.008]
    3. What is their attention like at that moment (a crowded or loud place, waiting in line, attention split across other things)? [L0497.008]
    4. Other behavioural traits: online browsing habits, purchase frequency, where they spend time on social media, and which media they use to communicate. [L0497.008]
  • Behaviours and triggers appear in the buyer's day. Record where and when the problem shows up and what sets it off. [L0450.006]
  • Observable behaviour is the tip of the iceberg. Demographics and behaviour are what you can observe. [L0997.013, L0997.015]

Needs, Goals and Motivations

  • Demographics and behaviours are what you can observe, while goals, needs and motivations are the underlying why behind what people do. Tapping into these needs is far more powerful than describing observable traits. [L0997.013]
  • Always focus on the underlying need or motivation behind a customer's goal, and whether the customer is moving toward a desired outcome or away from a problem. Positioning that addresses this drives more action than surface features. [L0450.023]
  • Goals, needs and motivations are the most important pillar, and they should be prioritised. [L0497.004]
  • Target audience research should start from the fears and pains of the buyer, not from product features. Each persona must be backed by a clear push and pull: what the buyer wants to move toward and what they want to move away from. [L0395.004]
  • Push and pull: split motivation into what the buyer moves toward and what they move away from. [L0395.001]
  • Target audience research should go well beyond age, gender and income. Define the audience's pains, passions, desires, daily search terms, magazines and topics. Use your own past experience with the problem as a guide to what they care about. [L0523.029]
  • The motivation behind the purchase often comes from a health or practical consequence. Example: pet dander causes itchy eyes and runny nose, and allergies disrupt sleep. Problems linked to health give motivation that can be used in marketing. [L0450.013]
  • Every buyer has a reason for what they do, even when the visible behaviour looks simple. You do not have to state deep motives openly in your ads, but you must understand your audience closely enough to speak to them at that level. [L0997.017]
  • Status and self-image often drive a choice more deeply than convenience. People often choose a coffee chain for its environment and for what being seen there signals about them, not because its coffee is the best. [L0997.014]
  • Every human need is a trade-off between opposing dimensions:
    • Social: power vs. belonging, conviviality vs. standing out and recognition. [L0497.013]
    • Personal: enjoyment and carefree fun vs. control and order, vitality and freedom vs. security and safety. [L0497.013]
    • Choosing one means giving up some of the other. [L0497.013]

Laddering

  • Behaviours and psychographics are surface layers. Ask "why" repeatedly (laddering) to uncover tensions, fears and underlying human drivers beneath an observed behaviour. [L0497.010]
  • Laddering example for a morning coffee and bagel purchase:
    1. Observed behaviour: buying coffee and a bagel on the way to work. [L0497.011]
    2. Reason: no time to sit down for breakfast. [L0497.011]
    3. Fear behind it: being late or missing something at work. [L0497.011]
    4. Deeper driver: feeling shame, losing control over one's destiny or significance. [L0497.011]
    5. Keep asking why until the deep human need appears. [L0497.011]
  • The iceberg model: what is visible at the tip is demographics and behaviour, and everything below the surface is the needs, tensions and motivations that explain it. Your job is to uncover the layer beneath the visible behaviour. [L0997.015]
  • Coffee and bagel example in iceberg terms: lacking time for breakfast is a need. Beneath it lies the fear of being late and missing something, which connects to shame, lost promotion, loss of control and financial insecurity. [L0997.016]
  • Core human drivers to test against a segment (the list is not exhaustive):
    • Power or control over outcomes [L0497.012]
    • Freedom from pain [L0497.012]
    • Gaining or conserving resources (time, money, energy) [L0497.012]
    • Status and superiority [L0497.012]
    • Connection, love and belonging [L0497.012]
    • Sexual selection (being a valuable mate) [L0497.012]
    • Keeping up with the Joneses (being at least normal) [L0497.012]
    • Growth [L0497.012]
    • Looking after loved ones [L0497.012]
  • Motorcycle gear case, Rurock: the brand entered a market flooded with helmets, leather jackets and trousers. Its target goal was to wear clothing the rider likes and express identity, look risky and rebellious, instead of wearing conventional leathers, uncomfortable boots or ugly helmets. Behaviours were riding naked bikes, pulling wheelies and avoiding conventional clothing. Psychographics showed they valued style over substance and would sacrifice some safety for better looks. Their helmets were not the safest or quietest but had designs the audience connected with. Ages ranged from 20 to 60, so demographics mattered less than the motivations behind the segment. [L0497.015]

Qualitative and Quantitative Data

  • Quantitative research gives countable value data, such as the number of people in a country or the number of buyers of a product category last year. [L0353.019]
  • Qualitative research gives opinion and problem-solving data from forums, reviews and Reddit. It is harder to read but richer in insight. Forums are a good source of qualitative research when a product is not listed on Amazon. [L0353.019]
  • Quantitative data answers how many, how much and what: numbers, percentages, ratings on a scale, and statistics that go into a spreadsheet and are analysed statistically (for example Shopify analytics or Google Analytics). Qualitative data answers why: opinions, long open answers, observations, interpretation and grouping of common themes. Both are needed. [L0497.021]
  • Quantitative data is measured and tested to show what happens (counts, percentages, scale ratings, analytics such as Shopify or Google Analytics). Qualitative data (open answers, comments, observed language) is interpreted and grouped into themes to show why it happens. Use both, because numbers alone do not reveal motives. [L0497.021, L0497.029]
  • Two research types: data analysis (statistics and hard figures) and voice of customer (what customers say in their own words about their problems, through reviews, surveys and social media). [L0497.022]
  • Start audience research with qualitative data such as customer reviews and forum posts, then back it up with quantitative figures such as counts and ratings. Quantitative data shows what and how much; qualitative data shows why. [L0995.003]
  • Combine qualitative data (customer comments on Amazon, Reddit and forums that express problems and reasons) with quantitative data (hard numbers such as how many people own a pet type). Customers reveal their problems when they explain why something matters to them. [L0450.022]
  • Desire and problem mapping, in order:
    1. List the primary desires driving the market and rate each urgency from 1 to 10, with an explanation. [L0613.005]
    2. List the problems that block those desires and how many solutions people usually try first. [L0613.005]
    3. Assess the emotional state of the market (desperate, curious, skeptical, hopeful). [L0613.005]
    4. For each desire and problem, collect at least 5-7 exact customer quotes with source links. Rate intensity from 1 to 10. Judge whether it is growing or fading, with evidence. Name the emotional triggers, and note seasonal or cyclical patterns. [L0613.005]
  • Final strategic analysis, in order:
    1. Market opportunity: market size and growth, competitive landscape, key trends, barriers to entry and growth, and emotional gaps. [L0613.022]
    2. Positioning recommendation: optimal strategy with rationale, key differentiators, repositioning options and identity angles. [L0613.022]
    3. Message strategy: core value proposition, messaging hierarchy, message changes for each segment, objection-handling framework and identity-level messaging. [L0613.022]
    4. Channel strategy: channels based on audience behaviour, content formats by channel, and a testing approach for scaling. [L0613.022]

Buyer Personas and Ideal Customer Profiles

Defining the Dream Customer

  • A dream customer is someone who buys your products and actively engages with your content to expand your brand's reach, becoming an advocate who refers new customers. [L0237.005]
  • Serving whoever buys, without a defined dream client, produces a business full of customers who cannot afford higher offers and need basic help. The owner ends up spending most time on fundamentals instead of valuable work, which drains energy over years. Choose the dream client deliberately at the start. [L0523.003]
  • Build a dream client avatar as a named character with written traits such as success level, passions, goals and attitude toward money:
    1. Choose a name and write down what you know about the person. [L0523.002]
    2. List their traits in concrete terms, such as business revenue, hobbies and personal values. [L0523.002]
    3. Search Google Images for a photo that matches the traits. [L0523.002]
    4. Print the photo and keep it visible while planning your offers and ads. [L0523.002]
  • Bait has to match what the dream client already wants and understands. Bait that only advanced buyers appreciate filters out beginners and attracts people who can later afford higher offers. Beginner-friendly bait does the opposite and brings in price-sensitive shoppers. [L0523.004]
  • Know the ideal customer in detail: the problems the products solve, where they already search for answers, who they listen to, where they spend time online, demographics (location, gender, age, income), interests and hobbies, and what they must believe before buying. Record the answers in a living document that can be shared with a team. (Older advice, Source B 2021-22.) [L0414.008]

Persona Construction

  • Build each persona from the problem, its importance, the behaviour causing it, psychographics (what the buyer values) and the goal behind the purchase. [L0450.015]
  • Build an audience persona before writing ads or a store. Name the ideal persona in one short phrase that combines a type of customer and a shared frustration. [L0395.001]
  • Persona example from the lesson: a German Shepherd owner loves the dog, but the dog sleeps on the bed and leaves hair everywhere. They have tried sticky lint rollers but dislike the paper waste. Their allergies flare up, their sleep suffers, and they feel frustrated, tired and ashamed of a dirty home. Their goal is a clean bed for peaceful sleep and peace of mind. [L0450.016]
  • Vague audience descriptions, such as a broad pet-owner group, do not give usable marketing direction. Narrow the persona to a specific type of customer with a specific shared frustration before writing ads or copy. [L0395.005]
  • Audience persona procedure, in order:
    1. Name the ideal persona in one short phrase that combines a type of customer and a shared frustration. [L0395.001]
    2. Describe demographics (geographic location, age, sex), psychographics (personality traits, attitudes, values) and behaviour (what they do and why). [L0395.001]
    3. Write the goal the customer wants to reach. [L0395.001]
    4. List their problems and pains in concrete terms. [L0395.001]
    5. Split motivation into what they move toward and what they move away from. [L0395.001]
    6. Identify where and when the problem appears and what triggers it. [L0395.001]
    7. Note why the problem matters to them emotionally or practically. [L0395.001]
    8. List their deepest fears. [L0395.001]
    9. Explain why your product is the preferred solution over alternatives. [L0395.001]
    10. Describe how the product helps them avoid or solve the problem and feel more capable, important or independent. [L0395.001]
    11. Write the brand or marketing message. [L0395.001]
    12. List where they spend time online (forums such as Reddit and Quora, Facebook groups, Instagram, websites they visit). [L0395.001]
    13. Research their questions, language and what they care about. [L0395.001]

  • Build a brand target audience sheet with questions for demographics, a goals-needs-motivations sheet (what the person desperately wants, what they fear, the biggest problem) and a research checklist. Record the goal, wants, pain points and trigger for each segment. A spreadsheet works well for comparing several segments. [L0450.007]
  • Build about three audience personas per product and test them. The best-performing audience is often not the one chosen first. Test small audiences with small budgets before scaling. [L0995.040]
  • The customer research spreadsheet is called the Build, Sell, Grow, Repeat Customer Avatar Tool. It holds one main sheet with three side-by-side avatar columns, so one file can describe up to three distinct customer profiles. (Older tool, Source B 2021-22.) [L0872.035]
  • Use survey answers to define each ideal customer profile (ICP) and the unique mechanism that explains why your product works for that group. Describe every avatar by its pain, its trigger and the outcome it wants, in the customer's own words, before writing any ad. [L0906.027]
  • Market definition is customer-led. Define the ideal customer first, then decide which products fit that customer. [L0414.048, L0771.012]

Avatars in Practice

  • Surfboard retailer example: split buyers into three avatars.
    • Beginners buying a first board.
    • Casual surfers with some experience who do not surf daily.
    • Advanced surfers who surf every day or compete.
    • A soft foam board fits the beginner avatar, so its copy should be written for beginners rather than advanced surfers, who would not respond to it. (Older example, Source B 2021-22.) [L0907.032]

Targeting Principles

  • Target gift and relative buyers as well as the end user where this is relevant to the ad. [L0819.012]
  • A product's persuasive power comes from framing it as a safe way to satisfy a natural instinct the buyer already has. Example: a toy that mimics prey lets an animal hunt without real risk. Explaining the solution in terms of the instinct it fulfils is stronger than listing features. [L0395.003]
  • Target audience research should start from the fears and pains of the buyer, not from product features. [L0395.004]

Segmentation

Why Segment

  • Market segmentation groups people who share characteristics, so a product can be aimed at a smaller audience. Segmenting a crowded product category into a new sub-market can lower competition, especially for an existing product that competitors already sell generically. [L0995.001]
  • Market segmentation means splitting a broad market into a smaller group of people who share characteristics, contexts or needs, so that one focused message can reach them. Segments respond much better to a focused message than to a general one. [L0997.002, L0998.003]
  • Market segmentation means splitting a market into a specific group of people who share characteristics or needs, so that you can address them with a focused message they respond to more strongly. [L0998.003, L0997.002]
  • Competitors who sell a product to many generic groups ("we cater for these guys and these guys") produce a diluted message that does not play well on social media. A narrow niche with a specific problem produces a more potent message. [L0450.020]
  • Targeting everyone with a general message weakens the response because the audience is too diluted. Example: a product that simply keeps items organised. A narrower context, such as travellers who must match items to outfits, makes the same product far more compelling. [L0997.003]
  • Targeting every buyer of a product category, such as all snowboarders or all motorcycle owners, dilutes the audience and weakens the message. Splitting the audience by context, such as beginners versus park riders, produces a more focused and more responsive message. [L0998.002]
  • Targeting all snowboarders is too broad. Narrowing to beginners or to riders in the park gives a segment with a specific problem in a specific context. The same logic applies to motorcycle products. [L0997.004]
  • A generic product message in a flooded market gets lost. Name a specific audience or problem (for example, a large-dog or specific-breed owner instead of "removes pet hair"). Use broad messages only when the segment is already well understood. [L0995.005]

Segmentation Dimensions

  • Segment the audience in several ways: by pet type (dog, cat), breed, use context (bed, sofa, car, RV) or trigger. Each segment becomes its own persona with its own problems and messages. A product can be re-targeted to another segment if the first one does not perform. [L0450.017]
  • Context-based segment from the lesson: owners who travel with pets in RVs. At least 60 percent of RV owners bring their pets, and a small space makes allergies worse. A product positioned as an RV or car pet hair brush, designed for tight spaces, speaks to that context directly. [L0450.018]
  • Segment by context or problem, such as beginners versus riders in a park. [L0993.020]
  • A narrow segment can shape the whole store, or it can be carried by the ad alone. A segment focused on the bed problem, for example, can drive the advert even if the store stays broader. [L0997.021]
  • Rebuild a proven product's page for a distinct audience, such as people working from home or gym-goers with neck pain. Use ads, GIFs and copy aimed only at that group. The same product can reach a new market this way. [L0871.010]
  • A segment is strong when it has a specific culture, identity or situation, such as a cultural couple segment for jewellery. Validate each segment with research in the niche. [L0995.041]

Segmentation Examples

  • A wallet product that did poorly with broad targeting worked better when aimed at a camping and outdoor audience. The creatives showed the wallet in mountains or at the sea, and the landing page was written for that audience. [L0995.031]
  • Two snowboard audiences were used: beginners and experienced riders trying new things. Snowboard interests performed better than broad targeting. [L0995.032]
  • A pet hair removal brush could be aimed at owners of heavy-shedding breeds, such as German Shepherds, whose dogs sleep on the bed and trigger allergies. Owners of other breeds, such as Golden Retrievers or Labradors, would be a weaker segment for that bed-related message. [L0998.007]
  • Pet hair brush research: German Shepherd owners complained most about shedding on the bed. Owners of other large shedders, such as Golden Retrievers and Labradors, also mentioned the dog being on the bed. The bed context came up repeatedly, which validated it as the segment angle. [L0993.021]
  • Pet hair brush research, detailed: German Shepherds were the main segment, with Golden Retrievers and Labradors as related owners. The most common scenario was the dog getting on the bed. Owners of German Shepherds are heavy shedders whose beds get dirty and who suffer from allergies. [L0997.020]

Segmentation Procedures

(Procedure text as found in the source; the same order appears in more than one unit.)

  1. Confirm the product solves a real problem. [L0993.020]
  2. Pick a candidate segment defined by context or problem, such as beginners versus riders in a park. [L0993.020]
  3. Validate that the segment actually has the problem by reading what people say on Amazon reviews and in forums. [L0993.020]
  4. Check that enough need exists in the segment before committing to it. [L0993.020]
  5. Choose the angle and message for the validated segment. [L0993.020]
  6. Do not invent a segment from thin air. Base it on research. [L0993.020]
  • Segmentation check, in order:
    1. Check whether the product solves a clear problem. [L0997.018, L0998.004]
    2. Identify which audiences the product can be targeted at. [L0997.018]
    3. Look at the kinds of audiences the product could be aimed at. Segment them yourself, but only where there is enough need. [L0997.018, L0998.004]
    4. Validate that people in the segment actually have the problem, using research on Amazon and in forums. [L0997.018, L0998.004]
    5. Decide which message to send to each segment. [L0997.018, L0998.004]
  • Narrow segmentation must not rest on assumption. A segment must have enough real need to justify it, otherwise a narrow group will not generate enough demand. Create a segment from your own assumption only if you can confirm the problem exists in that group. [L0998.005]
  • Never create a segment from thin air. Validate it by checking that people in the segment actually have the problem, using Amazon reviews and forum discussions to see what customers say. [L0997.019]
  • Validate a segment with evidence from what people already say, such as Amazon product reviews and forum discussions, rather than inventing it from scratch. Repeated mentions of the same usage context, such as a pet's behaviour at home, show which buyer group actually has the problem. [L0998.006]
  • Do not invent audiences without research. Validate each segment against research in the niche. [L0995.041]
  • Market segmentation in the store: a narrow segment can shape a whole store, or it can be carried by the ad alone. [L0997.021]
  • Verify the problem before building around it. A common trap, especially for sellers who make their own product, is assuming the problem they identified is widely experienced. If only a few people actually have it, the product will not sell. [L0872.003] (Older advice, Source B 2021-22.)

Awareness, Sophistication and Positioning

Awareness Levels

  • Target all three awareness levels:
    • Customers who have the problem and already know a solution.
    • Customers who feel the problem but do not know a solution.
    • Customers who do not yet see the problem.
    • Educating unaware customers about a hidden health risk is a route few competitors take. [L0450.014]
  • Positioning depends on how informed the market already is. Before drafting copy, gauge the buyer's knowledge of the pain point, their familiarity with the offering, and how many times they have already encountered the same solution elsewhere. [L0450.021]

Market Sophistication

  • A market shows inverted sophistication when claim complexity is high (every competitor uses identical messaging formulas) but customer understanding is low (most prospects fail basic knowledge tests). This produces jaded customers who are ignorant of the actual dangers. Strategy must shift from aspirational gain-framing ("maximise benefits") to naming specific, irreversible consequences and one-way doors that prospects face ("the decision you make now sets the floor she lives on for life"). [L0221.017]
  • Prospects give sanitised, socially acceptable answers when speaking publicly, but hold raw, honest, unfiltered thoughts privately. Market research must uncover the private truth. Not "I want to maximise my benefits" but "I don't want to be the fool who left money on the table," because private thoughts, not public statements, drive purchasing decisions. Ads must speak to the genuine driver, not the polished justification. [L0221.019]

Positioning

  • Position around the underlying need, not surface features. Example: a fitness brand positions around staying consistent and removing distractions, not around getting fit. [L0307.033, L0450.023]
  • Position a product by the specific problem it solves for a defined group, not by a broad category claim. [L0450.020, L0995.005]
  • Where a product is sold to a crowded market, the message should name the specific buyer and problem, so the message stands out on social platforms. [L0450.020]

Audience Research Checklist

(Consolidated from the procedures above; all source ids are kept.)

  1. Check that the product solves a real problem. [L0993.020, L0997.018, L0998.004]
  2. Define the ideal customer and their problem, importance, behaviour, psychographics and goal. [L0450.015, L0395.001]
  3. Use qualitative sources (Amazon reviews, forums, Reddit) and quantitative sources (Statista, Google Trends, platform analytics). [L0450.001, L0450.002, L0497.021, L0995.003]
  4. Validate each segment with what real customers say before building on it. [L0998.006, L0997.019]
  5. Choose the angle and message for the validated segment, and test about three personas with small budgets. [L0993.020, L0995.040]
  6. Refine demographics last, after motivation and problem are clear. [L0997.006, L0450.015]

Defining the target market

  • Older source (2021-2022): An e-commerce market has three parts: the products you could sell, the customers who would buy them, and the competitors already selling to those customers. Research has to cover all three before a market is chosen. [L1058.001]

  • A brand should serve a specific market segment with clear characteristics rather than everyone. Niching down lets the store speak to customers in a way they feel understood, and a broad brand can be built later as it grows. [L1044.009]

  • Target audience research covers three core parts:

    • Demographics: who they are.
    • Psychographics: personality and behavior, and how the product fits into their life.
    • Goals, needs and motivations related to the product or the desired outcome.

    A short customer research document is enough to start. [L1044.011]

  • Entering a niche that is already served can still work when a product is specifically adapted to a segment, such as separating a large-dog owner from a general pet owner. Specificity lowers competition in a flooded market where many products already solve the same basic problem. [L0999.047]

  • Market segmentation by culture can work well for products such as jewelry, because culture defines a specific buyer group. A niche chosen this way can be dominated early by a single brand before broader competition arrives. [L0999.048]

  • Avoid making up audiences out of thin air. Only target segments that research supports, and validate each segment with reviews, forums or data before building ads around it. [L0999.051]

  • Check the size of the appeal for a niche before building a product around it. A small niche may require hundreds of wasted impressions for each real prospect. Commit only when the cost per prospect fits the margin. [L1261.040]

  • When many competitors cover the same topic, look for the adjacent subject nobody is covering and build your position there. An overlooked angle near a crowded market can give you visibility that copying the leaders cannot. [L1343.041]

Procedure: Screen niches by customer persona

  1. Define the ideal customer, for example women above 45. [L1219.042]
  2. Choose the target market and language, for example the German market. [L1219.042]
  3. Consider seasonal demand for Q4, such as winter products, customised jewelry and toys. [L1219.042]
  4. Screen niches for products that fit that persona instead of testing products at random. [L1219.042]

Research order before testing

  • Spend at least three days researching why customers buy a product before the first test, and five to seven days if time allows. Research comes before testing, not after. [L1195.001]
  • Testing product demand first and studying the customer afterwards is the wrong order. Start by studying the ads and messaging that sellers use, then check demand. [L1195.002]
  • Define the ICP (ideal customer profile: demographics, age group, ethnicity, life stage) and the USP (the unique outcome the product gives that alternatives do not) before any product test. [L1195.003]

Procedure: Audience research for a product idea

  1. Define the target market: which problems the audience has, who they are, what they do, how they behave, and which underlying needs and motivations drive the purchase. [L0999.003]
  2. Gather qualitative data first from reviews and forums, such as Amazon reviews and Reddit threads on the product problem. [L0999.003]
  3. Note demographics, psychographics, behaviors and needs that people reveal about themselves in their reviews and posts. [L0999.003]
  4. Back up the qualitative findings with quantitative figures, such as how often a term or behavior is mentioned or which breeds, models or segments are most often named. [L0999.003]
  5. Narrow the audience to a few specific segments (commonly three) that each have a concrete problem and a tailored message. [L0999.003]
  6. Write ads, landing pages and creatives that speak directly to each segment. [L0999.003]
  7. Test the segments and keep the one that responds best, since it may not be the one first expected. [L0999.003]

Procedure: Get close to the product before writing

  1. Obtain the product and use it the way a customer would, including taking it apart and demonstrating it to others. [L1370.119]
  2. For a service, use it yourself if possible, talk to its users and to users of competing services. [L1370.119]
  3. For a special offer, try it out on real people and check that they understand it and find it intriguing before you build the copy around it. [L1370.119]

Qualitative and quantitative evidence

  • Quantitative data gives the what, such as counts, percentages, ratings and averages. Qualitative data gives the why, such as reasons, opinions and situations. Use both, but start with qualitative insight and confirm it with numbers. [L0999.004]
  • Before writing copy about a product, become an expert on it through specific knowledge: its features, how it works, and what is truly unique about it. Expertise on the product lets you find the angle that others miss, and it can take minutes, hours or weeks. [L1366.031]
  • For a product that needs to connect on an emotional or spiritual level, such as jewelry or healing products, research pain points, emotional drivers, and why customers buy. Then use 3 to 4 pages of that research as input to write copy. [L1158.031]

Procedure: Customer avatar research methods

  1. On Amazon, open listings of similar products, read reviews, check reviewers' profiles, age, and tone, and note their problems and situation. [L1158.022]
  2. On Google, search the pain point and the current solutions the market offers. [L1158.022]
  3. Research pain-point statistics in blogs, journals, news, and industry reports to use as social-proof data on landing pages and ads. [L1158.022]

Buyer, user and customer avatar

  • Build the customer avatar from people who have actually purchased, not from site visitors or leads. A profile built from non-buyers does not match who converts. [L1243.013]
  • Choose the target buyer separately from the user. A gift product may be bought by grandparents or parents for children, so define the customer avatar and the buyer who will pay for the product before writing the angle. [L1440.010]
  • Learn who the customer is and what motivates them before writing, using specific information about their likes, dislikes and expectations. If you are a typical customer for the product yourself, that knowledge already counts, but verify it against the real market. [L1366.032, L1366.102]

Segmentation pillars

  • The four segmentation pillars are:

    • Demographics: static facts such as age, gender, income, socioeconomic status and geographic location.
    • Psychographics: personality traits such as worry, price consciousness, integrity and desire for status.
    • Behaviors: what a person does in the buying context.
    • Underlying needs and motivations.

    [L0999.005]

  • Needs and motivations are the most important pillar because they explain why someone buys. Demographics and behaviors are surface-level and observable. [L0999.005]

  • Define demographics last when building a target audience, after you have worked out the behaviors, needs and motivations of the buyer. Demographics then refine the segment rather than define it. [L0998.011]

  • A jewelry product with a status appeal may sell to buyers aged roughly 20 to 50, so age alone does not define that segment. A product tied to a life stage, such as one related to menstruation, makes age a decisive filter, since the average age of ending menstruation is over 50. [L0998.012]

  • Psychographics cover personality traits, values, beliefs and attitudes. Examples are whether a person is organized, tends to worry, values control or belonging, or holds certain religious or world views. These help decide how a message should sound and what it should appeal to. [L0998.013]

  • Behavior covers what people do, in which context, and what triggers the need for the product. Map the product onto the buyer's daily routine or journey to see where the problem appears. [L0998.014]

  • A vegetable slicer fits the behavior of someone preparing food in the kitchen. The trigger is that they are cutting vegetables badly, getting tough and uneven cuts, and finding the process annoying. [L0998.015]

  • A watch storage case sells better to frequent travelers who must choose a different watch for each outfit on a trip than to all watch owners. A message about matching watches to clothes on a trip is more focused than a general claim that the case holds your watches. [L0998.028]

Awareness and positioning

  • Crowded niches such as supplements, beauty and pet products require knowing the awareness stages (unaware, problem aware, solution aware, product aware) and mapping the customer's pain point to the product. This is advanced knowledge, so it is not the starting point for a beginner. [L1175.005]
  • Older source (2021-2022): Around seven touchpoints with a specific e-commerce business happen on average before a customer completes a purchase, so a brand needs repeated exposure to become familiar to buyers. [L1117.003]
  • Each product has a nature in the mind of the buyer, and the appeal must match it. Work out what the buyer needs first, then what they insist on, such as a safety product that works reliably and installs easily, and build the copy around those points. [L1366.033, L1366.101]
  • Look for the specific use in which your product beats every alternative. Measure that advantage in a unit the buyer already values, such as output per unit bought. Target only buyers who gain most from that advantage. [L1369.005]
  • A product sold only by price per ton or per unit is a commodity that competitors can undercut. Reposition it on the result the buyer gets, such as cubic feet of gas per pound of coal or tons of iron melted per ton of coke. [L1369.006]

Case: Repositioning a coal and coke producer (early 1900s, historical)

  • Situation: A small coal and coke producer in the early 1900s sold through brokers at per-ton prices. Its mines ran only three or four days a week in good times and one or two days in bad times. [L1369.007]
  • Insight: The owner found that his gas coal produced more gas per pound than any other coal in the district. Gas companies within several hundred miles could therefore afford to pay 25 to 33 percent more for it. [L1369.007]
  • Gas coal letters: The letters argued the cost per cubic foot of gas and offered a carload of coal free of charge. If the buyer's own tests did not show at least 25 percent savings, the carload cost nothing. If they did, the buyer signed a contract for a year at 1.25 dollars per ton f.o.b. Powellton, W. Va. [L1369.007]
  • Competitive response: When a customer drifted to a cheaper bid, the seller bought samples of both coals, had them lab-tested, and sent the cost per cubic foot of gas from each without comment. Most of those customers came back. [L1369.007]
  • Coke: The seller found that low sulphur and phosphorus were valuable. A steel company agreed to pay 25 cents more per ton for coke low in phosphorus, on a requirement of 60 to 90 tons a day. [L1369.007]
  • Stove maker test: The coke melted 15 tons of iron per ton of coke, against 8 or 9 tons for ordinary coke. Melting cost fell to 47 cents per ton of iron against 80 to 90 cents, with coke priced at 7 dollars a ton delivered. [L1369.007]
  • Mailing: The seller then mailed letters built on tons of iron melted to every stove founder within 500 miles. This gave it nearly a monopoly in that stove and range territory until competitors matched its oven loading. [L1369.007]
  • Duration: Mail sales of its output continued for about five years from 1907 or 1908. [L1369.007]

Benefits, claims and offer fit

  • Choose benefits that a broad share of buyers care about, such as beauty or saving time. Do not lead with prevention or problems that few people act on. Prevention claims usually produce a fraction of the response that a direct, desirable benefit does. [L1261.037]
  • A toothpaste that prevents decay and also beautifies teeth will likely draw far more response from the beauty appeal than from the decay-prevention appeal. A soap that treats a skin condition and also improves complexion should lead with complexion, since the condition appeals to only a tiny share of buyers. [L1261.038]
  • Some claims, though not the main appeal, still influence a meaningful share of prospects and are worth including in ads. Their inclusion should be decided by traced returns, not assumptions. [L1261.039]
  • A mailing list must match the buying behavior the offer requires. A mail-order offer sent to a list of local retail customers rarely works, so choose lists of people who already buy by mail. [L1366.034, L1366.100]

Further details

  • Define target audience segments by combining demographic identity with core motivation or problem. Example: Sleep-Deprived Professionals (busy workers experiencing burnout and poor sleep quality), Luxury Self-Care Enthusiasts (buyers treating wellness as a ritual and accepting premium pricing), Beauty-Conscious Sleepers (primarily women concerned with skin and hair protection during sleep). This structure reveals the right messaging angle and value proposition for each group. [L0318.019]

  • Niche down into specific groups such as home grillers, student athletes, dog owners, or watch collectors, because broad groups such as general online shoppers or deal seekers have no shared passion. A niche should be narrow enough that its members recognize themselves in the content. [L0487.020]

  1. Identify the core need, the gap between where the buyer is and where they want to be.
  2. Map the buyer's demographics, such as age, gender and location.
  3. Map their behaviours, such as what they do each week.
  4. Identify the reason behind the behaviour and the deeper motivation driving it. [L0644.034]

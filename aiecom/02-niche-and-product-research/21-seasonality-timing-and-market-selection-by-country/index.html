2.21 Seasonality, timing and market selection by country

Season and timing principles

  • A product must match the season that is active in the target market. A cold-weather product should not run in hot summer months, and seasonal timing matters more than most beginners expect [L0347.005].
  • The same offer can fail in one month and succeed in another. A winter product that did not sell in April started selling in November when the weather turned cold [L0502.034].
  • A product that did poorly in August or September can be retried in November (Q4) and may perform better [L0501.034].
  • Seasonal products sell most when launched well before the event, because early sellers capture most of the demand. For Christmas products, the recommended timing is a launch in late October, on Halloween or on the day after [L0352.031].
  • Seasonal products can be copied quickly, and supplier shipping times can delay launches. Prepare the next seasonal product as early as possible to avoid competing with copycats [L0352.032].
  • Seasonal ideas need early timing. A trend from last year, such as a holiday costume trend, is usually too late once the season has started, so list seasonal products well before the peak [L0372.018].
  • Seasonal products can be planned months ahead. Check whether competitors already post content for the season, and search older seasonal terms to see whether demand existed in past years [L1186.008].
  • Use Google Trends to confirm a spike before testing, and check the trend for the product itself, not only its category [L0638.025].
  • Test seasonal ideas before the season begins. For a summer product, prepare the offer several weeks before the season starts [L0577.034].
  • Time the test of cooling or ice-based products ahead of the warm season, so the test runs while buying interest is rising. A product tested out of season may show weak sales that do not reflect its real potential [L0657.048].
  • Time the launch to the start of the season in the target market. The speaker launched a summer-type product in Australia around September, when the weather there turned warm [L0661.012].
  • Seasonal products must launch in their peak window. An ice cream product tested late in summer converted poorly, and early summer (about May to June) is the better launch window [L0764.027].
  • Timing can matter more than the video. A pen product generated about 30,000 USD in one month (August) with about 6 million views because it matched back-to-school demand [L0764.028].
  • Start research by identifying problems or needs people have in a specific period, then look for trending products that solve them. Do not hunt for winning products at random [L1108.005].
  • Before a Q4 push, choose a product unique enough to stand out in a crowded marketplace, such as one that solves a problem differently from common alternatives [L0950.068].
  • Products that are not yet saturated in January and February often have low CPC, because there is less competition for ad space. If you find a working product before that period, buy stock early, since factories may close during the Chinese New Year period [L1347.026].
  • Seasonal products such as kids' toys can be tested before Q4 and Christmas. Start searching early enough to have stock and ads ready before demand peaks [L1454.015].

Q4 and gift products

  • Products that peak in Q4 (October through December) include gadgets, jewelry, kids' items, dog and pet products, fashion items, customizable gifts, toys and baby products. Gift products sell especially well because customers buy for others, not only for themselves [L0309.003].
  • Gift niches that performed strongest in Q4 were kids toys, personalized jewelry and winter clothing. Christmas gifting drives this demand [L1108.006].
  • Winners in Q4 included children's gifts, winter clothing, winter shoes that keep feet warm, and personalized jewelry such as name or message pieces. These categories are not to be copied exactly but used as a basis for product research [L1220.019].
  • Personalized gifts and gifts for parents, kids or grandchildren sell well around Christmas and in Q4. Kids toys were also a Q4 product for the lesson's seller [L0774.045].
  • Gift products such as personalized necklaces, and gifts for parents or kids, suit Christmas [L0773.047].
  • Non-electronic gifts are preferred over electronics because they carry less customs and quality risk. Children's toys work well in Q4 [L1220.012].
  • Giftable products tend to perform strongly in winter, but shipping times must be reliable, because late delivery is a serious risk around gift periods [L0387.036].
  • Seasonal winners in Q4 2024 included:
    • hoodies that began in five simple colors and later grew into many designs;
    • family matching clothing for kids, babies and adults in the winter, when energy prices were high;
    • games, toys and puzzles, which also performed well in Q4 when people stayed at home during lockdowns [L0780.007].
  • Health supplements show strong Q4 demand around Black Friday and Christmas [L1187.009].

Climate and weather-linked products

  • Fashion and seasonal apparel align with climate shifts. Push sweaters and cold-weather items in fall and winter, when customers actually need them [L0209.023].
  • Look for products that solve a seasonal or environmental problem in the target market. In Switzerland, summer runs roughly from May to the end of September, so cold-weather products have demand during the rest of the year [L0492.007].
  • Energy shortage concerns in Europe from 2022 onward made warmth a stronger need, which supported a cold-weather product. Watch current events that affect how people heat or warm their homes. (Dated: this reflects the 2022 situation.) [L0492.008]
  • Climate-related products sell better where the cold is extreme. Sweden at minus 20 degrees compares with Switzerland at minus 4 degrees. Check where the use case is strongest before choosing the test market [L1316.028].
  • Warm-weather products such as summer outfits or swimwear should be tested in warm markets such as the US or Australia rather than Europe at the start of the year. Cold-weather products, such as winter items, can be tested in every market [L1161.007].
  • A season-tied product, such as golf scratch repair in winter, may have low demand at certain times. Cold and rainy weather in Europe can make a warming or comfort product relevant [L1176.009].
  • In cold months in Europe and the US, products that fit the weather and winter gifting can be tested, such as heated tea cups or tea warmers. Choose products by what fits the current season in the target country, not only by what a spy tool shows [L1108.042].
  • Tool-type products that solve a practical home problem may work best in Australia, the US and Canada. Weather-related products, such as summer dresses, should be tested in countries where the season is only starting, using a seasonal angle such as a pre-summer sale [L1171.010].
  • Relaunch a seasonal niche ahead of its demand peak. For home decor in Switzerland, the spring cleaning season starts about one month before the relaunch, and cold, snowy months showed little interest [L0388.030].
  • Weather-linked and seasonal products can be good niches, such as cooling products for summer and warm or pet protection products for winter [L0774.044].
  • Niches such as camping and adventure perform well when targeting Australia, New Zealand, Canada and the US. Sub-niches with passionate audiences also work well because they make audience targeting clearer [L0788.042].
  • Seasonal product example: Apple Watch straps are a summer product because people walk and do outdoor activities more and the watch is used more. Heat-friendly fabric and bright colors help. In the lesson's test, TikTok ads were cheaper per acquisition than Facebook ads for these straps. A free wallpaper or theme download was used as an add-on offer [L0577.033].

Hemisphere shifts and extending a season

  • A seasonal product can be extended by switching to a market in the opposite hemisphere. For example, a winter product can be sold to US buyers in winter while the Australian audience is in summer. This keeps sales going after the home-market season ends [L0347.006].
  • Seasonal products sell well only in their season, so a seasonal brand needs extra tactics and new markets in the off-season. Testing winter products in Australia while it is summer in Europe can keep sales going [L0502.033].
  • Seasons are reversed in the Southern Hemisphere. A product that sold well in the Northern winter can be tested in Southern winter markets such as Australia. Summer products that worked in the Northern hemisphere can be tested there during the Southern summer [L0562.022].
  • Winter products depend on hemisphere. It is summer in the southern hemisphere while it is winter in the north, so check the target market before choosing seasonal items [L0387.036].
  • Seasonal niches can be tested by checking what sells in one hemisphere's season for the other market. For example, winter products in the US can be tested in Australia during its winter. Searching for a product in the US and selling it in another market is a valid test method [L0678.031].
  • Products that did well in summer can be tested in Australia, where summer starts when the Northern Hemisphere is in winter. Products that sold well in Australian summer can be tested in Europe or the US during winter [L0773.047, L1108.007].
  • Products that worked in the northern summer can be tested in Australia, which enters summer in Q4 [L0774.044].

Holiday and event launch deadlines

  • A holiday is only worth targeting if there is time to prepare and run ads before it. If the holiday is only two to three weeks away, it is generally too late, so choose the next upcoming event instead [L0577.035].
  • Avoid testing a product with a very close deadline, such as Halloween when it is only a day away. Products for a holiday need to be tested well ahead of time [L1220.020].
  • Launch windows and deadlines differ by step, not by contradiction: the Christmas launch window is late October through Halloween or the day after [L0352.031], while a product with a deadline one day away should not be tested [L1220.020].
  • Seasonal products such as Valentine's Day and Mother's Day can be sold, but orders must be delivered before the date to avoid chargebacks. The presenter avoids fixed-date products because of delivery risk and Chinese New Year timing [L1184.024].
  • Q4 gift products should be tested early enough to arrive before Christmas. Establish the last date an order can be shipped and still arrive on time [L1220.013].
  • For a Valentine's Day campaign, pre-test content 2 months before the event. For gift products that overlap with Christmas, such as love bracelets and charm jewelry, use the same products and content with adjusted messaging 2 weeks before each occasion [L0309.017].
  • For a Valentine's Day campaign, the store is built about one month before the holiday, and the winning product should be found at least about 15 days before the holiday [L1469.023].

Choosing the target market

  • The same niche can be approved in one country and rejected in another, because competitor and supplier targets differ per market. Set the target country before grading any niche (older: Source B course, 2021 to 2022) [L0490.006].
  • When researching high-ticket dropshipping products, choose the market to sell into first, then select the specific products within that market. This keeps product ideas tied to a market that can support them (older: 2021 to 2022) [L0913.001].
  • Infer the target country from the competitor store. A store in US dollars and English is assumed to target the US. Traffic-estimation tools were unreliable in the mentor's own checks, so trust their country data only when it points overwhelmingly to one country [L1161.005].
  • Competitors running ads for the same product usually target the same top four or five countries. Use that cluster as the starting market list for a new test, and expand to other markets only after the first data comes in [L1176.043].
  • Most dropshippers in the lesson examples target the top markets of the US, UK, Australia, Canada and New Zealand. Products that target the US, Australia and Canada were treated as the main markets to evaluate [L1172.008].
  • Starting countries for first testing: the US, Canada, Australia and the UK are the strongest. They are English-speaking, accept credit cards and PayPal, and have good online buying habits [L0645.036]. (Conflict note: this is the default for English-speaking tests; when CPM in these markets is too high, the European choice applies instead, see the competition section.)
  • The Big Five markets (USA, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand) are the usual default. Smaller markets such as Greece are weaker because of lower buying power and higher shipping cost, which makes dropshipping harder, even though simple pages may convert more easily there [L0691.026].
  • Targeting options for initial tests: the US, the UK or Australia, or a native-language market such as Germany or France if the operator speaks that language [L1173.009].
  • Test first in a market whose language you speak and whose buying habits you understand, such as your home country. There is usually less competition from dropshippers there. Once you have results you can expand, and you do not have to stay in that country [L1219.016].
  • Germany, Switzerland and Austria can offer lower competition for many products and strong buying power. Test them when capacity allows [L1185.025].
  • When a shortlist is built for one home market, only test products that suit that country's buyers. A list of 5 to 10 products may yield only two or three candidates for a single market such as Switzerland [L1185.039].
  • For a trend-based approach, find products trending in the US or Canada over the last two months, then test them in smaller markets such as Australia, New Zealand, Germany and Switzerland [L1184.008].
  • Pick products that could sell in German-speaking and English-speaking markets, such as Germany, Switzerland, Austria and Australia. Build a separate store branding and language for each market [L1431.006].
  • The ideal product idea is one that is already selling well in another market and can be brought to a new one. Find the problem first, then the solution [L1432.015].
  • Sending a product to multiple countries may reveal different results. A product that failed in one market can work in another. Consider countries with e-commerce potential beyond the usual German-speaking markets, and test them [L1316.027].
  • Start in the country where you speak the language well. For an English speaker, that is the US. Starting in a country with a language barrier adds work at the beginning (older: 2021) [L0962.090].
  • France was one of the first markets where shoppers encountered dropshipping, so French buyers easily recognize generic dropshipping stores. A French launch needs custom content and full native translation, and it still requires research into local brands and trust [L1158.052].
  • In the EU, fashion is presented as a strong niche for general stores, especially in France, where fashion and orthopedic sneakers sell well. Beauty products also perform well in France [L0660.008].
  • Test products through pricing and messaging in a single market first, then expand to other countries once the audience is clear [L0995.046].

Competition, CPM and market-size checks

  • Check market competition in each country before choosing it. Alternative markets such as Germany, Switzerland, Austria, the Scandinavian countries, France and Belgium were recommended over the US and Canada, where ad costs are high [L0492.013].
  • Canada, Australia and the US were described as expensive for testing in recent periods, which pushed the choice toward European markets. Check current CPM in each country before choosing a test market [L1219.015].
  • A store focused only on Canada usually faces less competition than a store primarily targeting the US market. The same logic applies to other English-speaking markets such as Australia or the UK (older: 2021 to 2022) [L0905.019].
  • Products trending in the US can be sold in other markets, where competition is lower. A US trend tested on TikTok produced weaker results than expected [L0492.014].
  • Choose products that are trending in the United States and sell them in other markets such as Germany, Switzerland, Austria, Australia, New Zealand or Canada. Competition there is lower, and the combined German-speaking market (Germany, Switzerland, Austria) is about 100 million people [L0519.019].
  • Saturation in the US does not rule out a product. Being an early adopter in a new market and offering a better offer can still produce strong results, even if many advertisers run the same product in the US [L0519.020].
  • Avoid launching a product in the United States when it is already heavily sold there, since saturation makes it hard to win. Test instead in markets where the same product is not yet common, such as Australia, New Zealand, Germany, Switzerland or Austria [L0661.005].
  • Products already advertised heavily in one market are proof of demand, so they are a good starting point for research. Take such a product and test it in a different market where competition is lower [L0661.001].
  • An ad running in only one country tells you that the product may already sell there, not that it will fail elsewhere. A product with strong engagement in one market can be tested in another [L0661.006].
  • A product that is saturated in the US, which has high CPMs, can still be a valid candidate when moved to a cheaper market, where competition is often lower and ads cost less. Test the transferred product in the new market before judging it [L1161.011].
  • Target countries where the product is in season and CPMs are lower. For seasonal holidays such as Halloween, consider other countries with lower CPM if the US CPM is too high [L0773.048].
  • Halloween is celebrated in many countries, not only the US. For seasonal demand such as Halloween, sell in countries where CPM is lower if the US CPM is too high [L0774.046].
  • Niche criteria must be adjusted to the market size. The US demand numbers are not comparable with Canada because of population differences [L0805.042] (2024).
  • Countries with small populations, such as Canada, do not work with the same niche criteria as the US. Use revised criteria for smaller markets instead of applying US thresholds [L1285.013] (2024).
  • For a demand test in a smaller country, a population share can justify a lower target. If the UK has about 20 percent of the US population, either find a bigger niche or move one level broader rather than lowering the target blindly [L0962.089] (older: 2021).
  • The US niche validation uses a demand threshold of 10,000. Smaller markets such as Australia have fewer sub-niches, so sellers there may need to niche up a level to reach that threshold (older: 2020) [L1088.003].
  • Niches selling to a small population should be widened to a broader category so enough visitors arrive. Large-population markets can use very specific niche stores, which convert better (older: 2020) [L1111.010].
  • Pandemic conditions in 2020 did not change the product selection criteria. Recession or fear-driven concerns should not be used to drop a niche that passes the tests, because online buying remained strong (older: 2020) [L0967.011].

Research steps for a new country

  1. Set a VPN to the target country, then search the supplier name or product on Google Shopping to see the local results (older: 2022) [L0665.008].
  2. Use the Source A research tool with the target country selected to see whether people search for the product there (older: 2022) [L0665.008].
  3. Search results depend on location. When researching a market for a country you do not live in, use a VPN and set the location to the country you plan to sell in, or results may come from your home country or other countries (older: 2021 to 2022) [L1329.012].
  4. Use a VPN set to the target country so Google returns local results. Without it, search results in another country distort competitor and niche findings (older: 2021) [L1279.014].
  5. Market size check for the Facebook Ad Library:
    1. Search for the outlook for growth or market valuation of the industry (for example the pet industry).
    2. Check the ad library for each target country (for example Germany) to see how many sellers run ads there.
    3. Countries with lower saturation are better test markets [L0895.023].
  6. Facebook Ad Library country check:
    1. Search a product keyword in the Facebook Ad Library and use the country filter to limit results to one market, such as the United States.
    2. Compare it with a test market such as Germany. Low scaling in the test market suggests room for a new seller [L0895.036].
  7. Check whether a product is popular in each target country before testing. A product that sells well in one country may not sell in another. For example, air fryers are less common in Switzerland than in the US [L1176.010].
  8. Compare the new country's shipping infrastructure and supplier responsiveness with a market you already know. Check how quickly suppliers reply and how reliably parcels arrive before committing money to ads or inventory [L1441.045].

Language, translation and language arbitrage

  • Test products with English-speaking markets before translating the store into other languages, even if competitors are not marketing there. Translation requires both professional translation services and a customer service virtual assistant fluent in that language, which adds significant cost and complexity [L0280.006].
  • Start product research with English-language ads. The US English market has a larger total addressable market (TAM) than smaller language markets. Study which creatives and messages already run there before looking at underserved markets [L1195.055].
  • Language arbitrage: take an ad that already converts in one language and run it in a language market that has not seen it yet. The Spanish-speaking market in the US has about 60 million people, a smaller total addressable market than the English market, but far less competition [L1195.008].
  • The Spanish-language market in the US is largely untapped. Its total addressable market is smaller than the white and Black American markets, but auctions are less saturated, so demand is proven while competition is not [L1019.029].
  • Products whose ads are mostly in Spanish can be run, but only if the landing pages are customized and everything is translated into Spanish. This is harder for a beginner, so beginners should skip Spanish-language ad products at the start [L1187.007].
  • Taobao videos mostly feature Asian presenters and may underperform in the US market. Recast them with white or Black American talent when producing custom ads for the US [L1195.014].
  • When the market is English-speaking, a product that worked for a long time there can be tested in other languages such as German or French. Native-language markets often have less competition [L1173.008].
  • Validate a product that sells well in one country by running the same product with ads in the language of another target market. If the new market also shows a strong hook rate and CTR, the product is worth more store work [L1108.041].
  • When testing an ebook or digital product in a new country, translate the mockup and ad copy into that country's language before running ads. The lesson's example built a German mockup for a German market test, which makes it possible to judge whether the offer itself is attractive to local buyers [L1131.063].
  • Entering an untapped market needs only minimal effort beyond the product. Native translations and local payment methods are required, and Stripe lets you enable the most used payment methods for each country. Custom branding or UGC is not needed from the start [L1158.013].
  • Study what works in other markets to find message patterns, but do not copy a competitor's exact wording. Adapt the underlying structure to your own market [L0378.014].
  • Pricing differs by market. Australian dollars look cheap to US buyers, so pricing and currency must be checked per country [L0995.046].
  • When pricing in countries where the local currency is significantly smaller than USD (such as Malaysia at 4 MYR to 1 USD), do not automatically adjust price points by converting wholesale cost. Validate your intended prices against what other online stores in that country are selling for, and use market-based pricing (older: 2022) [L0249.103].
  • For an Italian-market test in Q4, classic, mid-priced jewelry was suggested because Italian buyers were said to prefer classic styles over expensive fashion. Treat this as a hypothesis and validate it with a small test budget before scaling [L1219.058].

Testing a proven product in a new market

  • Do not reinvent the wheel. Take a product that sells well in one market and test it in a market where nobody is selling it in volume. Check the product in the new market before scaling [L0388.026].
  • Start by testing products that already show scaling ads in the USA or Australia, because a product that works there may also work in other countries. Adapt the store to the new market, including language and payment methods [L0361.017].
  • Differentiating by market: take a product proven in the US and launch it in English-speaking countries such as Australia, New Zealand, the UK and Canada, or in European markets such as Germany, France, the Netherlands and Switzerland. Check in the Facebook Ad Library whether the product is scaling in the new market. Low competition there is the differentiator [L1158.012].
  • Comfy case: a sleeved blanket, first sold in the US market, was adapted with minor product and design changes and launched in Australia and New Zealand, reaching over 300 million dollars in sales. The same concept was later launched in Switzerland under a different brand name by another seller, who knew the market well and sold well there [L1158.014].
  • Question for research into a new product market: if a product is not selling in a new country, check whether competitors exist there, whether their store and ads are good, and whether the market accepts this product type. A lack of sales usually comes from missing trust, missing native-language content, or the market not yet knowing the product, which requires educating customers [L1158.042].
  • Competitor products proven in one market can be tested in another by copying the product and store and changing the store currency (for example to euros or Australian dollars) and the language. Stores in weak condition with good ad counts still prove the product sells, so the offer and store can be improved [L1161.010].
  • Example Swiss test: an early dropshipping test in Switzerland with about 200 to 300 USD in ads produced roughly 3,000 CHF in sales. That low-spend result was the signal to test the product further [L0492.010].
  • Test the product's appeal on a shared identity rather than on local details. A product tied to a shared identity, such as an African heritage map shape, can reach a broad audience across many countries because buyers respond to the same feeling of connection [L0502.048].
  • Validate a product idea by testing it in the new market before scaling, and let the new market's hook rate and CTR decide whether it earns more store work [L1108.041].

Supply chain, lead times and delivery

  • A supplier with inventory in the country where you sell is necessary. Cross-continent shipping (for example Australia to Europe) makes costs too high and roughly doubles product prices when shipping is included [L0971.041].
  • Products that are backordered for months should not get paid traffic until stock returns. Genuinely custom made-to-order products with a known build time (for example custom pool tables taking about two months) can still be advertised [L0834.009] (older: 2021).
  • Products with production or delivery times of about 10 weeks or more should get very limited ad spend, because customers rarely buy them without knowing the lead time, so the conversion rate tends to be very low [L0834.010] (older: 2021).
  • Fitness products suffered the most from port backlogs and supplier shortages after 2020. In the 2021 situation, the fitness industry was the niche to avoid for the time being, because stock was thin and a store could not reliably fill orders (older: 2021) [L1090.009].
  • A weak supply chain can make an otherwise promising niche unworkable, so check supplier responsiveness and parcel reliability before committing money [L1441.045].
  • Seasonal products may be copied quickly, and shipping times from suppliers can delay launches. Plan the next seasonal product early [L0352.032].

Older examples and previous-period results

  • A product that worked in 2018 in the kids niche and was picked up for 2020 generated about 10k in first sales after its competitor data was used to build a better offer (older: 2018 to 2020) [L0895.004].
  • Products that take a practical approach, such as a tool-type home product, can be tested in these markets as shown in the earlier sections; treat them as examples, not guarantees [L1171.010].

Holiday and event procedures

Valentine's Day campaign (procedure, as written):

  1. Build a separate store for the Valentine's Day campaign about one month before the holiday.
  2. Prepare around five products with everything needed: creatives, offers and videos, so ads or organic posts can start immediately.
  3. Target men and parents as well as women, since many buyers are not the female partner.
  4. Consider products for couples, such as matching bracelets where each partner wears one part.
  5. Check shipping times and start ads early enough that the product arrives before the date.
  6. Find the winning product at least about 15 days before the holiday [L1469.023].

Q4 product research procedure (as written):

  1. Find products that are trending in Q4 (gifts, winter clothing, personalized jewelry).
  2. Look for similar designs on AliExpress.
  3. Choose designs that look decent and test them in ads.
  4. Repeat in Q1 with fitness products [L1220.022].

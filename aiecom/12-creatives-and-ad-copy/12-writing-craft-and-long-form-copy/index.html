12.12 Writing craft and long-form copy

Voice and stance

  • Write ad copy as if you are telling a friend about an amazing product you just bought. Never seem pushy. Let the customer make their own decision rather than trying to convince them. [L0206.001]
  • Blog copy should sound like a friend giving a recommendation, with short words, short sentences, short paragraphs and plenty of white space. Images also help readers stay engaged. [L0382.026]
  • Use plain, direct language in the register a good salesperson would use face to face. Avoid literary style, slogans and attention-seeking devices, because overly polished writing draws attention away from the offer and triggers resistance. [L1261.062]
  • Before keeping a line, ask whether the same wording would help a salesperson sell the product face to face. If it would not, cut it, because clever wordplay, slogans and performance-style copy pull attention away from the offer. [L1261.139]
  • Inform and persuade; do not entertain. Ads target prospects making a serious spending decision, and readers come for information, not amusement. [L1261.063]
  • Avoid a transactional tone. Lead with what the customer gains and how the brand adds value, because buyers reject overly transactional brands. [L1348.005]
  • Humour and blunt tone suit products with a casual, direct brand voice. They can backfire on premium or high-end products, so match tone to price point and audience. [L0524.048]
  • For a serious product, an informational and scientific tone works better than entertainment. Celebrity or humorous articles brought traffic, but far fewer relevant buyers than direct informational articles did. [L0382.027]
  • Write for new prospects only. Existing customers have already decided and seldom read ads, so spend space on them only if it can be done in the headline. [L1261.068]
  • Include every important claim in each ad. A reader sees an ad once and may need a different argument than the one in the headline, so do not spread claims across a series of ads expecting readers to connect them. [L1261.067]
  • Long, complete copy sells more than short copy for products that require consideration, because the reader decides in one reading and needs every important claim. A complete story is more persuasive than a single slogan or short claim. [L1261.066]
  • Clever, cute or award-seeking ads that are not built around persuading the buyer waste budget. Before approving an ad, ask whether it moves a specific reader to a specific action. [L0340.072]
  • Message clarity comes first. If the reader does not understand the message, no level of evidence or style will persuade. Disorganised or misread ads can also harm the business over the long term. [L0340.047]
  • Build connection through a personal story related to the reader's situation. Use it when you start in an industry to give context, and show relatable attributes of yourself or the brand. This is foundational for the Know, Like, Trust phase. [L0196.054]
  • Make stories about the listener, not the seller. Every audience relates a story back to their own life, so the story should give them a situation they recognise. [L0378.062]
  • Tell the stories you least want to share, especially emotional, vulnerable or embarrassing ones. Readers identify strongly with shared experiences, and these stories tend to produce the highest response. [L0378.061]
  • Procedure for finding the personal stories that sell your product:
    1. Write a list of ten personal moments that were embarrassing, painful or hard to talk about.
    2. Mark the ones you would least want to share publicly.
    3. For each marked story, note the feeling a buyer would have in a similar situation.
    4. Choose the story whose feeling matches the feeling your product addresses.
    5. Write it in the first person, focusing on what happened to you and what you learned.
    6. End by linking the lesson from the story to one step the reader can take. [L0378.115]
  • Show the rough edges of your product. Perfection can make a message feel unbelievable, while honestly named weaknesses build trust when framed as acceptable trade-offs. [L0378.063]
  • Educate before you sell when the audience is unaware. Explain the problem and its consequences before offering the product. Trust signals such as before-and-after photos, money-back claims and real customer proof make the product claim believable. [L0515.018]
  • Brand guideline document for writers, in five sections:
    1. Resource: what the business sells, why it sells it, who the company is, and why the reader should care.
    2. Guidelines: style rules such as short words, short sentences and short paragraphs, written casually as if speaking face to face.
    3. Key factors: required items per article, such as a minimum word count, three links to external sites, and using the keyword a set number of times, plus SEO basics that editors check.
    4. Ground rules: topics writers must avoid, for example health claims that a product cures a disease, which readers and regulators will not accept.
    5. Examples of articles that follow the guidelines. [L0382.025]

Voice guide (Source D method)

Prompt for writing the voice guide, to be used unchanged:

Given high-performing ads and a diagnosed awareness stage, extract the consistent tone and voice across them so every future ad maintains the same character. Write a guide covering the single core reason this voice works for that specific audience stage, the sentence mechanics that create the voice (length, rhythm, contractions, repetition, subordination), recurring word choices and registers that work or break the spell, whether the stance is peer-level or authoritative, how the winning ads open (and what opening patterns dominate), and a one-line test any sentence can pass or fail.

[L0039.004]

  • Pull every rule from patterns actually present in your winning ads, not from generic copywriting advice. Quote short fragments from the ads as proof for every rule stated. [L0039.023]
  • The Voice Guide has six required sections, in this order:
# Section Required content
0 PRIORITY RULE The single core insight about why this voice works for the identified awareness stage, in 3-4 sentences. Everything else defers to it. [L0039.029]
1 SENTENCE-LEVEL RULES Mechanics of sentence length, subordination, declarative tone, contractions, repetition and rhythm, with banned-to-rewritten example pairs pulled from real ads. [L0039.029]
2 VOCABULARY Recurring word registers, and words that would break the spell. [L0039.029]
3 NARRATIVE STANCE Whether the voice is authoritative and outward, or peer-level and confessional. State it as a fixed rule with its failure mode (inverting it). [L0039.029]
4 HOOK RULES Recurring opening moves and entry-point variety. Ban any opener formula that repeats across ads. [L0039.029]
5 THE TEST A one-line gut check applied to every sentence, e.g. "Would a slick copywriter write this? If yes, rewrite it." [L0039.029]

Specificity and proof

  • Lead with the reader's outcome, not the company's factory, staff, size or equipment. Most readers filter every message by what it does for them, and describing the business rarely moves anyone to buy. [L0340.002]
  • Use mental-movie copy: replace vague verbs and nouns with specific visual ones so the reader can see the product in use. Build each sentence in steps: a vague action, then a clearer action, then a specific place and object, then a sensory or sequential detail. Example of the ladder: "Set the water heater to 60 degrees, step into the shower, and feel the first warm rush on your neck." Write new lines in this same way for your own product. [L0340.007]
  • Use positive visual adjectives to describe the product's result, not just its features. Every word that does not create an image lowers the chance that the reader forms a picture of the benefit. [L0340.008]
  • Use colourful language, metaphors, similes and poignant stories to break out of generic marketing. Balance gusto with substance, and avoid flowery language for its own sake so the marketing does not feel like marketing. [L0196.009]
  • Concrete examples persuade more than statistics, because they trigger self-demonstration, where the reader imagines using the product. Lead with stories and examples, and add statistics as support, in a proportion set by the product. [L0340.048]
  • Recommended balance by product type:
Product type Recommended balance
Beer Mostly emotional imagery of people and good times, minimal statistics [L0340.049]
Cars Lead with examples, add statistics on performance, safety or efficiency for the buyers who care [L0340.049]
Laser printers Mostly statistics: paper capacity, resolution, ink life, monthly duty cycle [L0340.049]
Landscaping services Examples about beauty, neighbour perception and convenience [L0340.049]
Gym memberships Photos of attractive members, before-and-after results and testimonials; statistics only as a secondary detail [L0340.049]
  • A rhetorical question is a statement phrased as a question, and it can make the reader think about the claim. Research on persuasion is mixed, but rhetorical questions reliably help retention when used to emphasise a point rather than to argue. [L0340.054]
  • Use "imagine", "what if" and "think about" to stop the reader's scanning and move them into picturing the outcome. These triggers take the reader out of their current state and into thinking about what the copy describes. Examples: "Imagine finishing your run and still feeling like you could go another mile." and "What if your kitchen clean-up took five minutes instead of forty?" [L0396.020]
  • Use definite, specific words such as proper names, concrete nouns and precise verbs instead of vague terms like "someone" or "something". Specific words reduce the work the reader must do to understand the message. [L0340.069]
  • Limit adjectives and describing words, especially when describing a product. [L0925.011]
  • Write the mechanism section in a conversational tone with short sentences and short paragraphs, and use "you" and "your" frequently. Write as if talking to one person, because this works better than formal product text. [L0465.024]
  • Copywriting principles stay the same across formats. Ads, emails and web pages follow the same structural approach and best practices. [L0241.012]
  • Copy strategy target. A strong copywriting target combines clarity, implementability, grounding in reality and a defined timeframe. Model statement:
Using this [email/campaign/sales page/webinar/ad], I will persuade my audience that [named feature] works better than their existing solution for [named goal]. My proof: tangible data, documented examples, and resonance with xyz emotional drivers.

[L0196.008]

Readability

Note: the Source B units below are from 2021-2022 and count as older advice. Where the 2021-2022 advice and the Source D or Source C material differ, both values are shown with the context in which each applies.

  • Readability is measured with the Flesch Reading Ease formula, which runs from 1 to 100, where higher scores are easier. Microsoft Word shows this score in its readability statistics. Check every ad, sales letter, email and landing page before publishing. [L0340.059]
  • Reading Ease score to school grade level:
Flesch score School grade level
0-30 College graduate
30-50 College
50-60 10th to 12th grade
60-70 8th and 9th grade
70-80 7th grade
80-90 6th grade
90-100 5th grade

[L0340.061]

  • Readability targets for ad copy (Flesch Reading Ease framework): average sentence length of about 11 words, mention of people such as he, she or a name about 14 times per 100 words, and 70 to 80 percent of words that are one-syllable words. [L0340.062]
  • Flesch-Kincaid target (2021-2022, older): a score of 6 or 7, which corresponds to a reading level of roughly sixth or seventh grade. Read against the table above, a grade of 6-7 sits at a Flesch Reading Ease of about 70-90. [L0925.014, L0340.061]
  • Sample scores: "John loves Mary" scores 92; "John has a profound affection for Mary" scores 67; a long, wordy version with many hard words scores 32. Each added complexity measurably lowers the score, so simplify words and sentences when a score is too low. [L0340.063]
  • Readability checkers (2021-2022, older): the Flesch-Kincaid Readability Score, the Hemingway App, Grammarly and Readability. The Flesch-Kincaid score is built into Microsoft Word. Hemingway or Grammarly serve the same purpose on a Mac. Grammarly also finds spelling and grammar errors and sentences that could be shortened, and its premium version gives more features. [L0925.013]
  • Other readability formulas exist: the Fog Index estimates the years of education a reader needs, the SMOG formula from 1969 estimates grade level from long words, and the Forcast formula was built for technical forms and manuals. A single score such as Flesch is enough for most ad copy, so use the others only when a specific audience or document type calls for them. [L0340.076]
  • Ads that are reread or studied only rarely need to be simpler, because most readers skim and do not work hard to decode the message. Write for quick scanning. [L0340.073]
  • Write for an average reader with average or lower reading comprehension, not for the most educated person, because most visitors do not have a high command of the language. (2021-2022, older) [L0925.007]

Simplicity and plain language

  • Explain it so a six-year-old can understand. If you cannot explain it simply, you do not understand it well enough. Revise first drafts to simplify and clarify the message. Simple is good; simplistic, which means talking down, is bad. The rule may be broken to prove a point, but use this with caution. [L0196.010, L0196.047]
  • Oversimplifying copy until it sounds condescending is a mistake. Explain in everyday language without losing respect for the reader's intelligence. [L0196.064]
  • Lead by translation: distil complexities into everyday language. Clarity matters most. [L0196.047]
  • Grammar matters, but not that much. Marketing copy is about getting the point across, and core function often overrides form. Casual is fine if the audience is casual. Simplicity matters more than perfect grammar, and formalities distance readers. [L0196.048]
  • Use short, simple words instead of formal substitutes. Plain words like "tired" or "hungry" outperform inflated synonyms, and pompous phrasing reads as complicated and slows persuasion. [L0340.065]
  • Use short sentences. Long sentences are harder to make sense of, and short sentences are easier to read and are read more often. (2021-2022, older) [L0925.005]
  • Express only one thought in each sentence, and let the next sentence carry the next thought. Aim for about 11 words per sentence, varying length so the copy sounds natural. [L0340.066]
  • Write the way you would speak to a single person sitting next to you. Use plain, direct language and avoid stiff, overly formal phrasing. (2021-2022, older) [L0925.008]
  • Use the vocabulary of your target customer. Technical or formal language is appropriate only when the audience is itself a trade or expert group, such as scientists or engineers. For general consumers, avoid supplier and manufacturer jargon and explain terms in simple words. (2021-2022, older) [L0925.009]
  • Use plain, to-the-point wording with no flashy or flowery language. Short sentences that get straight to the point keep the text readable. (2021-2022, older) [L0580.011]
  • Remove flowery language and fluff, meaning any words or sentences that add no value. Check each sentence during editing by asking whether it makes sense and is needed. Brain-dump drafts are fine, but they must be edited. (2021-2022, older) [L0925.010]
  • Format and simplify a product description in this order (2021-2022, older):
    1. Increase the font size to at least 18pt, keeping the same font.
    2. Break long paragraphs into shorter ones of about two lines each.
    3. Shorten overlong sentences and rework the sentence structure where needed, so that sentences are short.
    4. Remove fluff, flowery language and unneeded adjectives.
    5. Replace jargon with plain words.
    6. Test the result with a readability tool such as Flesch-Kincaid, Hemingway or Grammarly, and aim for a score of 6 or 7. [L0925.015]
  • Example: a dense product description with a font size of about 12pt, very long paragraphs and four-line sentences was improved only by raising the font to 18pt and splitting paragraphs into roughly two-line blocks, without changing any words. The text became noticeably easier to read, which shows that formatting alone changes readability. Sentences of four lines still need rewriting. (2021-2022, older) [L0925.016]
  • After setting font size and paragraph length, break long product copy further with bullet points and short headings so the page is easier to scan. Keep every fact and claim the same while doing this. Sentences that still run across several lines need a separate rewrite into shorter sentences. (2021-2022, older) [L0925.017]

Length and layout

  • Use a minimum font size of 18pt for web copy such as product pages, long sales pages and content pages. Never go below 18pt, and 20pt can be used on text-only sales pages. Larger text is also needed because most traffic now comes from mobile devices. (2021-2022, older) [L0925.001]
  • Use one font family throughout a piece of copy. Mixing several fonts in a small section makes the text hard to read. (2021-2022, older) [L0925.004]
  • Keep paragraphs to two lines or fewer most of the time. Occasional longer paragraphs are acceptable. (2021-2022, older) [L0925.002]
  • Short paragraphs create white space, which helps the eye separate elements and scan the page with less strain. This makes the reader more likely to keep reading. Dense blocks of text, as found in printed books, are harder to read on screens. (2021-2022, older) [L0925.003]
  • Limit the length of regular paragraphs in direct-response text to about four or five short sentences, so the page reads quickly. [L0340.067]
  • Copy length should match the amount needed to communicate the message, not a fixed word count. Long copy works when it has quality, and short copy works for simple products that need only a few strong points. Length is a matter of quality, not quantity, and this applies to conversion copy, not SEO copy. (2021-2022, older) [L0925.006]
  • Length rules by context. The sources differ, so apply each value where it fits:
Context Value Source
Paragraphs, general Two lines or fewer most of the time (2021-2022, older) [L0925.002]
Paragraphs, ad copy One to two lines maximum (2021-2022, older) [L0241.011]
Paragraphs, direct response Four or five short sentences maximum [L0340.067]
Thought-command section One or two paragraphs, with short sentences and short lines (2021-2022, older) [L0580.010]
Story section Five or six paragraphs if engaging (2021-2022, older) [L0580.010]
Considered purchase Long, complete copy [L1261.066]
Simple product Short copy with a few strong points [L0925.006]
  • Ad length depends on three factors: how much copy is needed to build desire to its full strength, how much is needed to make the buyer feel comfortable and flattered and see the product fitting into their life, and how much is needed to overcome existing beliefs and make the claims believable. [L0330.091]
  • Format ad copy in short, simple sentences in paragraphs of one to two lines maximum. Make the call to action stand out and visible. Use only one call to action per ad. (2021-2022, older) [L0241.011]

Banned patterns and checklist

Checklist prompt, to be applied before finishing copy:

Compile a checklist of forbidden patterns and language that a writer applies before finishing copy. This includes structural problems (stacking false contrasts, using generic opening formulas, starting with rhetorical devices), the parallel-repetition trap where consecutive lines repeat the same structure to fake emphasis instead of escalating, dead transition phrases, hedging language that weakens conviction, and formatting crutches like excessive emoji or punctuation.

[L0039.006]

The Banned List has five required sections, in this order:

  1. Banned structural patterns. False-contrast stacks such as "It's not X. It's not Y. It's Z." Rhetorical section-openers. Generic "Imagine if" openers. Each comes with a bad example and a fix. [L0039.030]
  2. The parallel repetition stack (highest priority). Repeating the same opener or skeleton across consecutive lines to fake emphasis. Define it with 5-6 prohibited examples and explain why it fails: it is mechanical and signals AI-written copy. Replace it with the Escalation Principle, where each line adds new information or raises stakes. [L0039.030]
    • The Escalation Principle: in ad copy, every consecutive line must add new information or raise stakes. It must never echo or repeat the structure of the previous line. Repeating the same opener or skeleton across consecutive lines creates mechanical, AI-like copy and signals fake emphasis rather than genuine momentum. [L0039.012, L0039.036]
  3. Banned transition and filler phrases. Dead connectives such as "Let's dive in," "Here's the thing," "At the end of the day," and "But wait, there's more." [L0039.030]
  4. Banned qualifiers and hedging. Weak words such as "might," "could potentially," "often" and "usually" are replaced with absolutes plus proof. [L0039.030]
  5. Banned formatting crutches. Emoji overload, exclamation overuse and dense paragraph blocks, each with its own rule. [L0039.030]
  • Repetition for emphasis, Source C view: anaphora (repetition at the start of sequential lines) and epistrophe (repetition at the end) drive home points. Examples include "I have a dream…I have a dream…" (MLK) and "We shall fight…we shall fight…" (Churchill). Use them to break copy into digestible bits, make related information rhythmic, and repeat core arguments. [L0196.035]
  • Repetition, reconciled: the Source D rule and the Source C anaphora advice conflict on the surface. Operator reading: apply the Escalation test to every repeated line. If the repetition adds new content or raises the stakes, it is anaphora. If it only copies the skeleton of the line before it, it is the banned parallel stack. [L0039.012, L0039.036, L0196.035]
  • Rhetorical questions, reconciled: a rhetorical question used to emphasise a point helps retention [L0340.054]. Do not open a section or a piece of copy with a rhetorical device, and do not use a rhetorical section-opener [L0039.006, L0039.030].
  • Avoid FAQ sections in sales letters. They break the story flow, repeat material already covered and remove the seller's control of the conversation. [L1356.069]
  • Avoid "Imagine if" openers and generic opening formulas. [L0039.030]

Editing and proofreading

  • Have every article edited, even if you wrote it yourself. Use native English editors, who cost about $20 to $25 per hour. Let the editor help pick and vet writers, using their judgement of good writing. Then write the headline and meta description, create a custom image, and upload the article with its image and alt tag. [L0382.030]
  • Proofread scripts and angle sheets before publishing. An example template contained spelling errors such as "Emarrassed", "prperly", "malting" and "healty". Errors in ad copy make the brand look careless and reduce trust before the viewer reaches the offer. [L0392.012]
  • Check the balance of reinforcement by rereading the finished copy a few days after writing it, when the wording looks fresh. The passage where a new angle turns into repetition is easier to spot at that distance, and any passage that brings no new angle should be cut. [L0330.102]
  • Read every sentence during editing and ask whether it makes sense and is needed. [L0925.010]
  • Hire a copywriter if your own copywriting skills are weak, and practise regularly to improve conversion rates for ads and landing pages. Copywriting is the most important part of Facebook ads, because the image stops people from scrolling. [L0643.055]
  • Print your drafts (ad variations, email sections, product angles) at a small size. Spread them on a table or floor and group them into piles by theme. Lay the pieces out in the order you want and check the flow. Type the final order into your digital file, and return to the printed pieces whenever the sequence stops working. [L1343.037]

Drafting process

  • When learning copywriting, aim to be better than you were before, not to become the best copywriter in the world. Each additional piece of copy should be a little stronger than the previous one. [L1143.002]
  • Writing copy feels difficult at first, and that is normal for beginners. The difficulty decreases with repeated practice, so keep writing even when early results are weak. [L1143.003]
  • Practice procedure:
    1. Write copy for your products on a regular, ongoing basis.
    2. Test different approaches (different angles, styles or messages) across different times and different areas you write copy for.
    3. Keep applying what you learn consistently in your business, and keep improving over time. [L1143.004]
  • Build copywriting skill through a daily routine: read one piece of sales copy actively with a pen, write one page of copy (it may be discarded, or a hand-copied page from a strong writer), and generate one idea per day by asking "what if". Repetition and pattern recognition are the main drivers of skill. [L1356.002]
  • Active reading means reading with a purpose: looking for what the writer is trying to communicate beneath the text, marking patterns, and noting new lines that can inspire original copy. Passive reading only absorbs the words. [L1356.003]
  • Direct-response copy is a demanding school for copywriters, because a single ad must make the whole sale with no help from displays or salespeople. Techniques that work under those conditions carry over well to general advertising. [L0330.104]
  • Each sentence of the ad, from the second onward, has the same single job: force the reader to read the next sentence. The ad is a chain of sentences that moves the reader from headline to product to purchase. [L0330.016]
  • Direct-response copy must provoke action by first moving the prospect emotionally and only then persuading them intellectually. The emotional work is done in the opening, because the reader gives an ad only seconds before deciding whether to keep reading. [L0756.001]
  • A strong idea is big enough to stir interest, easy to understand, immediately convincing and clearly useful to the reader. Believability matters as much as size. [L0756.004]
  • Build the copy as a sequence of checks on the reader's questions (see the Ten-Question Sequence under Sales letters). [L1365.008]
  • Principle of consistency across channels: the same structural approach and best practices apply to ads, emails and websites. (2021-2022, older) [L0241.012]

Slippery-slide flow

  • Each sentence has one job: make the reader read the next. The first line exists only to make the reader go on to the second line. Test it by asking whether it makes leaving the page almost impossible. [L0330.016, L0378.049]
  • Do not open with your name, credentials or company history unless the piece is a parody or the audience already knows you. Start with the reader's situation or with a question that opens a loop. [L0378.050]
  • Strong first-line pattern: name a specific person or situation, state a concrete change or loss, and leave the outcome open so the reader must continue. Examples: "Maya found out her dog had been eating the wrong food for two years on the day the vet called." and "Nobody on the boat knew the forecast had changed until the wind hit the sails." [L0378.051]
  • Opening with "my name is" or "I've been an expert for years" wastes the most valuable seconds of attention. Replace it with a line about what matters to the reader. [L0378.052]
  • Case: a long-running sales letter opened with a credentials paragraph about the author being a certified specialist with 15 years of experience. The seller removed that opening line, and conversion rose by 22%. The team then tested a new opener built around a question about a hidden problem, which lifted conversion by another 20%. The lesson: the opening should focus on the reader's world, not on the author. [L0378.053]
  • Desire-line pattern: use "I'm going to" plus the feeling or outcome for the reader, never "I want you to" about the seller. Examples: "I'm going to make your Saturday mornings feel unhurried again." and "I'm going to have your kitchen smelling like fresh bread by the time your guests arrive." [L0378.056]
  • Every consecutive line must add new information or raise stakes. Never echo the structure of the previous line. [L0039.012, L0039.036]
  • In longer copy, the lead-in creates curiosity and prepares the reader for the body. The body sells by surfacing benefits and building desire until the reader wants to click through to the landing page. The call to action comes last and can be an offer or a "learn more" click, depending on how aware the audience already is. [L0516.057]
  • For the interest stage, open with a problem the ideal customer can relate to, then agitate it and explain why it happens. The stronger the agitation, the more the reader feels the need for a solution. [L0578.018]
  • Create a character who resembles the ideal customer and tell their problem story, with photos to make it engaging. Readers who see themselves in the story become warm without realising they are being sold to. [L0578.019]
  • In a long-form editorial, end every paragraph or section with a hook that pulls the reader into the next one. Readers who stop mid-story do not reach the product pitch, so the hook matters as much as the headline. [L0578.036]
  • Short-paragraph trick: ask a question or make a quick statement, then answer it in a short follow-up line. Example form: a short question, a one-line answer such as "I thought so.", then a short lead-in such as "Now let me explain…". [L0340.067]
  • Open-loop (bribe): a pledge to disclose information later. Examples: telling the reader that within a moment you will demonstrate precisely how the statements just made hold up completely, or that if they stay with you, you will show how to obtain the advantages you already promised. Launch a fresh loop before the earlier one is resolved so the reader keeps going. [L1356.034]
  • Pattern interrupt: open by showing something the reader has not seen in other ads, such as an unusual image, document or opening line, to grab attention. Check what competitors use as pattern interrupts and avoid repeating them. [L1356.030]
  • Payoff lines follow a headline and give the reader slightly more detail on the headline's topic without revealing the key information. A payoff holds attention while the copy moves toward the big promise, and the big promise should be reached as soon as possible. [L1356.031]
  • The lead should contain one big idea that can be summarised in a single sentence (the copy's "soul" or "golden thread"). Everything in the headline, lead and body must tie back to that idea, and content that deviates from it should be removed. The soul can be vague in the first draft and sharpened during writing. [L1356.028]
  • Express the reader's situation in plain steps and keep one thought per sentence so each sentence moves the reader forward. [L0340.066]

Opening lines and lead length

  • Lead length, sources differ (use the one that fits the format):
    • The opening few hundred words (roughly 100 to 600 words in a long-form sales letter) decide whether the rest of the copy is read. A weak lead loses the prospect no matter how strong the later sections are. [L0756.002]
    • The lead is the densest part of the copy and typically covers roughly the first 500 words, ranging from 2 to 7 pages in long-form letters. If the proof for the lead's claims has not started by page 7, the lead is likely too long and should be shortened. [L1356.026]
  • In long-form sales copy, roughly 80% of the emotional impact comes from the first 20% of the text, so the opening deserves the most drafting and testing effort. Revising the rest rarely fixes a weak opening. [L0756.053]
  • Opening test procedure:
    1. Read only the opening of the sales copy, without the rest.
    2. Ask whether it excites the reader and creates anticipation to keep reading.
    3. If it does not, rewrite the opening before editing anything else, because a weak opening usually means the whole promotion will underperform. [L0756.054]
  • Two otherwise identical sales letters that differ only in their opening lead can produce response rates that differ by a factor of two or three, for example 0.5% against double or triple that. Test whole leads rather than small details to find the largest gains. [L0756.055]
  • Dominant-emotion leads: once a control winner is found for a rollout, write up to six cover tests, each with a different lead type, including benefit-led and dominant-emotion leads. Run heads-up A/B splits between the strongest results. In recent heads-up splits of this kind, dominant-emotion (indirect) leads won more often than benefit leads. [L0756.056]
  • Listing every feature and benefit in one piece of copy and hoping one lands dilutes the message and splits the reader's attention. Keep one main benefit and one core emotion per lead, and cut the rest. [L0756.060]

Sales letters

Structure

  • Structure a long-form direct-response sales letter in four sections, in this order: (1) headline, (2) lead, (3) body copy, (4) offer. Keep the order, because rearranging these sections makes the copy harder to follow and loses the attention of busy, skeptical readers. [L1356.019]
  • Write the lead in this order:
    1. Capture attention, via a pattern interrupt or the headline.
    2. Expand on the headline with a payoff that gives more detail without giving away the offer.
    3. Establish credibility with a single line, such as a quote from an authority or a track record.
    4. State the one big promise as early as possible.
    5. Open a loop that promises information revealed later in the copy. [L1356.027]
  • Credibility in the lead can be a single line (for example, a quote from an authority figure or a credential at the start of the letter), since full credibility is saved for the body. The quote or credential should make the claim seem inevitable or predictable. [L1356.032]
  • The lead does not need to reveal the product or offer. In many long-form letters the product is introduced only in the final pages of the lead, after the guru or seller is introduced. The lead's job is to build hype around an opportunity or to illustrate a severe problem. The body then proves the points. [L1356.029]
  • Body copy: repeat this sequence for each claim: (1) address an objection, (2) make a claim that answers that objection, (3) give proof, (4) describe the benefit. Keep to this template for body copy, unlike the emotion-based approach used elsewhere. [L1356.035]
  • Find objections by generating them from the four core emotions and their alter egos. For each of New/Only, Easy/Anybody, Safe/Predictable and Big/Fast, ask what a skeptical reader would question, e.g. "Why should I buy from you?", "What if I don't have money?", "How do I know this will work?", "How fast will I see money?". Common objections include "Why should I act now?", "Who are you?", "How does it work?", "Is this risky?", "How much does it cost?" and "What if it doesn't work?". [L1356.036]

Body sections (patterns)

  • Guru or credibility section: follows the lead and marks the transition from lead to body. It answers "Who are you?" and "Why should I listen to you?" by showing photos with credible people, appearances on TV or news networks, or a track record of success or accurate predictions. Case studies and testimonials are usually saved for the offer. [L1356.044]
  • Discovery story: explains how the seller found the opportunity, answering "How do you know about this?" and conveying Only. Use a secret source, an elite network or a chance event to show the information is not available to the general public, supported with proof that the relationship exists. [L1356.045]
  • Key insight: the single piece of information the reader must believe for the opportunity to seem real. Back it with "signs" that the event is coming (e.g. stocks of related companies quietly rising because insiders are buying), which conveys Safe and Predictable. [L1356.046]
  • Layman's explanation: describes how the opportunity works in simple words anyone could repeat, like two people talking at a bar. It answers "How does it work?" and should convey the New or Easy emotion, e.g. three simple steps plus a patented or new technology. [L1356.047]
  • Technical explanation: uses dense jargon and scientific detail to reassure the reader that the seller understands the mechanism, even though the reader does not follow it. This conveys Safe and Predictable through expertise rather than understanding. [L1356.048]
  • "Better Than" section: compares the offer to the most popular item currently in the market (e.g. FAANG stocks or Bitcoin) to show it outperforms it. The claim should be backed by a quote or figure from a news source, a chart or comparison, and a second proof point. The section should convey Big, optionally paired with Safe. [L1356.042]
  • "Last Time Something Like This Happened" section: uses a historical case with a subhead such as "The last time something like this happened was over 25 years ago!", tells the story, and says this time the opportunity is much bigger. The historical pattern conveys Predictability. A case study with a famous name, such as Gary Halbert's "Water Ad" example, can make the pattern strong. [L1356.043]

Ten-question sequence

Structure the letter around the reader's questions, in this order:

  1. How is this different from everything else I've seen? State the new opportunity and make sure it is truly unique, since without uniqueness the idea is not worth testing.
  2. What's in it for me? Open with a big promise tied to the reader's key desires, because the reader must see the benefit within seconds.
  3. How do I know this is real? Add early proof such as historical charts, testimonials or third-party quotes, wrapped in a story.
  4. What's holding me back? Reveal the real reason past solutions failed, found by reverse-engineering the answer to step 1, so the reader is not blamed.
  5. Who or what is to blame? Name a common enemy that the audience already distrusts or resents, drawn from their existing beliefs.
  6. Why now? Create urgency with an either-or choice and a real deadline or event that raises the stakes.
  7. Why should I trust you? Establish credibility through an "I've been in your shoes" storyline, a Robin Hood storyline or an expert storyline.
  8. How does it work? Explain the new mechanism logically, anchored in a belief the reader already holds, and wrap it in a story with credible sources.
  9. How can I get started? Present a no-brainer offer with a large gap between stacked value and final price, plus bonuses and a guarantee.
  10. What do I have to lose? Close with a push-pull: let the prospect choose freely among options, then remind them what they lose by doing nothing.

[L1365.008]

Offer and close

  • Use the P.S. in sales letters to restate the offer, repeat the contact details and push for action. Readers often read the P.S. before the body copy, so it should contain the strongest closing line. [L0340.070]
  • If a P.S. is used after the sign-off, summarise the offer briefly, like an abbreviated lead. [L1356.069]
  • Close with one strong, one-time-only offer. [L0756.075]
  • Sales-letter build procedure (lift note and opening):
    1. Write a lift note that asks a question which inverts the promise, then support it with a single-sentence story that validates it.
    2. Open the sales letter with one statement that implies the core promise.
    3. Support the statement with a short bulleted list of facts.
    4. Validate the statement with your own result.
    5. Close with one strong, one-time-only offer. [L0756.075]

Advertorials and native-style long-form

  • An advertorial is an article-style pre-sell page that looks like editorial content and leads to a product page, often as a story, listicle or "how I did this" narrative. It works as a psychological bridge: visitors get drawn into the story before they see the price, so they are less likely to bounce straight from a product page. It is time-consuming to produce, so it is most worth testing for problem-solving products. [L0691.002]
  • Test with an advertorial right away if the product suits the format, rather than only a product page, when the goal is to avoid wasting test budget on a weak funnel. The trade-off is more production time. Teams that prefer fast testing may skip this step. [L0691.004]
  • Advertorial research and writing method:
    1. Find advertorials that already sell in your niche or an adjacent niche, using search on YouTube and similar platforms.
    2. Copy the full advertorial text and paste it into a Google Sheet.
    3. Go through it paragraph by paragraph and note what each paragraph says and what job it does in the sequence.
    4. Rewrite the text for your own product, adapting the same paragraph roles, and then test it. [L0691.001]
  • Editorial page steps, with a 70/30 split of about 70% information and story and the final 30% for the product pitch:
    1. Plan the page around the 70/30 rule.
    2. Write the headline first, making it benefit-driven and attention-grabbing with a bold claim, curiosity or news angle.
    3. Interest: open with a problem the ideal customer can relate to, then agitate it and explain why it happens.
    4. Desire: explain why other solutions fail, present your product as a new approach, and add social proof with customer reviews, photos, results over time and data.
    5. Action: introduce the product and place a clear call to action that leads to the product page.
    6. Add photos throughout the text to keep the reader engaged. [L0578.030]
  • Native-style long-form ad pattern: a large primary text that reads like an article. Structure: a bold headline, a first-person story of a small symptom that grew slowly, an ordinary check-up that found nothing alarming, a turning point where the person tried the product, then the product as the answer, and a disclaimer at the end. It works because the story earns attention at the top of the funnel, and the disclaimer keeps it compliant. Two new examples: "I Kept Losing Track of My Running Times for Two Years. A Coach Found the Cause in One Session." and "My Desk Routine Had Stopped Working by Spring. A Physiotherapist Showed Me What I Was Missing." [L1164.061]

Direct mail and envelope techniques

  • Mail-order ads must make the full sale on a single run, so they pack the greatest number of intensification devices into one long ad. They are often frozen and repeated after three to six months. National advertising keeps the product in front of the public constantly, so it runs far more often, and its product lifespans are much longer. [L0330.083]
  • Catalog-style direct mail: open the first paragraph of each product ad with several very short sentences of two to four words, which pulls the eye down the page and keeps readers moving toward the offer. Use the same opening rhythm across product ads to keep the reader moving through the page. [L0340.075]
  • Operator note on the rhythm rule: the repeated opening rhythm applies across separate product ads. Inside one ad, every consecutive line must still escalate and must not echo the line before it. [L0340.075, L0039.012]
  • Envelope technique: no knowledge units were supplied for this topic.

Core principle: one reader, one action

  • Direct response copy is a writing style built to make a reader take one specific action right away. Its formula is: name the reader's problem, show the product as the answer, and end with one clear instruction. It works because every sentence pushes toward that one action rather than informing for its own sake. Example in the same pattern: Tired of cracked phone screens after one drop? This case absorbs the impact and ships in 48 hours. Tap to see the protection. [L1460.002] (older advice, 2021-22)
  • Each piece of copy must aim at exactly one action, chosen by its place in the funnel. On a product page the action is adding to cart and buying. In an ad it is clicking through to the website. In an email it is visiting the website. [L1460.003, L1460.014] (older advice, 2021-22)
  • Adding a second competing action to the same piece weakens the first. [L1460.014] (older advice, 2021-22)
  • Ads that try to make the viewer buy directly fail when the ad does not allow purchase on the spot, because the viewer must first visit the site. The ad should only earn the click, and the sale happens later on the site. [L1460.004] (older advice, 2021-22)
  • Ads do not sell the product; they sell the click. A few lines of text cannot sell a product effectively, so the ad only needs to arouse enough curiosity that the person wants to visit the page. [L1507.002] (older advice, 2021-22)
  • Words in emails, search text ads and social ads all count as copy and must be written with one defined action in mind. This includes Google search text ads and Facebook ads. [L1460.011] (older advice, 2021-22)
  • The purpose of ecommerce copy is to move the reader to the action the business wants, which in most cases is buying the product. Every headline and text block should be judged by whether it pushes toward that purchase. [L1493.004] (older advice, 2021-22)
  • Today's online copywriting methods largely come from direct-response text marketing developed from the early 1900s through the 1950s and 1960s. Before TV and the internet, mail and newspaper advertising sold almost everything through text alone, which forced writers to become very skilled. [L1523.002]

Voice and reader focus

  • The word "you" matters more than any other word in a sales letter, so the copy should focus on what the reader wants rather than on the seller. People are interested in their own goals, so the letter should speak to them directly. [L1365.012]
  • Copy written as one person speaking directly to one reader, using I, you and me, feels personal and converts better than copy written as if addressing a crowd. A byline with a real person's name strengthens this feel. [L1366.014]
  • Personal first-person invitation (pattern): rewrite a crowd-directed corporate line as a note from one person who would like to meet the reader again and show the new product.
    • Impersonal form: We invite all customers to visit our booth.
    • Personal form: You may remember me from the last show. I would love to see you at the next one and show you the new machine.
    • The personal version creates warmth and a direct invitation. [L1366.080, L1366.015]
  • Honesty shows through the copy. Hidden flaws or evasive claims are noticed by readers, while a candid tone about the product builds trust and makes the buying environment feel real. [L1366.064]
  • Creative and personal writing shows through the copy. Readers sense the personality of the writer, so write in the voice that represents the company rather than mimicking someone else. [L1366.065]
  • Logic does not have to be strictly correct to work, as long as the phrase conveys the intended feeling clearly. A reassuring phrase about returns can sound odd when read literally and still be effective, because it signals a prompt and caring company. [L1366.017]
  • The goal of all copy is to get the reader to exchange their hard-earned money for the product or service, so every choice in the copy serves that exchange. [L1366.059]

Buying environment and layout

  • The first paragraphs of an ad set a buying environment, and layout and copy tone must match the price and nature of the product. A premium product needs a refined, confident look and tone, while a bargain needs a busy, deal-focused look and tone. [L1366.012]
  • In print, the copywriter controls the buying environment completely through layout and word choice, unlike a store where setting it up costs thousands. Use this control on purpose instead of leaving the mood to chance. [L1366.013]

Word choice and emotion

  • Single words carry emotional associations, so word choice can change the reader's feeling even when the facts stay the same. Choose the more evocative word when it is accurate, for example cottage instead of motel office for a small building, and do not change the facts to suit a mood. [L1366.019]
  • Word-level emotion choice (pattern): when the facts allow it, choose the more evocative word, such as cottage instead of motel office for a small building, without changing the facts. Test single-word swaps on high-traffic claims, because one swapped word can change response measurably. [L1366.081]
  • Test case: in a mail order offer, replacing repair with fix raised response by about 20 percent in the original test. Test single-word swaps on high-traffic claims, because small wording changes can move response measurably. [L1366.020, L1366.082]
  • Readers respond more to vivid, emotional description than to a dry specification list. People enjoy emotion at any time but must be pushed to study facts, so put life and occasionally a light touch into the copy to stir the reader out of indifference. [L1369.057]
  • Replace long lists of points with a few vivid images the reader can see. Long sequences of firstly, secondly and thirdly put readers to sleep and lose the attention you have built. [L1369.058]
  • Sensory description (pattern): describe the product through a few concrete sights, sounds or textures the reader already knows, rather than through a list of specifications. The reader pictures the product in his own life, which makes the benefit feel real and memorable. [L1369.119]
    • Example one: The lamp throws a warm, even pool of light across the desk, and the dial turns without a flicker.
    • Example two: The jacket feels heavy and soft in the hand, and it closes with a sound that tells you the zip will hold through a wet walk home. [L1369.119]
  • Build a word picture in the reader's mind: [L1369.059]
    1. Pick a familiar object or scene the reader already pictures. [L1369.059]
    2. Add one point of interest at a time, in logical order, starting with the framework and then the parts that matter. [L1369.059]
    3. Stop when the reader can build the complete picture himself. [L1369.059]
  • Use sensory language and big-impact phrases that let the reader see and feel the benefit, such as describing a cold, dark office late at night when selling a fast computer. Build a card file of vivid phrases from advertising, novels and television, and use reference works of descriptive words when stuck. [L1370.049]
  • Sell an emotional story first and back it up with logic. Buyers decide on emotion and then justify with logic. Cold, purely factual letters rarely work, so the letter needs an enthusiastic voice to compensate for being ink on paper. [L1370.052]
  • Boredom is the most serious sin in marketing copy, so find something to be excited about even for dull products. Excitement can come from the feeling of owning the product, the enjoyment of the money or time it saves, or from exaggerated and passionate language that the reader will still find moderate. [L1370.053]
  • Passion exercise: write a letter that tries to persuade someone to do something risky but exciting, using vivid sensory detail, bold promises and the removal of each objection as you go. Then carry that energy into the real offer. Example for a hardware retailer: Picture the first morning your shop opens with the new displays up. Your customers walk in and see exactly what you promised them. Here is how to get that morning ready in ten days. [L1370.055]

Readability and simplicity

  • Write sales copy in very simple language, keeping sentences and words basic and introducing one new idea at a time. Simple wording makes it easier for a reader on a phone to follow the argument and reach the offer. [L1365.055]
  • Make copy readable with short, punchy sentences, sentence fragments, short paragraphs of three or four sentences, everyday language and no jargon. Writing that sounds like speech moves the reader along, and grammar rules matter less than momentum. [L1370.048]
  • Write at a simple reading level. The target audience is often less literate or less patient than the writer assumes, a trap called the sophistication trap. Assuming sophisticated readers costs money, and the simpler approach is safer because even TV commercials are pitched at about a sixth-grade reading level. [L1370.059]
  • Reduce the perceived effort of your offer by stating the time it takes in plain terms, such as 15 minutes a day. A small, specific time commitment makes the reader more willing to start. [L1369.066]

Opening and first page

  • Answer the first two questions, what is new and what is in it for the reader, on the first page. Readers often browse on phones and look for a reason to leave. If the prospect does not see their benefit quickly, they will leave and return to what they were doing. [L1365.011]
  • Structure the first paragraph as an extended headline that sells the reader on reading the letter, and only then sell the offer. Cut any preamble, such as apologies for not writing a personal letter or long explanations of why the mail matters. [L1370.044]
  • Write the headline so that it makes the reader feel the letter is welcome. [L1370.040]

Core belief and planning

  • To find the core of a promotion, ask: what is the single most important thing the reader must believe? Write the answer as one sentence before drafting anything, because it works as the mission that every later section must support. [L1365.046]
  • Planning sequence for a promotion idea: [L1365.044]
    1. Write the one belief for the promotion idea. [L1365.044]
    2. Answer the first seven questions with a couple of lines each. [L1365.044]
    3. Map out the whole promotion, which takes about 5 to 10 minutes. [L1365.044]
    4. Do the research the promotion needs. [L1365.044]
    5. Write each section in order, starting with the sections the map has already defined. [L1365.044]
  • Full sales letter sequence, in order: [L1370.040]
    1. Research the buyer thoroughly with the ten questions and connect with their priorities. [L1370.040]
    2. Use the offer yourself or talk to its users, and list every feature with its benefit, sorted by importance. [L1370.040]
    3. List every objection and identify the flaws in the offer, then decide how to admit them. [L1370.040]
    4. Plan delivery: postage class, envelope design, and whether it is a letter, postcard or self-mailer. [L1370.040]
    5. Write the headline that makes the reader feel the letter is welcome. [L1370.040]
    6. Build the reading path with subheads, bold text and short paragraphs so skimmers get the gist. [L1370.040]
    7. Minimise price impact with comparisons, value stacking and payment framing. [L1370.040]
    8. Use the proven persuasion techniques: scarcity or intimidation, ROI, ego, a strong guarantee and a story. [L1370.040]
    9. Write a first draft without editing or worrying about length. [L1370.040]
    10. Rewrite for strategy, adding repetition, a yes sequence and page-ending teasers. [L1370.040]
    11. Rewrite for style with short sentences, big-impact words and personality. [L1370.040]
    12. Answer every question and objection. [L1370.040]
    13. Create urgency with a deadline, premium or limited availability. [L1370.040]
    14. Add a P.S. that restates the offer. [L1370.040]
    15. Run the checklist. [L1370.040]
    16. Add graphic devices and build the layout with the copywriter involved. [L1370.040]
    17. Rewrite for passion and edit aggressively for clarity. [L1370.040]
    18. Compare the draft with strong examples. [L1370.040]
    19. Pretest it aloud and with real customers at no cost. [L1370.040]
    20. Prepare the final version, mock it up, cool off for a day, get second opinions, do a final review, then print and mail. [L1370.040]

Flow and the slippery slide

  • The first seven questions should be answered within the first third of the letter. By then the reader should be emotionally sold. Only after the emotional sale is complete should the copy turn to logic. [L1365.030]
  • Once the reader understands how the mechanism delivers the result, they are pre-sold and ready for the offer. Present the offer only then, because the reader has already accepted that the new opportunity is the key to what they want. [L1365.033]
  • Story formula (pattern): [Background fact] and [supporting context], BUT [conflict or problem], THEREFORE [result or conclusion]. Use and to set up background and momentum, but to create tension, and therefore to deliver the conclusion.
    • Why it works: stories are how people process information, and the but creates the tension that keeps the reader reading. Proof and quotes should therefore be wrapped in this structure instead of being listed. [L1365.016]
    • Example 1: Reorders had been climbing for two years, and the team celebrated, BUT the newest customers had stopped coming back after month two, THEREFORE we rebuilt the onboarding sequence around month-two results. [L1365.016]
    • Example 2: Our waitlist grew every week and the signups looked healthy, BUT more than half of those people never bought, THEREFORE the first purchase offer now appears on the confirmation page. [L1365.016]
  • Each sentence should only have to earn the next sentence, so the reader moves through the copy like a train gathering speed. Once the reader is moving, the next lines become easier to read, which is why short opening sentences work. [L1366.004]
  • Slippery slide rule: every element and sentence must pull the reader forward so strongly that they keep reading to the end. Once a reader gets past about 25 percent of an ad, they are likely to read the whole ad, so the goal is to get them past that point. [L1366.006]
  • Seed of curiosity (pattern): end a paragraph with a very short line that promises the next paragraph holds something worth reading, so readers keep going through slower sections. Use it sparingly, because overuse makes copy feel manipulative. [L1366.008, L1366.074]
    • Examples: The catch is hidden in paragraph four. Most buyers miss the one detail that changes everything, so keep reading. [L1366.008]
    • Most buyers miss the detail that changes everything. Keep reading. [L1366.074]
    • The cheapest fix takes less than a minute. Here is why it works. [L1366.074]
  • Hidden payoff (pattern): state a real benefit near the top of the ad and say it will be revealed later, so the reader has to read the full copy to get it. The payoff should be real and should arrive at the end. [L1366.009, L1366.073]
    • Examples: Your refund question gets answered in the final section, after the warranty details. [L1366.009]
    • The cheapest way to run this setup is explained at the bottom of the page. [L1366.009]
    • The fastest way to cut your phone bill is in the last paragraph, and most readers skip it. [L1366.073]
    • The one setting that doubles battery life is explained after the list of features. [L1366.073]
  • Never end a page with a complete sentence, since that gives the reader permission to stop. End each page mid-sentence or at an exciting phrase, and add teaser copy at the bottom of the page that works like a headline for the next page. [L1370.046]
  • Copy flows like a one-way chain: each block must anticipate the question raised by the one before it. A reader who stops at any question will not reach the order request, so check each transition. [L1366.050, L1366.124]
  • Create a double readership path. Impulsive readers skim headlines, subheads, photos with captions and boxed short paragraphs, while analytical readers read every word and want facts, figures, charts and statistics. Write the skim path so it carries the full offer, then use it as signposts along the full text for analytical readers. [L1370.045]
  • Repetition in one letter (pattern): restate the core promise in six different forms: as a plain statement, as an example, as a short true story, as a testimonial, as a quote from an expert or customer, and as a numbered summary. Repetition is one of the two ways people accept new ideas, the other being shock, so vary the wording on each pass. [L1370.041]

Length

  • Copy length rule: copy should be long enough to create the buying environment, build interest, relate to the buyer's needs and make the full case, and no longer. Judge length by whether the reader takes the requested action, not by word count. [L1366.054, L1366.089]
  • Higher-priced products need more copy to justify the price and create the need. A low price that looks like a clear value needs less copy, while a low price that seems too cheap to be credible needs more copy to build trust. [L1366.090]
  • Short copy works only when the photos, the price and the medium already do the environment-building work, as in a catalog with strong photography and low prices. Otherwise write more copy. [L1366.091, L1366.057]
  • Readers read long copy when they care intensely about the subject. Interest drops once the reader has mastered the topic, so long copy pays off mainly for a target reader who is still learning and deeply involved. [L1366.092, L1366.058]
  • The more unusual a product, the more copy it needs to relate it to the user, build the buying environment and explain its new features. Mail order suits unusual products that need that much copy, because most retail settings do not allow it. [L1366.056]
  • Long copy works when it targets qualified buyers, because most readers will glance and discard, and that is acceptable. Write for the buyer, not for the non-buyer, and add pages until the buyer has every fact needed to act. Some letters grew from one or two pages to many and got better results with each addition. [L1370.042]
  • Research cited by the author found that readership of industrial ads with extensive text drops sharply for copy under about 50 words, and drops much less between 50 and 500 words. Do not shorten copy just to fit a format or page limit; write to tell the whole story. [L1370.043]

Drafting process

  • Practice produces the largest gains in copywriting. Write every day in small forms such as letters, postcards, articles for local papers and ads, because volume builds skill faster than study alone. [L1366.045]
  • A first draft is expected to be weak. Do not judge the draft by how it reads, because the first draft is an emotional release of everything you want to say and the real quality comes from editing. [L1366.046]
  • When drafting, get the copy out first and ignore spelling and grammar, then switch to logic when editing. Write with the intuitive, emotional side of the mind and edit with the analytical side. [L1366.047]
  • Write the first version as a print direct response ad, even when the final product will be sold on the internet, in a catalog or on TV. The print version reveals the core selling points that can then be used in every other medium. [L1366.060, L1366.093]
  • Use a checklist as a mid-course correction in the writing process, scanning the draft against every formula, technique and strategy in the playbook, because drafts drift from good practice as they are revised. [L1370.061]

Editing

  • In editing, refine copy so that it expresses exactly what you intend with the fewest words. You can rearrange sentences, cut words that add little, substitute stronger words, or add words for clarity, but keep the same emotional feel. [L1366.048]
  • Edit aggressively to clarify, not only to shorten. Remove any word or phrase that does not advance, strengthen or reinforce the core sales story. Edit the draft in several passes, setting it aside for hours between passes, because the shorter version usually follows naturally from clearer wording. [L1370.056]
  • Compare your draft with several strong examples, not just one. Check whether your text flows as smoothly, speaks to the audience as well, is structurally sound, is as easy to understand, and moves the reader to act as effectively. [L1370.057]

Objections, admissions and proof

  • List every objection, fear, doubt and excuse a prospect could have for not responding, writing each one on its own card. Then answer each objection in the copy, because a letter that leaves an objection unanswered lets the reader stop at that doubt. [L1370.007]
  • Damaging admission (pattern): openly state the real drawbacks of the offer and who it is not for, then show why the trade-off is still worth it for the right buyer. This raises credibility because readers trust a seller who admits weak points.
    • Example: This is not the cheapest option, and it takes two weeks to ship. If you need it tomorrow, a local store is the better choice, but if you want a tested product that lasts five years, this one is built for you.
    • Second example: We do not offer same-day installation or phone support after 6 pm. If you can plan a week ahead and want a repair done right the first time, you will be glad you waited. [L1370.008]

Closing

  • Use a P.S. after every sales letter, because many readers look at the end first. A P.S. that restates the offer or promise acts as a second headline. Do not consider the letter finished until a P.S. is written. [L1370.060]

Envelopes: attention and first impression

  • Assume recipients sort their mail over a wastebasket and decide in seconds. In business settings an assistant may screen the envelopes first. The envelope must therefore earn the open before the letter is read. [L1370.014]
  • Never be half-way in envelope design. If the envelope reveals that the mail is business or sales material, use the whole face as a billboard with teaser copy and a strong image. Choose the disguised look or the billboard look deliberately, not a mix of both. [L1370.016]
  • Whatever the envelope promises must be delivered inside, such as a Personal label followed by something personal in the mailing. Unfulfilled envelope promises destroy the credibility of everything enclosed, while fulfilled promises increase it. [L1370.015]
  • Sneak-up envelope (pattern): a plain white envelope with no business name, only an address, a person's name as return address, no teaser text, individual or ink-jet addressing and a live stamp, so it looks like personal correspondence. The author estimated about a 98 percent chance of delivery and opening with first-class postage. Use it when prospects are cold and you want the envelope read before it is judged. [L1370.012]
  • Avoid gummed white address labels, which look like junk mail. Individually addressed or ink-jet addressed envelopes produce better response. The author paid home workers to hand-address envelopes because testing showed the lift justified the cost. [L1370.011]
  • A mailing to doctors that used the individual doctor's name and address as return address, with no company name, pulled up to 300 percent better than the same letter in envelopes showing the company. The author reports this from his own tests with a professional audience. [L1370.017]
  • Hybrid envelope (tool): combine personalisation with teaser copy. For example, use a window envelope showing a personalised letter headline with the recipient's name and city, plus printed teaser text on the front and testimonials on the back. Personalised headlines, body copy and enclosures are now easy to produce with printing technology. [L1370.018]
  • Use an attention-getter on the envelope only with a clear purpose. On third-class mail a catch-phrase pulls readers into the envelope, but on first-class mail a plain corner card usually pulls better because it looks like a personal letter. Test both versions against each other. [L1369.070]
  • If you use a catch-phrase or attention-getter on the outside of an envelope, send the mailing third class to save about half the postage. Otherwise the postage cost of a first-class letter is wasted on an envelope that already looks like an advertisement. [L1369.071]
  • Envelope picture (pattern): place a picture linked to the recipient's work or organization on the envelope, with the recipient's name line replaced by a care-of line for his company. A picture of a person he recognizes from his trade flatters him and draws favorable attention before he reads the letter.
    • Example one: a photo of a shop owner from a trade magazine, addressed as care of the owner's store and street address.
    • Example two: a photo of a plant manager from an industry newsletter, addressed care of the plant's name and city. [L1369.072]
  • When a mailing carries a named person's name as the return address, enclose a small notepad-sized note signed by that person. The note says the information helped them and asks the reader to go through everything enclosed. This note is the promise the envelope makes, delivered inside, so the reader trusts the rest of the mailing. [L1370.121]

Postage and delivery

  • Third-class bulk mail can have a non-delivery rate of 10 to 30 percent according to older postal audits, and the author believed it was worse. Bulk mail is only justified when the economics cannot support first-class postage, since non-delivery can wreck a campaign without the sender ever knowing. [L1370.009] (older advice)
  • Use first-class postage with a real stamp instead of a meter imprint to improve delivery odds, because postal workers are least likely to discard mail they believe the recipient expects. Automated stamp-affixing machines are available for mailers who cannot hand-stamp. [L1370.010]
  • Stamps and small postage details (historical tests, US mail of the 1930s):
    • Two red 2-cent stamps pulled more replies than one 4-cent stamp, and one red 2-cent stamp pulled better than two green 1-cent stamps. Test stamp color and denomination before a large mailing. [L1369.082]
    • A brown 1.5-cent stamp looked like a 4-cent stamp and pulled better than a green 1-cent stamp, but no better than two half-cent stamps. Postage stamps also pulled better than metered postage or postal indicia in the same tests. [L1369.083]
    • Printing the 1-cent metered postage in black ribbon may outpull a 2-cent stamp or meter in some post offices. Small details such as ink color and postage type are worth testing, but only after the core letter already works. [L1369.084]
  • Fold, indents, postage and stamp color are minor details that matter only after the opening, description, argument and close are working. Adding them to a weak letter will not produce results. [L1369.085]

Gatekeepers

  • When a gatekeeper such as an assistant screens mail for an executive, send the sales package this way: [L1370.125]
    1. Place the sealed sales package inside a plain kraft envelope. [L1370.125]
    2. Address the kraft envelope personally to the decision-maker, with no company name on the front. [L1370.125]
    3. Put the sender's personal name in the return-address corner. [L1370.125]
    4. Send it by overnight courier, placed inside the courier company's outer envelope. This makes the piece look like a personal, urgent item rather than advertising. [L1370.125]

Letter presentation and folds

  • Fold the letter so that only the salutation and first line show when the reader picks it up, using a military-style fold. This fold raised orders by as much as 10 percent in tests. [L1369.080]
  • Indent the paragraph that carries your most important point. In tests, indentation drew more attention than underlining or all-capital text. [L1369.081]

Executive mailings and grabbers

  • For executive and business-owner mailings, use superior paper and an envelope with texture or watermark, keep enclosures to a minimum, and place brochures and order forms in a second sealed package inside the main envelope. Prestige words such as exclusive, select, superior, charter member, prominent, worthwhile, yield and individual membership raise perceived status. [L1370.029]
  • A grabber is a physical object attached to or sent with the letter to create curiosity, such as a coin, tea bag, miniature object or a trade-show invitation in a bottle. Pairing the grabber with a message that explains why the object was sent gives a reason to read the letter. [L1370.030]
  • Cases: a seminar operator mailed a miniature trash can with peanuts and a letter framed as a squirrel calling the prospect nuts for not responding, with the point that not responding equals throwing a large sum of money away. Separately, a trade-show mailing sent a bottled treasure-map invitation with a compass, magnifier and highlighter, and produced a 75 percent increase in targeted traffic compared with prior years. [L1370.031]
  • Consumer mail needs bold, colourful and attention-grabbing pieces that give the reader something to do in every section, such as tear-off stamps, scratch-off cards, tokens or stickers. Colour is practically essential in consumer mailings, and colour photographs help. [L1370.032]

Folders and multi-panel mailings

  • A folder works like a magazine advertisement and needs the same parts: an illustration and headline to win attention, an opening that arouses interest, a clear description, a logical argument, a clincher, and a coupon or card that makes ordering easy. [L1369.073]
  • A circular enclosed with a letter adds about 25 to 33 percent to the pulling power of almost any letter. The enclosure should carry the illustrated detail and the letter should carry the argument. [L1369.074]
  • Layout of a multi-panel mailing folder: [L1369.120]
    1. Front panel: place a headline and illustration that win attention on their own. [L1369.120]
    2. Opening pages: use a story or question that arouses interest in the offer. [L1369.120]
    3. Center pages: describe the offer and present the logical argument with its key features. [L1369.120]
    4. Last page: place proof, such as a letter from a third party who verifies the claims. [L1369.120]
    5. Include a card or coupon that makes ordering a single action. [L1369.120]

Further details

Build opening-line ideas from published books:

  1. Go to a physical bookstore and pick up novels from authors who write in your reader's genre.
  2. Write down the first line of each book and note the hook it uses, such as danger, a question, or a loss.
  3. Group the lines by hook type and see which types appear most often.
  4. Write several opening lines for your own product using the most common hook types, then test the strongest ones against your current opener. [L0378.116]
  • Sales copy is a core online marketing skill, because it sells through words in print and in search results. Developing this skill can improve results more than most other changes. [L0382.049]

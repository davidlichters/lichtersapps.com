12.1 Creative strategy and the production system

Creative strategy as a system

  • Treat creative strategy as a system with clearly defined parts, each with a specific role, so the process can be followed step by step and scaled. This structure is what makes it possible to hand the work to AI. [L1478.001]
  • Creative strategy runs in four stages: conception, planning, production and management. AI can support each stage. [L0485.013]
  • Creative strategy involves three parties: the ad platform where ads run, the brand's potential customers who search for or use that platform, and the creative team, which is the brand itself. The brand's job is to make all three work together in a relevant way. [L0508.001]
  • No creative strategy works unless the advertiser understands how each party behaves. Platform needs, audience habits and the brand's own production capacity must all be understood before ads are planned. [L0508.002]
  • Decide which kinds of ads the brand wants to produce before choosing a team structure or AI tools. Without this decision, the team, the tools and the instructions cannot be set up properly. [L0464.016]
  • Diluting many opinions often reduces effectiveness, just as a committee with different priorities decides worse than one knowledgeable person. Before using AI for creative work, choose one proven source or methodology to train it on rather than mixing many. [L1478.005]
  • Testing creatives is one of the most important activities in e-commerce, so new creative ideas should flow continuously through a pipeline. Keeping ideas visible and moving them through stages prevents good concepts from being forgotten. [L1181.035]
  • Avoid over-engineering the brief. The product problem, desire and solution do not need to be filled in on paper; the worksheet is a guide to frame the message, not a task that must be completed in full. [L0353.038]
  • A product page and creatives only need to be good enough for a test, because perfection takes too much time and money before any data arrives. [L0349.029]

Campaign ideas and creative methods

Three levels of creative method

  1. Swapping a product name into a proven headline. This is the shallowest and most common method, and it works only when the product, market and timing match the original. [L0330.059]
  2. Formulas help with how a headline is worded, but the idea itself cannot be fitted to a formula. [L0330.059]
  3. Analysis is the only reliable approach. It yields a unique answer for every product, market and moment. [L0330.059]

Building a national campaign

  1. Find the dominant image or idea that will sell the product the first time and stay true to the market over many uses. [L0330.084]
  2. Compress that image into one statement or picture that is strong enough to sell alone. [L0330.084]
  3. Do not repeat the same headline or lead picture every time. Present a series of variations, each emerging from the central idea but different enough to make the reader read on. [L0330.084]
  4. Keep the central idea recognisable across the series, so past desire is reused while the variations renew interest. [L0330.084]

Repeated-headline pattern

  • Formula: a central campaign idea is repeated word for word in every ad as the headline, with a changing series of illustrations or opening scenes that make the same question fresh each time. The reader has to examine the picture and the copy together to get the answer. [L0330.085]
  • Why it works: continuity is kept while familiarity and boredom are avoided. [L0330.085]
  • Example A: the same tagline for a shampoo in every ad, with a new fashion scene each week. [L0330.085]
  • Example B: the same question about a bread brand in every ad, shown with a different family breakfast each time. [L0330.085]

Focal points and product-led series

  • The central idea of a campaign can be carried by a single focal point rather than a slogan. The focal point might be a product quality shown in every photo, a consistent style in typography and fashion, or the product itself shown from a new angle in each ad. Each ad reveals a new fact about the product, while subject matter and visuals stay consistent. [L0330.087]
  • A campaign built around the product can use a bare background, close-up views and one new fact per ad. Each ad alone should be enough to interest a buyer, and together they build an impression of novelty and value. [L0330.088]
  • Each headline should touch the dominant desire, express the campaign theme and draw the reader into the body copy, often through a visible contradiction between the headline and the picture. [L0330.088]
  • Build the campaign message from slogans or taglines used as themes, unified by a core principle of belief. The formula is Impact + Frequency + Time = Winning Message. The core message is the engine; biases are accelerants, not the main driving force. [L0196.037]
  • Brand messages should appear in ads, emails and content at the same time. The brand's core claims should be repeated at every touchpoint to build a community around the problem the brand solves. [L0334.038]

Volume, diversity and relevance

The three pillars

  • Three pillars guide creative strategy: volume, diversity and relevance. They work best as objectives the brand aims for across its whole ad account, and platforms such as Meta and TikTok look for all three. [L0508.003]
  • Aim for these three objectives in ad production. Relevance means each ad is useful or valuable for the viewer and does not create a negative user experience. [L0464.017]
  • Account-level objectives of volume, diversity and relevance act as guardrails for the whole ad account. They are separate from the single-ad variables of idea, style and hook. [L0485.016]

Volume

  • Volume means running a high number of ads that might perform well, not uploading an occasional ad. Platforms such as Meta and TikTok give little support to accounts that upload rarely. [L0508.004]

Diversity

  • Diversity means running different kinds of ads instead of repeating one ad, especially one that is underperforming. Winning ads are found by testing varied approaches. [L0508.005]

Relevance

  • Relevance means ads must be genuinely valuable and useful to people on the platform. Platforms fear that annoying ads will push users to competing apps, so the algorithm rewards brands that publish useful, relevant content. [L0508.006]
  • Creative quality is becoming more important than audience targeting because platforms are moving toward broad targeting. When targeting is broad, the ad creative does most of the work of finding the right buyers, so new creatives must keep being produced and tested. [L1426.001]

Failure modes of the pillars

  • Mass-producing low-quality AI ads without checking them, for example 300 ads per month with no review, produces poor results and wasted time. At worst it can lead to restricted or banned ad accounts. Visible flaws such as off shadows, spelling mistakes or very short low-effort videos hurt performance. [L0464.018]
  • Spending a long time polishing one ad with AI, such as movie-level backdrops, endless editing and repeated revisions, leaves no time for other ads. In the same time, five to ten more diverse ads could have been produced and tested. [L0464.019]
  • The best approach avoids both extremes: no unchecked mass output and no perfectionism on single ads. Create a steady range of diverse ads that are relevant and reviewed. [L0464.020]
  • Heavy editing of ad creatives in dropshipping often lowers performance, with worse CTR and higher CPM. Simple, direct creatives tend to outperform polished ones for typical dropshipping products. [L0660.028]

Ad variables: idea, style and hook

Definitions

  • Every ad is built from three variables: the idea (the message), the style (how the message is presented) and the hook (what catches attention first). Each one can be changed to alter how the ad is perceived. [L0485.001]
  • The pillars of volume, diversity and relevance describe the account goals, while the three variables describe what a single ad is made of. [L0508.007]
  • The idea is the core message of an ad: its main point, sales argument or selling point. It answers what the ad is trying to say. [L0508.008]
  • The idea is the core message, and a separate selling point usually makes a separate idea. An ad only holds attention when its message is relevant to the viewer, so an ad for an unrelated problem gets scrolled past. [L0485.002]
  • The style is how the idea is presented, for example a founder speaking to the camera, a static comparison with another product, or a demonstration of the product. The same idea can be delivered in many different styles. [L0508.009]
  • The hook is the element that grabs attention first. In a static image it is the layout or visual element that catches the eye instantly. In video it usually falls in the first seconds and can run longer. [L0508.010]
  • Ad concept = idea + style. An ad cannot have a concept with only an idea or only a style. The hook is the opening part of the concept that the viewer sees first. [L0508.013]

Priority of the hook

  • The hook makes more difference to ad performance than any other variable, as seen repeatedly in testing. For that reason the hook deserves the most attention when creating and testing ads. [L0508.012]

Avoiding over-complication

  • Many agencies, brands and coaches push teams to consider nine or ten variables, such as sub-message, angle, motion and sub-proof. This becomes confusing and delays testing the three variables that matter most: idea, style and hook. [L0508.014]
  • Creative merit depends on how good the ad is, including quality, angle and narrative. Try different angles and story structures before deciding a creative has failed. [L0644.027]

Levers and copy

The three levers

  • Each ad has three levers you control: visuals, written copy and narrated copy. Pulling them in different directions changes how the ad looks and how it is perceived. [L0485.005]
  • Visuals are everything shown on screen: static images and assets, B-roll in videos and camera angles. [L0485.006]
  • Written copy is all text appearing on the creative, such as subtitles, a core caption or a text tile. [L0485.006]
  • Narrated copy is the voiceover or spoken audio. Music rarely changes performance much. [L0485.006]
  • The best ads have all three levers pointing in the same direction toward one chosen idea and style. Visuals, written copy and narration should reference and show the same thing, and different styles should not be mixed. [L0485.008]

Ad copy

  • Ad copy text is often the real cause of poor results, not only the image or video. Write copy carefully because it can decide performance. [L1212.016]
  • Before focusing on the store, improve existing footage and images, add AI tools where useful, and communicate the offer clearly. The goal of these creatives is a strong click-through rate. [L1219.019]

Checklist for a good ad

  1. Attention-grabbing hook that highlights the problem and the main benefit, relevant to the target audience. [L0640.022]
  2. A compelling offer. [L0640.022]
  3. A clear call to action that tells people what to do and where to go. [L0640.022]
  4. Benefit-focused copy. [L0640.022]
  5. Social proof such as testimonials, case studies or reviews. [L0640.022]
  6. Scarcity or urgency, such as an end date or limited stock. [L0640.022]
  7. High-quality visuals that illustrate the point. [L0640.022]
  8. Educational or entertaining content. [L0640.022]
  9. Curiosity built up without hiding what the product is. [L0640.022]

Native style and iteration

  • Build creative iterations by combining your best-performing images with your best-performing videos. Use split-screen layouts, add overlays for colours and sizes, layer image ads as overlays, and test endcard variations showing colours, before-and-afters, offers or CTAs. [L0258.016]
  • Editors should include voice-over, subtitles and product benefits, formatted in the native style of each platform: TikTok style for TikTok, Hormozi style for storytelling. [L0258.016]
  • Winning ads match the target season's aesthetic, feel emotional, look native to TikTok and Reels, and use lifestyle or POV angles. They must not feel salesy, because viewers buy when the ad blends into their feed. [L0347.028]
  • Scroll-stopping creatives drive impulse buying, so the first seconds must make a product look desirable and relatable at a glance. When producing new ads, replicate the lighting, colours and camera angles that match the season. [L0347.031]

Workhorse and warhorse ads

Categories

  • Ad creatives fall into three practical categories based on effort and quality: donkeys (low effort, low quality), workhorses (low effort, high quality) and warhorses (high effort, potentially high quality). Most creative effort should go into the middle ground between the two extremes, not into either end. [L0483.001]
Category Effort Quality Guidance
Donkey Low Low Avoid [L0483.002]
Workhorse Low High Core of production [L0483.001, L0483.004]
Warhorse High Potentially high Make sparingly [L0483.005]

Donkeys

  • Donkeys are quick, generic ads that exist only to fill a slot, often made with unchecked AI output. They are rarely seen as relevant or valuable, and they often lack creative diversity because styles and ideas are not tested. Avoid them. [L0483.002]

Warhorses

  • Warhorses are very high-effort productions, such as hiring a full studio, booking many people, commissioning custom B-roll and using AI to add cinematic backdrops. They can look impressive but may become a cinematic piece instead of a sales ad, so the test is whether they sell, not how good they look. [L0483.003]
  • Warhorses should be made sparingly, because even very high-effort productions do not always perform well or reach high quality. Their extra effort does not guarantee sales. [L0483.005]
  • Field-shot ads, such as street interviews with real people, are warhorses if the team cannot currently film them, because they require going out to meet people. Choose a style the current team or AI tools can produce now, and keep field-shot formats for later. [L0485.030]
  • Some warhorses become workhorses over time. As a brand builds assets, learns its processes and gets better at using AI, they take much less effort, and the workhorse share should grow. [L0483.013]

Workhorses

  • A workhorse is an ad that one person can complete in about one day or less, using existing assets or a set of assets already in production. Anything that requires a new studio booking or new camera equipment for that single ad is a warhorse. [L0483.010]
  • If a studio or shoot is already booked for other content, extra footage captured during it can count as a workhorse because it adds little additional effort. If the brand already has B-roll and only needs AI to fill the rest, the ad is a workhorse. [L0483.011]
  • A workhorse ad is high quality mainly because it is relevant, not because it is polished. The idea and message matter most, so a simple, clear presentation with a strong hook that targets the right audience is enough. Because it is cheap to produce, the brand can keep up the volume and diversity of tested ideas, styles and hooks. [L0483.006]
  • Most static ads are workhorses because they can be built quickly from templates. One person can fill in a template, generate or adjust visuals with AI, and finish the ad. [L0483.016]

Allocation

  • Allocate about 80% or more of creative effort and energy to workhorse ads and about 20% to warhorse ads. These percentages are flexible, and a brand can start with 100% workhorses until it has the time and budget for warhorse productions. [L0483.004]
  • The workhorse-warhorse line differs by brand and has no universal definition. Each brand should decide based on what it finds simple and quick to create. Borderline cases are normal. [L0483.012]

Balancing extremes

  • Mass output without review and perfectionism on single ads are both mistakes. The goal is a steady range of diverse, relevant and reviewed ads. [L0464.020]

Procedure: listing workhorse ads

  1. Before using AI, decide what it will be used for in your ad production. [L0483.009]
  2. List the workhorse ads you could produce: the ones that take little effort and could be relevant and sell. [L0483.009]
  3. Describe what each workhorse ad would look like. [L0483.009]
  4. Break each workhorse ad into a step-by-step production process, so each step is clear and doable. [L0483.009]
  5. Use existing inspiration, such as other ad accounts and swipe files, to check which styles you can realistically produce. [L0483.009]

Procedure: choosing workhorse ads

  1. Which ideas and styles are relevant to the product and the audience? [L0485.010]
  2. Which of these can you actually produce now with your current team, capacity and skills? [L0485.010]
  • Example: a founder-style ad counts as a workhorse only if the founder can speak confidently and fairly easily about the product's benefit, such as how it relieves back pain. If that is possible now, the ad can be produced immediately. [L0485.011]

Procedure: breaking down the levers

  1. Decide where the visuals will come from. [L0485.012]
  2. Decide how the written copy will be created. [L0485.012]
  3. Decide how the narrated copy will be recorded, including whether you have a microphone to capture good audio. [L0485.012]
  4. Check that all three levers are simple to produce. [L0485.012]

Hook and style feasibility

  • Before committing to a workhorse ad, check whether the idea is easy to identify, the style is easy to create and the hook is simple to produce. A hook that takes a long time to produce, such as a scientific diagram without AI help, turns the ad into a warhorse rather than a workhorse. [L0485.004]
  • Check each hook's feasibility before committing. Re-check hooks like this with current AI image tools, since many complex visuals can now be made quickly and may qualify as workhorses. [L0485.029]
  • Most static ads are workhorses, and UGC-style and TikTok-style raw footage recorded on a phone converts better than studio ads for most products today. [L0398.016]
  • Authentic, raw footage with a normal background makes viewers feel a neighbour is recommending the product. This relatability drives action more than polished brand footage, which viewers recognise as an ad. [L0398.017]
  • UGC-style and TikTok-style content is recommended over classic salesy ads for Facebook and Instagram. Classic ads can be used for testing, and creators should be hired once budget allows. [L1335.020]
  • Low-production, native-looking content, such as a smartphone video that looks casually uploaded, outperforms polished traditional ads on TikTok and increasingly on Instagram Reels. In the owner's testing, UGC-style content was ahead of traditional ads. A strong offer can still lift polished ads, but UGC style is the default starting point. [L1077.017]
  • For the mentor's product, picture ads outperformed video ads over the past year, so picture formats should be tested alongside video. Products that need explanation of what they do for the customer still benefit from video, while simple novelty products need less explanation. [L1107.012]
  • Video ads are much harder and slower to produce for a complete beginner, though many people manage them. Start with image-based bottom-of-funnel ads to validate a custom product before investing in video. [L0794.032]
  • Food-based videos are more time-consuming and messier to produce, since the products must be bought, prepared and filmed. Factor the production effort into the decision to test a food-related product. [L0657.024]
  • Content for a product that gives a one-time result runs out quickly, because the creator cannot repeat the same demonstration over and over. Consider how many different angles a product can support before committing to it. [L1444.033]

Choosing the message before the format

  • The instructions given to a person or AI decide the kind of ad that comes out. A static image with a cost-focused benefit and a UGC-style video talking to the camera require different messages, skills, instructions and production styles, even for the same brand. [L0464.014]
  • A vague request, such as asking AI to make an ad for the brand, gives unpredictable results: the wrong format, the wrong message or a poor mix of styles. Define the ad type and the message before asking AI to create anything. [L0464.015]
  • Letting AI generate ad concepts without first checking them against your own research and proven ad best practices produces ads that are not relevant to the audience. Use research to choose the idea, hook and style first, then use AI to produce it. [L0483.017]
  • If the messaging misses the customer's pain point, changing the ad format will not save the ad. This applies whether the format is a flipping book, street interview, conference, seminar, podcast, claymation, cartoon or UGC AI. Messaging comes before format, budget and targeting. [L1019.001, L1195.031]
  • Choose two value levers: save money, better quality or save time. [L1016.023]
  • Map those two levers onto the list of twelve ad creative formats, such as versus-them, product value proposition and before-and-after comparisons. [L1016.023]
  • Start with three or four formats that match your strongest differentiation rather than producing all twelve, because doing everything usually means doing each one poorly. [L1016.023]
  • Choose social media channels based on business type. B2B stores may need a LinkedIn presence, while newer consumer brands may need Pinterest or Instagram. A store already running Facebook retargeting ads still needs its own decision about which additional social channel to add. [L1396.073] (Older source: Source B course, 2021-22.)
  • Demand-generation channels such as Meta, TikTok and YouTube can create demand for a new hybrid category, so a niche may be marketed there even when Google search volume is low. Google search and Google Ads only capture demand that already exists. [L1389.013]
  • Different platforms need different creative styles. Classic polished Facebook ads from freelance marketplaces do not work on TikTok, where UGC, lifestyle videos and a fast pace are needed, and a trending sound is important. [L0349.008]

Team, production and tools

Team

  • Prioritise which products get custom AI images, because generation takes time per product. Good candidates are new launches, best sellers that need a higher CTR, and underperformers that should be pushed harder. [L0882.016]
  • Different people react to different creatives, so a founder needs to produce many creatives and master customisation. Test many variations of the same winning concept. [L1195.033]
  • Authentic production starts with the founder's own skills. The founder-style example in the workhorse section applies here: only ads the current team can speak and film now belong in production. [L0485.011]

Tools and costs

  • Photoshop costs about £10 per month and is used for product photo editing and background replacement. [L0561.047] (Source lists the price in pounds; verify current pricing before budgeting.)
  • Canva offers free graphic design and video editing with a small course. CapCut is free for video editing. [L0561.047]

Organising production files

  • Give each product its own dated folder, named with the date and product name, such as 2023-04 plus the product name. [L1427.012]
  • Inside each product folder, keep Assets (static images, emojis, logos), Audio (music, voiceovers, sound effects) and Video. Split the video folder by theme, such as testimonials, TikTok winners, paid influencer or UGC footage and dated shoots. [L1427.012]

Producing your own creatives

  • Once a product reaches about $500 to $600 in revenue and shows potential, create your own ads. [L1173.013] (Sources differ on the threshold; see the proof-of-concept figures below.)
  • Create your own ads by editing the footage yourself, ordering new footage, or briefing a UGC creator to make a video. [L1173.013]
  • To make a product stand out from competitors after a few days of consistent sales, produce custom content: order the product, film your own videos or hire creators and actors, then give the product a unique custom name. Custom content makes it harder for copycats to reuse the same videos. [L1382.056]
  • Own creatives and unique marketing angles differentiate a store from competitors who use the same ads. Without them, growth stays slower even when the product sells. [L1171.040]
  • When launching a product, use existing drop-shipping video to test quickly, then switch to custom content for products that show promise. Speed and resource use matter early, but custom content gives a stronger return later. [L0871.068]
  • A store can build an organic video library first, then run ads only on the one video that performs well. This keeps paid spend focused on proven creative. [L0706.054]

Lean starts and when to go custom

Starting a test with competitor material

  1. Copy the competitor's active ads exactly to see how they perform for your store. [L1173.013]
  2. Once the product reaches about $500 to $600 in revenue and shows potential, create your own ads. [L1173.013]
  3. Create your own ads by editing the footage yourself, ordering new footage, or briefing a UGC creator to make a video. [L1173.013]
  • Start a product test by copying competitor ads to see whether the product works. Once the product reaches roughly $1,000 to $2,000 in sales, invest in your own creatives to stand out. [L1171.039]

  • Creative quality usually matters more than website quality for a new store. Strong creatives can make an average store convert, while the website alone rarely fixes weak ads. [L1454.009]

Creative as the main lever

Ranking the levers (sources differ)

  • Creative is the second biggest lever in a paid ad account after product and offer. Fresh creative supply matters more than most campaign tactics or bidding tricks. Without a steady stream of newly tested creatives, cost caps, bid caps and structure changes do little. [L0902.001]
  • Creative is described as the main lever in current Meta and TikTok advertising. Invest in better creators and in understanding how hooks and hold rates work. [L1213.020]
  • Creative is the main lever in Meta ads now. A business that stops testing new creative usually sees its campaigns decline within one to two months, so constant creative testing is a core process, not an occasional task. [L0398.004]
  • The creative is the main lever in Facebook ads. When the creative is strong, Facebook learns from the people who engage with it, so creative quality matters more than the interests chosen. [L1469.035]
  • Creatives are the main driver of Meta campaign performance. With weak creatives, even good ad sets fail, while strong creatives let broad targeting without interests perform because the algorithm finds buyers. [L1335.019]
  • Creative is the main driver of performance on TikTok, and roughly 90 percent of the outcome depends on it. When CTR is poor, change the creative rather than the audience, because the audience is rarely the cause. [L1377.017]
  • On TikTok, creatives matter more than targeting. The seller should test many creatives, varying the hook, length, caption style and trends rather than only the product shot. [L1177.029]
  • Creatives drive targeting results more than CBO or ABO settings, because Facebook increasingly targets through creatives when targeting is broad. The main priority is avoiding overspending during product tests. [L0349.009]
  • With the same audience and budget, the creative determines how many viewers engage, click the call-to-action and reach the website. It is therefore the main variable to optimise. Other factors such as CPM matter less over the long run. [L0639.002]
  • Creative merit is a main driver of results because strong engagement lets Facebook push the ad to more people. Creatives with strong engagement receive a higher engagement ranking, which tends to lower CPM and CPC compared with competitors. [L0646.040]

Reconciling the rankings: the sources agree that creative is the decisive lever once the product and offer are validated, and they differ on where it ranks before that point. Validate the product and offer first, then treat creative as the main lever for scaling. [L0902.001, L0704.018, L0398.004]

Video and platform differences in the ranking

  • Creative drives results on Facebook and Instagram when targeting is broad, so keep producing and testing new creatives. [L1426.001, L1335.019]
  • On TikTok, creative drives most of the outcome. [L1377.017, L1177.029]

Testing cadence and refresh

Creative testing volume

  • Creative testing is a numbers game. Brands can test 100 to 200 creatives per week, and three to ten winners can cover the testing cost. Plan for many variants rather than expecting one video to win. [L0638.037]
  • In the early phase, one operator launched at least five new creatives per week and tested about 20 offers, while the landing page converted at about 3.5%. Testing volume came before optimising any single ad. [L1315.054]
  • On TikTok, test at least three new creatives per week while a product is running, since TikTok is almost entirely creative-driven. [L1177.028]
  • Upload at least 20 videos within the first month, or in one to two weeks for fast learning, before judging your creative skills and expecting results. [L0820.012, L0821.016, L0821.017]
  • Test new creatives every week. The mentor said this is the rule even though his own schedule often prevents it. Consistent weekly creative testing is the target. [L1107.011]

Refresh rhythm (sources differ)

  • Plan to test new creative concepts roughly every two weeks, because a single creative typically delivers good results for about one month before performance declines steadily. [L0902.002]
  • Refresh the testing cycle with new creatives weekly when a fresh set is available, and at least every two weeks otherwise. Each creative typically performs well for about one month before results decline, so the testing pipeline must keep producing new concepts. [L0902.029]

Expected rotation size

  • Creatives in rotation = concepts × creatives per concept. Example: 10 concepts × 5 creatives each = 50 creatives in rotation, and the count grows as you iterate. [L1019.039, L1195.035]

Using the testing pipeline

  • Not every creative will convert. Many creatives work as cheap touchpoints that help other creatives convert, so test a large amount of variation. [L1225.056]
  • Creative testing should use a pipeline of stages. A typical example is seven creatives in the pipeline, with five in preparation and two in production. [L0646.053]

Testing a new market with creatives

  1. Create four creatives for four audiences, with different benefits and call-outs. [L0646.053]
  2. Go deeper into the one that responds best. [L0646.053]
  3. Aim for a range of concepts, such as seven creatives in the pipeline with five in preparation and two in production. [L0646.053]

Not dropping a product too early

  • When a product's first batch of ad creatives fails, do not drop the product right away. Produce several more creative batches and test them before deciding the product does not work. [L1469.001]
  • Start the test with the widest range of approaches before judging a creative as failed, because merit depends on angle and narrative as well as quality. [L0644.027]

Volume versus perfection

  • When producing ad creatives, volume beats perfection. Launching more variations quickly and gathering test data outperforms spending time perfecting a small number before launch. [L0199.009]
  • The creative agency goal is to produce volume of creatives that each generate positive contribution margin, not to find one rare breakout ad matching your highest-performing creative from the past. Many creatives will underperform. [L0217.053]
  • Creative success should be measured by the batting average, the percentage of creatives generating positive contribution margin, not by finding one breakout ad that beats all previous records. [L0217.027]
  • The goal of a creative agency is to produce volume of creatives that each generate positive contribution margin, similar to how professional baseball players succeed only 20 to 30 percent of the time at the plate. [L0217.028]
  • Success is measured on the percentage of ads hitting positive contribution margin, similar to a professional batting average. [L0217.053]

Output limits

  • More creative volume is not automatically better. The real question is how much is too much for a given brand. For larger brands, plotting revenue against the number of creative assets shows whether more output correlates with more revenue. [L0885.011]
  • Mapping creative launches per product exposes mismatches, such as heavy spend on one product while only three or four new creatives launch for it each month. This approach is especially suited to brands with three or four products. [L0885.012]

Expected rotation for a set of concepts

  • Expected volume is roughly 10 concepts × 5 creatives, about 50 creatives in rotation. [L1019.039]

Benchmark output tracking

  • Track net new concepts and iteration concepts separately to see how much output is truly new versus variations of existing ads. Both numbers together show whether creative work is actually broadening the account. [L0885.010]
  • Iteration versus net-new tracking is not in the naming convention at the time of the lesson. Options are to add a flag for iteration or new concept to the names, or to let Meta classify each ad as iteration or new concept, since briefs already exist. [L0885.013]

Weekly cadence and creative routine

  • Plan to test new creative concepts roughly every two weeks, and refresh weekly when a fresh set is available. [L0902.002, L0902.029]
  • Consistent weekly creative testing is the target. [L1107.011]
  • On an unprofitable day, customer research and sharpening the next creative are a better use of time than monitoring metrics. Research improves the next ad rather than reacting to yesterday's numbers. [L1445.010]
  • Testing creatives is one of the most important activities in e-commerce, so new creative ideas should flow continuously through a pipeline. [L1181.035]
  • Build the test around finding proven creatives. [L0704.018]

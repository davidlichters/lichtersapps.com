12.16 Video ad scripts and creator briefs

Script-first rules and priorities

  • Write the ad script before filming, briefing or sourcing clips, because creative testing needs written scripts. Videos made by sending products to creators or filming without a creative strategy do not produce effective ads. [L0902.004]
  • Write the script around a narrative such as problem, agitation and solution before editing, so the message drives the edit. At the testing stage the marketing message matters more than whether the ad uses images or video. [L0344.013]
  • Settle the target audience and the script first, because an ad built without them becomes irrelevant. These decisions come before sourcing clips or editing. [L1427.001]
  • Build a video ad in this order: [L1427.002]
    1. Define the target audience and the narrative or script.
    2. Source clips that match each scene of the script.
    3. Sort the clips into narrative order.
    4. Cut the clips together to get the structure right.
    5. Add the extras: transitions, sound effects, voiceover and text overlays.
  • Before filming, define what the product is, what it does, its unique features, how it benefits the buyer, and who the target audience is. Write the script from these points so every shot serves a specific feature or benefit. [L1403.002]
  • Do not send a video creative service only the product name and ask for an ad. This usually gives a very poor result. Write a detailed script first, using copywriting principles, and send that script with the brief. [L1107.019]
  • Do not film the A-roll without a recorded script. Without it the footage has no timing reference and the edit becomes guesswork. [L1403.027]
  • Choose the format before writing. A talking-head or direct-to-camera script works for both video sales letters and UGC-style ads. [L1140.027]
  • Before filming a sales video, write the full script first. Treat the script as copy and apply the same single-action direct response approach to it. (Older, Source B 2021-22.) [L1460.010]
  • Apply the priority system below when writing scripts. [L0039.010]
Follow a hierarchical priority system when writing scripts: voice and tone rank highest and govern word choice, structure and beat sequence rank second and govern composition, a hard blacklist of forbidden patterns ranks third and requires self-auditing before delivery, the avatar ranks fourth and defines the audience, and psychology frameworks rank fifth and inform the thinking behind the other four. For each assignment, identify the structural mode needed, lock the beat sequence, verify the angle addresses the right avatar fear, write the full script applying voice and psychology together, audit against the blacklist, then flag any conflicts with the reference documents.

Core ad structures

Hook, middle, call to action and endcart

  • Use this four-part framework: [L0258.012]
    1. Hook with a compelling problem or product angle.
    2. Present the solution in the mid-section with additional features.
    3. Include a clear call to action.
    4. Use the endcart to display colors, sizes, before-and-after comparisons, offers, or other conversion elements.

Facebook video, 30 to 45 seconds

  1. Hook: the first 3 to 5 seconds, the most important part of the video. It must stop the scroll. [L0398.007]
  2. Product introduction: the next 3 to 5 seconds, showing the product in hand or a quick unboxing right after the hook's pain point, claim or suspense. [L0398.007]
  3. Middle section: about 15 to 20 seconds, cut into clips of 2 to 3 seconds each. Change the camera angle or scene every 2 to 3 seconds and show use cases and benefits. [L0398.007]
  4. Proof: about the last 5 to 6 seconds of the middle, with reviews or testimonials. [L0398.007]
  5. Ending: a benefit-driven call to action, such as shop now or a website link. [L0398.007]
  • Showing a hand holding the product right after the hook lets viewers feel subconsciously that the product is already theirs, which makes the product introduction more convincing. A quick unboxing shot works for the same reason. [L0398.058]

Classic product video, 30 seconds or less

Use this when no UGC is available and existing stock clips are used. Keep the total length to 30 seconds or less. [L1335.021]

  1. Hook (first 3 to 5 seconds, scroll stopper): bold claim, bold headline, pain point, or suspense in a caption.
  2. Product introduction (next 3 to 5 seconds): POV shot or product in hand or in use, so the viewer feels they receive the product.
  3. Benefit body (about 10 to 15 seconds): change clips every 2 to 3 seconds with a benefit-driven caption or product-in-use shot. Add review or testimonial clips and feature captions to build trust.
  4. End with a benefit-driven call to action.

Six-part ad order with call to action

  1. Hook in the first 3 to 5 seconds: stop the scroll by showing the product in action with something shocking, unexpected, or UGC. [L0777.006]
  2. Educate: show the viewer what the product is and how it works. [L0777.006]
  3. Provide the solution: show how the product solves the viewer's specific problem. [L0777.006]
  4. Provide benefits: state the outcomes the viewer gets. [L0777.006]
  5. Provide features: show the concrete product attributes, such as fit, materials, or use cases. [L0777.006]
  6. Call to action in the last 3 to 5 seconds: tell viewers to tap the Shop Now button and include the store URL in the closing frame. [L0777.006, L0777.005]

Problem-solution video

  • A problem-solution video converts better than a talk-heavy or giveaway-style video because it shows the product doing its job. The strongest case is the problem first, the solution second, and then the product in use. [L0657.008]
  1. Open the video with a visible problem in the first seconds. [L0657.009]
  2. Build tension around the problem, for example by showing a risk such as slipping or failure. [L0657.009]
  3. Reveal the solution, which is the product. [L0657.009]
  4. Show clearly what the product does, using zoom-ins and close shots to make the result easy to see. [L0657.009]
  • For a problem-solution video: [L1219.025]
    1. Show the problem clearly so viewers recognise it.
    2. Introduce the product as the solution to that problem.
    3. Add voiceover or captions if they help the message land.
  • Short-form social video needs storytelling. A video must either promise the viewer a clear takeaway in the first few seconds or tell a story they want to watch to the end. Short clips that only say to check the caption fail to hold viewers. The key skill is finding creators who can carry these stories. [L0505.003]

Blunt sequence

  1. Open with a blunt, direct statement that stops the scroll. [L0524.046]
  2. Name the problem the viewer may not have questioned. [L0524.046]
  3. Push on pride or identity so the problem feels worse. [L0524.046]
  4. Label the viewer with the identity they want, such as being tough or a real man. [L0524.046]
  5. Show the product and its key specification. [L0524.046]
  6. Show the ingredients or features that matter and what they do. [L0524.046]
  7. Show how the product is used. [L0524.046]

Shock demonstration with fixed close

  • Formula: open each commercial with a short, startling demonstration that stresses the central idea. Then repeat the same closing sentences in every version. A dramatic opening grabs emotion, and the fixed closing gives the selling message consistency. [L0330.086]
  • Example A: a demonstration of a ball hitting a protective shield, followed by the same closing line about protection in every version. [L0330.086]
  • Example B: a quick demonstration of a stain lifting in one second, followed by the same claim sentence in each spot. [L0330.086]
  • For products whose main claim can be shown on camera, such as water resistance on a phone screen protector, start the first video with a live demonstration of that claim. Visible tests stop viewers quickly and make the benefit clear without a long explanation. [L0372.047]
  • A simple 16-second video with background music, a clear problem solved, and a call to action performed well for a low-cost household product. Videos of this kind can work even when the problem is small, as long as the benefit is clear within the first few seconds. [L0706.055]

Category examples

  • Pain-relief product: educate by showing how the device works on the body, then present it as a solution to back pain. Name the benefit of using it at home without a therapist. Close the middle with features such as a comfortable fit that can be worn while working, resting, or playing. [L0777.008]
  • Pet product: educate on a behavior problem such as separation anxiety in dogs, then show the product as the solution. State the benefit as reduced anxiety symptoms when the owner is away. List the features, such as soft faux fur, a raised outer rim, and a spot that suits sleeping. [L0777.009]

Problem-agitate-solution and UGC narrative

Problem-agitate-solution script prompt

Write a problem-agitate-solution video ad script and scene suggestions for [PRODUCT: product name and what it does], based only on the information I have given you: [FEATURES AND BENEFITS], [PERSONA: demographics, behavior, pains and fears], [DESIRE the customer wants to reach or escape], [MARKET AWARENESS STAGE: unaware, problem aware, solution aware, product aware or most aware] and [MARKET SOPHISTICATION STAGE 1 to 5]. Structure the script as follows: in the first scene, name the problem; then agitate it by spelling out its consequences and how it makes the customer feel; then present the product as the solution; then intensify each benefit by saying what it means for the customer. For every scene give the visual, the spoken or on-screen line, and the approximate length in seconds. End with a call to action, and mention [OFFER] only if it is real and has a true deadline or stock limit. Keep the language plain, specific to the persona, and free of vague phrases.

[L0515.028]

UGC narrative formula

  • Surface a problem the viewer feels, agitate it to make it feel urgent, present the product as the solution with its features and benefits, then show the desired outcome. It works because it mirrors a human story arc that viewers recognize and remember. [L0561.029]
  • In UGC scripts, present the problem as a villain that the viewer must fight. This makes the story more engaging and the solution more appealing. Outline the problem, make it feel urgent, then present the product as the answer and the desired outcome as the payoff. [L0561.056]
  • Kitchen gadget example: viewers see a sticky counter that makes them feel embarrassed when guests come over. The gadget removes the mess in seconds, and the closing shot shows the host hosting friends with pride. The shift from embarrassment to pride is what triggers the purchase. [L0561.030]
  • Back pain product example: a person who cannot sit through a workday because of pain. The product gives relief during the commute, and the end shot shows them playing with their kids on the floor. It turns a physical complaint into a clear emotional payoff. [L0561.031]

Talking point for creators

  • Formula: Show how [product] [key benefit], making [everyday moment] feel [emotional result]. Name the feature or launch news the video must show, then describe the tone and the feeling the viewer should get. This gives the creator a clear point to hit and a mood to film for, without a shot list. [L0579.020]
  • Example 1: Show how our quiet, compact air purifier keeps a bedroom fresh overnight, making every morning feel like a reset. [L0579.020]
  • Example 2: Show how our lightweight travel backpack fits a full week of clothes, making every trip feel effortless. [L0579.020]

Script templates

UGC testimonial script, 30 to 60 seconds

Use for a skincare stick or similar product, filmed on a phone by a real person in natural light with no studio set. Fill every placeholder in square brackets. Do not add claims the product cannot back up. [L0707.019]

Hook (0-3 seconds): Speak straight into the camera to the target buyer, for example: [PRODUCT FORMAT] in a stick? I did not believe it at first either, and I am [TARGET IDENTITY, e.g. a woman in her forties with fine lines].

Proof (3-8 seconds): Show a before and after image or clip and state the result you saw: [SPECIFIC RESULT, e.g. less hollow under-eye darkness] in [TIMEFRAME, e.g. three weeks].

Problem (8-15 seconds): Name the exact problem the viewer already has: [PROBLEM, e.g. smoker lines around the mouth or dark hollow under-eyes], and say how long you struggled with it: [DURATION].

Insecurity (15-20 seconds): Admit the personal feeling behind the problem: [INSECURITY, e.g. feeling older than you are in photos].

Authority (20-25 seconds): Mention your credible role naturally: [ROLE, e.g. a nurse who researches products before buying].

Solution and benefits (25-45 seconds): Walk through the ingredients and results in one run, one benefit per sentence: [INGREDIENT 1] helps with [BENEFIT 1]; [INGREDIENT 2] helps with [BENEFIT 2]; continue to about ten benefits, each matched to a real ingredient.

Social proof (45-50 seconds): Mention reviews or demand: [REVIEW COUNT] five-star reviews, and the product [SOLD OUT OR WAIT TIME, e.g. was out of stock for four weeks].

Call to action (50-60 seconds): Create real urgency tied to stock or availability, for example: [URGENCY LINE, e.g. stock looks low, so order now], then point to the link or product card: [CTA].

90-second talking-head script prompt

Write a 90-second talking-head script for [product] based on the angle [angle name]. Use about 215 words at a natural ad pace. Use simple everyday words and the exact buyer phrases from the survey: [paste 3 to 5 phrases]. Follow this order: hook (0:00), name the private wound the buyer feels (0:14), reframe why nothing worked and that it is not the buyer's fault (0:30), introduce [product] by name as something made for this gap (0:52), show the proof and the relief (1:08), and close with an invitation to take a look instead of a tap-now push (1:22). Do not promise anything the product does not deliver. Avoid jargon and hard-sell commands. End with the word count and the estimated runtime.

[L1140.020]

90-second script order

  1. Hook (0:00): open with a private thought the buyer has in a hard moment. [L1140.022]
  2. Agitate the wound (0:14): name the self-doubt and the failed attempts the buyer already made. [L1140.022]
  3. Reframe and mechanism (0:30): explain that the failure was a missing tool or method, not a character flaw. [L1140.022]
  4. Introduce the product (0:52): name the product and describe it as something built for that exact gap. [L1140.022]
  5. Proof and relief (1:08): show what changes for the buyer and the words they use after it works. [L1140.022]
  6. Call to action (1:22): invite the viewer to take a look, not a tap-now push. [L1140.022]

Formula for the 90-second script

  • Formula: open with a private moment, name the self-doubt, reframe the failure as a missing tool instead of a flaw, introduce the product by name as the fix, and invite a look. [L1140.023]
  • Example A (sleep product): Most people blame themselves when the nights stay bad. The reason nothing stuck is a missing routine, not a lack of discipline. [Product] gives you that routine in ten minutes a night, so come see how it works. [L1140.023]
  • Example B (running insoles): You have tried every insole and your feet still hurt, so you assume running is not for you. Your feet were never the problem; the support had the wrong shape. [Product] is built for that shape, so take a look at how it fits. [L1140.023]
  • The reframe must agree with the buyer's skepticism that nothing worked, then explain why it failed. Arguing against that skepticism makes the buyer resist the ad. [L1140.024]
  • Do not hide the product name until late in the script, and do not use advanced vocabulary for simple products such as books. Simple, warm language converts better than a hard direct-response tone for these buyers. [L1140.025]

Voiceover and narration

  • Narration can repeat the written copy word for word, say it slightly differently, play as a conversation, or run as a blind voiceover without a visible speaker. The choice changes how the ad is perceived. [L0485.007]
  • Add voiceover or captions where they help the message land. [L1219.025]

Emotional and identity-based scripts

  • Formula for emotional products: a named, relatable persona with a specific problem tied to the niche's emotional goal. The persona discovers the product, uses it in a daily routine, and sees a visible change in their life. Viewers identify with the person and imagine the same result. This works better than listing features for products bought on identity or feeling. [L1158.048]
  • Example 1: Lena, a mom of three who never had time to unwind, added a calming lavender roll-on to her evening routine and describes her nights as quieter. [L1158.048]
  • Example 2: Marcus, a night-shift nurse struggling with restless sleep, began a bedtime ritual with a weighted eye mask and now falls asleep faster after work. [L1158.048]

Dialogue and complaint formats

  • Fake-conversation pattern: stage a short exchange where one character makes a claim and a second character responds, then reveal the outcome at the end. The conflict or outcome should keep viewers reading to the end. [L1384.023]
  • Fake-conversation example A: a customer complains about a late delivery and the brand replies with a surprising fix. [L1384.023]
  • Fake-conversation example B: a friend disputes a product result and the reply reveals the truth. [L1384.023]
  • Complaint format: a customer posts a complaint where his own mistake caused the problem, and the brand responds. The viewer watches to see who is right, so the customer's error works as the hook. The brand's reply must be fast and specific. [L1384.060]
  • Complaint example A: a buyer says the product broke, and the brand replies with the correct usage steps. [L1384.060]
  • Complaint example B: a buyer claims an item is missing, and the brand answers by showing the unboxing footage. [L1384.060]

Creator-made and UGC content

  • A person on camera is optional for a UGC-style ad. The founder can film the product, or a friend can. A self-recorded testimonial with the seller's own face can work as social proof. The condition is that the footage looks like a real person using the product, not a polished brand ad. [L0398.061]
  • Creator videos must look genuine and believable, because viewers can tell when a sponsored clip feels fake. A believable review performs better than an over-produced one. [L1379.031]
  • Discovery-style opening formula: open with a surprised first-person discovery line that names the source, then show the product being used. It works because viewers trust a casual find more than a polished ad. [L1379.030]
  • Example 1: "Look what I just found on TikTok, it actually works!" [L1379.030]
  • Example 2: "Wait, I bought this from a random Instagram ad and it solved my problem." [L1379.030]
  • Videos made for ads should be produced specifically for paid placement, not reused from organic content. Ad videos need stronger calls to buy, reasons to purchase, and a clear hook. A video that went viral organically may fail as an ad, for example when it went viral through a giveaway that attracted people who only wanted a free item. [L1383.021]

Testimonials and demonstrations

  • A before and after image placed on top of the video works as visual proof and resembles a testimonial. It shows the result the viewer wants before the speaker makes any claim. [L0707.010]
  • Show the product applied to more than one body area during a testimonial, for example face and lips, so viewers with a different problem see themselves in the demonstration. The visual widens the set of problems the ad appears to solve without adding a new claim. Only show areas the product is labelled for. [L0707.030]

Shot planning and feature demonstrations

Feature and benefit on screen

  • Each product feature is a demonstration, and each benefit is the reason for the viewer to care. Show the feature on screen, such as a Bluetooth connection, light-up mode or floating, while the script states the benefit, such as connecting to any device or enjoying it in the bath or pool. [L1403.016]
  • Feature-to-benefit shot pattern: pair each product feature with a short on-screen demonstration and a spoken benefit line, then show the product in a real use context. [L1403.018]
  • Example A (kitchen knife): the feature "blade stays sharp for months" is shown by slicing tomatoes in a clean cut. The benefit "prep dinner in half the time" is spoken over the footage. [L1403.018]
  • Example B (foldable yoga mat): the feature "folds into a bag" is shown by folding it in one motion. The benefit "take your practice anywhere" is spoken as the user walks out the door. [L1403.018]

Golden rule and shot list

  • Film for the context. The footage must support what is being said. When the speaker talks about a feature, the scene should show that feature working, such as the brush picking up hair when the script says it does. [L1425.021]
  • Use a shot list tied to the script. For every script line, write the scene that shows it before filming. This prep makes the edit faster and reduces wasted takes. [L1403.019]

Scene sheet fields

Scene-by-scene script template for handing to a creator or editor. Fields per scene: [L1425.024]

  • Scene: a label such as Hook 1, Hook 3, the pain, the solution, or the scarcity point.
  • Emotion (optional): the feeling to convey.
  • Script: the spoken lines.
  • Visual: secondary footage, b-roll or talking-head shot.
  • Editing Instructions: the editing style, for example trending on TikTok, or to showcase the mood.
  • Start with the ad angles and scripts, then table the full script so creators and team members can follow it easily. [L1425.024]

Twelve-scene creator brief

  1. Create a brief for video creators requesting 12 five-second scenes instead of one 60-second video. [L0321.017]
  2. Specify 5 hooks, 5 different use cases, and 3 different calls-to-action. [L0321.017]
  3. Send the product to the creator for free; they keep it after shooting. [L0321.017]
  4. Receive the raw video clips. [L0321.017]
  5. Send the clips to an internal video editor. [L0321.017]
  6. The editor combines the clips into 5 to 10 different final videos with varied hook and scene orders. [L0321.017]
  7. Test the videos on Facebook or TikTok. [L0321.017]
  • This structure yields more content variations for testing than a single video. [L0321.017]

Production sequence and reuse

Turning a winning ad into a script

  1. Pick the winning creative with the highest website purchases in the Kalodata testing CBO. [L1195.029]
  2. Paste it into Google AI Studio and transcribe it. [L1195.029]
  3. Use the transcript as the basis for a custom VSL with the same winning messaging. [L1195.029]

Reusing formats

  • Reuse the same intro across videos to make three uploads a day easier to produce. Repeated intros keep production fast without losing the format that works. [L1383.039]

Calls to action and endings

Placement of the call to action

  • Add a call to action at the end to raise conversion, but expect it may lower views, because viewers who spot an ad often skip immediately. Test it per product rather than using it by default. [L0764.013]
  • Include the call to action inside the video itself, not only in the ad text or buttons. (Older, Source B 2021-22.) [L0488.006]
  • The last 3 to 5 seconds must contain a clear call to action that tells viewers to tap the Shop Now button. Include the store URL in the closing frame. [L0777.005]
  • Conflict between sources: the standard video ad closes with a Shop Now tap in the last 3 to 5 seconds [L0777.005]. The 90-second talking-head script closes with an invitation to take a look, not a tap-now push [L1140.022, L1140.025]. Use the invitation for talking-head scripts that name a private wound; use the Shop Now close for standard video ads, and test per product [L0764.013].

Retargeting reminder

Script for a collection ad reminder, spoken by the store owner on camera. [L1243.025]

Hey, thanks for checking out my store. I noticed you never finished checking out, so maybe you got busy. I just want you to know that your cart is still waiting for you. Point up to the link in the ad copy, or point down and say: Click below to go straight back to the product.

[L1243.025]

  • Keep it short, face the camera and speak as the owner, because a personal video stands out more than a generic image. [L1243.025]

Business-owner introduction videos (older, Source B 2021-22)

  • In a video introduction ad, keep the video to a couple of minutes at most, or even shorter, because it plays directly in the news feed. [L0488.007]
  • Order for a short introduction video from the business owner. It should stay within a couple of minutes: [L0488.016]
    1. Greet the viewer briefly and say who you are.
    2. State that you are the owner of the business.
    3. Offer help choosing the right product for the viewer's situation.
    4. Say that your team is ready to help and invite the viewer to get in touch.
    5. Briefly describe what the business does and which problems the products solve.
    6. Mention key selling points such as free shipping or easy returns, if they apply.
    7. Repeat the call to action at the end.

Lesson-style episodes

  • Keep each lesson video at about five minutes, which was the chosen length for this format. A consistent length makes the format easier to repeat and to produce regularly. [L0888.017]
  • Episode procedure: [L0888.035]
    1. Open with a short greeting and a hook that names the topic.
    2. Read the source text aloud in its original language.
    3. Give the translation.
    4. Explain one clear takeaway for daily life.
    5. Close with a call to watch the next part.
    6. Keep each episode to one passage so the video stays within about five minutes.

12.11 Offers, urgency, scarcity and calls to action

Offer structure and pricing

  • Limit the choices on the page. More than 3 pricing tiers or too many configuration options cause decision paralysis and lower conversions. [L0196.060]
  • Keep the ordering step simple. Include at least a short sentence or paragraph that explains how easy it is to accept the offer, what the prospect must do, and why they should act quickly. [L0756.022]
  • Give the prospect a reason the deal exists, such as limited supply, a deadline, a news event, or a shared concern. This answers the unspoken question of what the catch is and reduces suspicion that the offer is too good to be true. [L0756.012]
  • If there is a catch, such as a membership requirement, a purchase obligation, or a limit on quantity or time, reveal it or hint at it early and explain it before asking for the sale. The catch should also be a net positive for the buyer. [L0756.013]
  • The prospect should see the benefit as both valuable and a bargain compared with their normal price. A discount does this, but so can an emphasis on a higher or elite price, as luxury brands do. [L0756.014]
  • For digital products, make the offer feel like more value than the price. The buyer should sense that the extra bonuses or content are worth more than the money paid. [L0513.017]
  • Repeating the same generic offer in ad copy does not drive performance. Standard discounts plus free shipping are expected, so use unique, enticing offers. [L0206.008]
  • When an offer stops working, test a higher offer approach, such as a free gift where the customer chooses between two gifts. [L0950.042]
  • Attract the right buyer with the offer. A free-gift offer draws people who sign up mainly for the gift and may cancel once they have it. This is acceptable for low-priced products but risky for high-priced ones. If immediate cash matters most, an easy-payment offer may not be the right test. [L0756.017]
  • Keep the offer in the lead identical to the offer at the end of the sales letter, using the same language, hot buttons and rationale. Offer details can be tested in the lead just as they are in the close. [L0756.018]
  • Use these price tests for offer-driven copy:
    • A higher nominal price with a 2-for-1 deal
    • A trade-in offer
    • Covering all shipping costs
    • Introductory or limited-time pricing
    • A $1 trial, where the card is charged $1 and then the full amount after a 30-day trial [L0756.020]
  • Front-end and back-end offers differ. The front-end product is typically priced around $50 or less and aims to get the customer onto the list. The back-end product is priced at $2,000 to $10,000 or more and generates most of the profit. [L1356.049]
  • A money-back guarantee suits cheap front-end offers. Expensive information-based back-end offers usually avoid one, because buyers could download the content and then ask for a refund. [L1356.049]
  • A price or result expressed as a percentage should also be shown in dollars for the reader, especially when comparing your cost with a competitor's. Dollar savings make the value concrete. [L0756.019]
  • Check the price band before publishing. The same product reads differently at different prices. A device at a high price reads as serious professional equipment, at a middle price as a useful gadget, and at a low price as a toy, even with identical copy. [L1366.079]
  • Build the offer section in this order. The steps need not follow this order, but this order works well:
    1. Summarize everything discussed so far.
    2. Introduce the intro report.
    3. Introduce the intro service: unique selling proposition, how it works and what to expect, and credibility.
    4. Add bonuses.
    5. Share testimonials.
    6. Recap with a price anchor.
    7. Offer a guarantee.
    8. Limit spots or quantity.
    9. Set a deadline.
    10. Ask for the sale. [L1356.050]
  • In a membership or subscription offer, the copy often sells two things: a free or cheap report that holds the opportunity, and the subscription that grants access. Introduce the report without revealing its details, then state the requirement to subscribe, for example a membership for $49 per year. [L1356.051]
  • Do not reveal the details inside a report or product in the offer. Raw data does not persuade, and a reader who thinks they have figured out the secret will leave or find a cheaper version. Use stories rather than raw data as proof. Give the report a distinctive name such as "Dossier", "Bulletin", "Hot Sheet", "Guide" or "Manual" rather than "Report". [L1356.052]
  • Describe what the buyer gets by covering these points:
    • What comes with the service or membership
    • How often they will receive emails
    • What the emails contain
    • Show a graphic of a sample email, complete with letterhead and text, so the reader sees the format and branding. [L1356.056]
  • Describe ease of use with specifics. Explain the step-by-step instructions, how quickly the reader can act (for example "in mere minutes"), and what is not required (for example a special brokerage account or a minimum amount of money). The reader should see that anybody with an internet connection and some time can follow the steps. [L1356.057]

Discounts and percentage framing

  • Percentage discounts such as "Save 67%" or "Save 70%" are psychologically more persuasive than equivalent dollar amounts such as "Save $20". Use percentages when the numbers impress. [L0232.016]
  • A claim that prices were cut 25 percent carries far more weight than saying prices were simply reduced. Always attach a figure when the number is real. [L1261.078]
  • Frame shipping as "Free expedited shipping" rather than "Free shipping". This makes the offer sound more premium and valuable. [L0232.011]
  • Pair a discount with a countable or dated limit, placed right before the call to action. A finite offer gives the customer a reason to act now rather than later. [L0648.063]
    • Example 1: 25% off for the first 100 orders, then full price.
    • Example 2: Free gift with every order until Sunday midnight, while stock lasts. [L0648.063]
  • Combine a spending threshold, a discount percentage and a deadline to create urgency. [L0232.013]
    • Example: "Free shipping on all orders over $45, 30% off today only"
    • Example: "Free 2-day shipping on $100+ purchases, 25% off expires tonight"
    • The source notes that the urgency signal drives impulse buying even if the deadline is not hard-enforced. Under this handbook's honesty rule (see Honest urgency and scarcity), use this only when the store really ends the offer at the stated time. [L0232.013, L0461.008]
  • When a discount is strong, reframe it in several ways to make the price feel smaller. Use a percentage saving, a per-day cost and an affordability statement.
    • Example: "over 80% off the total value"
    • Example: "13 cents a day"
    • Example: "a price anyone could afford" [L1356.064]
  • Keep the price anchor (the stated total value of the offer) under about 20 times the actual price. A stack of $421 reduced to $49 is more believable than $8,414 reduced to $49, which is a reduction of over 99% and triggers skepticism. [L1356.063]
  • Apples-to-oranges anchoring compares the price with a much more expensive alternative the buyer would otherwise pay. Example: hedge fund fees of 2% management plus 20% of returns, which could exceed $250,000 a year, set against a $2,000 subscription. [L1356.065]
  • A price cut or money-saving offer is the simplest news for a product with an established market. State the sale date and the exact price to make it specific and timely. [L1369.069]

Bonuses and free gifts

  • A bonus should reduce hesitation. It should not make the paid product look worth less. A "dissolving bonus" is a gift added to a deal with enough extra value to melt away remaining resistance. [L0756.015]
  • Leading with "FREE" gifts that feel nearly as valuable as a high-priced product can make the paid product look less valuable than claimed. For low-priced items, generous free bonuses are usually harmless. For high-priced items, use them carefully. [L0756.016]
  • Bonuses should relate to the core opportunity. Unrelated bonuses weaken the main offer. For example, a gold-buying guide attached to a cryptocurrency letter weakens it. Suitable bonuses include extra reports, physical products tied to the theme (such as a laptop with the chip the company makes), or other opportunities in the same sector. [L1356.058]
  • Test a free bonus alongside a product. Example copy: "FREE Posture Fix Guide ($19.99 Value) with every Posture Pro Corrector. Stand taller. Feel better. Limited time only." with a "CLAIM YOURS." button. The free bonus raises perceived value, and the time-sensitive message creates urgency without a countdown timer. Include both the product and the bonus in the creative. (Older advice, 2021-22 for the pattern above is not claimed; this unit is Source D 2026.) [L0316.039]
  • A free bonus ebook of 15 to 25 pages can be offered as a gift with a product to add perceived value and capture buyers. Write it around one specific problem the target customer has, so the bonus supports the product rather than distracting from it. [L1323.014]

Guarantees and returns

  • A guarantee does not have to be money back. It can promise a result or a chance at a result. For financial copy, a guarantee such as "the chance to see 10x your money or we'll work for free" only promises the chance of a result. The "work for free" part is often a second year of service at no cost if the result does not happen. [L1356.066]
  • Test highlighting a strong guarantee in the headline when a great guarantee exists. A bold guarantee can close the sale for a prospect already searching for the product. [L0756.023]
  • State the return window as a concrete number of days and add one emotional word about how the refund is handled. This makes the buyer feel the company is prompt and respectful. [L1366.018, L1366.077]
    • "If the fit is wrong, send it back within 14 days for a fast and fair refund."
    • "Not happy in the first 60 days? Return it and we will refund you quickly and kindly."

Offer lead and headline

  • The offer lead formula:
    1. Focus immediately on the most emotionally compelling detail of the offer.
    2. Underline the most valuable benefit of that deal.
    3. Expand on the same deal-benefit in the lead that follows.
    4. At some point, give a compelling reason why the deal is offered. [L0756.026]
  • Lead with the deal, price, guarantee or premium in the headline or first sentence. Then add a strong promise. Pattern: "Don't pay a penny until [result]" or "You risk nothing unless [outcome]". [L0756.011]
    • "Don't pay a dime until your garden doubles its harvest."
    • "Test-drive the new coffee grinder for 30 days, and pay nothing if it doesn't cut your morning prep in half." [L0756.011]
    • This works because it reverses risk before the prospect can object, and the guarantee makes the deal feel like a clear gain. [L0756.011]
  • When a trusted person is known to the list, add their endorsement to the lead. Put their signature on the letter, show their photo next to the headline, or include their name in the product title. [L0756.024]
  • Copy that explains the problem, the likelihood the customer will reach the goal and the effort required makes the product's value easier to see. This framing comes from Alex Hermosi's 100 Million Offers, which the lesson recommends for copywriting. [L0774.023]
  • For a digital product, use the offer to show more value than the price. [L0513.017]

Honest urgency and scarcity

  • Do not show a coupon field at checkout if you do not offer a discount. Readers feel they are losing out when they see one. [L0196.029]
  • Emphasize the potential loss of not acting near discounts and limited-time offers. [L0196.029]
  • Use scarcity signals only when they are true. Examples include "Only 1 Room Left" and a small number of available times. [L0196.029]
  • Strike through the features of lower tiers to show what is locked. [L0196.029]
  • Reframe copy as risk avoidance. Example: "Avoid Easy-to-Miss Writing Mistakes Costing You Sales" instead of "Learn How to Write Better". [L0196.029]
  • Design elements that disappear when urgency or scarcity runs out. Examples are a time-limited bonus or an offer for the first X buyers only. The language should clearly frame missing out as a loss. [L0196.030]
  • Create a "Staged FAQ" section that addresses common objections and misconceptions. Reframe the risks and give facts to counter them. [L0196.030]
  • Creating urgency without a deadline does not work. "Don't miss out" and "Offer is limited" give the customer no reason to act today. Specific deadlines such as "Offer ends Sunday" or "Today only" create real perceived scarcity. [L0313.012]
  • Text-based urgency works as well as countdown timer graphics. Examples include "Limited time only", "Sale ends Sunday at midnight" and "Offer ends this weekend". It feels less jarring and may have higher perceived legitimacy. [L0316.049]
  • A deadline date alone does not create urgency. Raise the stakes by framing the choice as act now or lose out. Build the urgency into the lead, repeat it in the offer section, and reveal details of an upcoming event gradually so the reader feels the window closing. [L1365.025]
  • Use this either-or formula:
    • Formula: Either [act now, before the specific event or deadline] or [specific loss], because [event] will close the window on [benefit].
    • Why it works: The either-or framing removes the option of waiting and makes inertia costly, which is the purpose of the urgency section.
    • Example 1: Either you lock in the early-buyer price before the Friday restock, or you pay the full price once the window closes.
    • Example 2: Either you start your listing before the holiday search spike, or you compete for the same buyers at double the ad cost. [L1365.026]
  • Scarcity, the partner of urgency, limits time and quantity. Example: "get in by this date, if you still can". It works best just before the ask for the sale and should be used sparingly. Urgency can be dropped from a headline, but it is valuable in the offer section, where it adds the final push. [L1356.067]
  • Do not sound desperate for the sale. Begging the prospect to buy, or showing how much you need the revenue, turns readers away. In the offer section, the copy should sound confident and indifferent to whether the reader decides to buy. [L1365.043]
  • When a product truly solves a problem and the copy has made a clear case for it, a natural urgency appears, because the reader does not want to keep living with the problem. Extra scarcity or fake urgency is then unnecessary. [L0461.003]
  • Real scarcity and real offers may be used in a call to action. Both must be true before the copy goes live. [L0461.006, L0461.008]
    • Stock example: For a high-priced product, state the true number of units left and the true replenishment time, for example "only 10 left, otherwise a six-week wait". Use this only when it is factually accurate. [L0461.007]
    • Offer example: "30-40% off for next week only, and not again for a few months." The discount and the no-repeat window must both be true. [L0461.008]
  • Tie any urgency line to a real stock or availability signal. Example: "Only a few left, grab yours before midnight" and "Restocking soon, lock in today's price." [L0706.043, L0707.018]
  • Under this handbook's honesty standard, only state deadlines and stock counts that the store will enforce. [L0461.008, L0707.018]

Countdowns and timers

  • Time limits increase urgency for ready buyers. Test countdown deadlines based on time or quantity. On online sales pages, use a live countdown timer that shows the remaining time on the deal. [L0756.021]
  • Countdown timers can be used for booking, along with a small number of available times. [L0196.029]
  • The countdown headline formula is: time left, then event name, then the deal. The deadline is visible before the click. A concrete time frame creates urgency with few words, but the time must be accurate for the live offer. [L0547.009]
    • "Only 6 Hours Left on Spring Clearance"
    • "Last Call: Summer Sale Ends Tonight" [L0547.009]
  • An upsell can show a short countdown next to a lower unit price. Example: "Want a second copy at a better price? This offer expires in 3 minutes. Add one more and pay less per unit before the timer runs out." [L1141.031]

Calls to action: wording and placement

  • End sales copy with a clear, specific and urgent call to action. It should tell the reader exactly which next step to take. Even if Add to Cart or Buy Now buttons exist on the page, the copy itself should restate the next step. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0461.001]
  • Tie the call to action to the main problem the customer wants to solve and the product's core purpose. Adding a short sentence of "why" next to the action helps. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0461.002]
  • The call to action should tell the buyer exactly what to do, including the button or the step, so there is no doubt about how to proceed. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0461.012]
  • A short call-to-action block of roughly 25 to 35 words is enough for a closing action. In one technical product, the call-to-action paragraph was about 26 to 33 words, and the bridging paragraph before the feature list was about 33 words. Adding five extra feature bullets raised the copy from about 461 to about 616 words. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0461.020]
  • Use this call-to-action pattern for a product with a clear instant benefit. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0461.009]
    1. Restate the core convenience or power benefit in one sentence.
    2. Name the button the reader should press, in quotes, and its colour.
    3. Add a promise of fast delivery or an immediate start.
    • Example: "Start your at-home recovery today: press the blue "Add to Basket" button below and we will ship your unit to your door within 48 hours."
    • Example: "Get your daily routine back this week: tap the orange "Order Now" button and we will send your starter kit tomorrow." [L0461.009]
  • Test call-to-action wording such as Save Now, Buy Now, Shop Now and Get Deal. Most of these are expected to work, so pick the one that performs best in testing. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0799.014]
  • Use explicit calls to action such as "Learn More" or "Shop Now" in ad copy when space permits, even if the ad has a clickable area. This makes the intended action unmistakably clear. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0241.008]
  • Every ad needs a call to action. Options for product reminder ads include Shop Now, Buy Now, Add To Cart and Learn More. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L1169.020]
  • Use Shop Now as the call-to-action button for a product test ad. [L1193.022]
  • Use Get Yours Today only when the reviews already show happy customers using the product, because the ad then reads as the next step after proof. Shop Now is the safer default. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L1398.013]
  • Every retargeting ad needs a call to action that matches the outcome after the click. For content-stage visitors, use a learning call to action such as Learn More or Read Now. For visitors near the bottom of the funnel, send them to a product page with a buying call to action such as Shop Now. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L1238.009]
  • Do not place the product price in bottom-of-funnel ads. Show a discount percentage, such as "20% OFF Today", or a value claim, such as "$19.99 value". This keeps the focus on the offer rather than the cost. [L0316.028]
  • Every short-form ad video needs an explicit call to action near the end, such as a "shop now" prompt or a link instruction. Viewers who like the product but are not told what to do usually scroll on. The call to action turns interest into a click. [L0398.057]
  • A video with a strong viral hook but no call to action, urgency or description loses sales. Add a clear call to action on the video text and in the description, such as "grab yours today" or "limited availability". [L0655.008]
  • Write the call to action in plain, brand-appropriate language. Crude or insulting wording offends viewers and lowers the click-through rate. [L0392.013]
  • The call to action should be clear and direct, for example "Shop now", "Buy now", "Click here" or "Learn more". [L1425.007]
  • Benefit-driven call-to-action pattern: a benefit phrase followed by the buying action, placed right after a persuasive paragraph or headline. The visitor is already convinced and only needs a clear next step. [L0916.015]
    • "Start stress-free mornings: add the bundle now."
    • "Get a cleaner kitchen this week: order yours today."
  • Assume the reader will take the next step. Invite them to join others instead of questioning why they have not acted. Formula: a direct instruction plus the simple next step. [L1261.075]
    • "Order your starter kit today and start in one evening"
    • "Request your free guide now and see the results for yourself." [L1261.075]
  • Use this urgency call-to-action formula: [timing or scarcity trigger] + [clear action to take now]. Short deadlines and limited availability reduce hesitation and encourage viewers to act while they are watching. [L0706.043]
  • Crossroads close: present two clear choices. Join and get the benefits, or click away and keep the old situation. Keep it to two options, because more choices water down the contrast. Let the reader feel the decision is theirs while the choice stays obvious. [L1356.068]
  • Push-pull close: first push the prospect away by making them feel in control, with options and the freedom to decide that this may not be right for them. Then pull them back by reminding them of the one belief and what they would lose by not acting. [L1365.041]
  • Three-option close:
    1. Do nothing and stay where you are.
    2. Do it yourself, with the time and effort it requires.
    3. Let us handle it for you.
    • Describe each option honestly, avoid choosing for the reader, and close by asking which fits them best.
    • Showing the costs of each path lets the reader see that inaction has a price, and the easiest path is the one the seller offers, without sounding needy.
    • Example 1: Option one: keep paying your current agency for slow results. Option two: learn the tools yourself, which takes evenings for several months. Option three: our team runs the campaigns for you starting Monday. Which of these fits your week?
    • Example 2: Option one: stay with the spreadsheet you update every Sunday. Option two: build the dashboard yourself, which can take a full weekend and some trial and error. Option three: we install a ready dashboard and walk you through it in one session. Which fits your schedule? [L1365.042]

Upsells, order bumps and downsells

  • Show an order bump, a one-time offer, only at checkout. [L0196.029]
  • Upsell headlines work when they invite the buyer to add one more unit, with the lower price and a short countdown next to it. The short question makes the add-on feel like a natural extension of the purchase. [L1141.031]
  • Upsell headline pattern: name the purchase that just happened, offer a second unit or companion product, state a clear discount, and add a time limit. The buyer is still in buying mood and needs only one click. [L1412.035]
    • "Your order is confirmed. Add a second set now at 20% off, offer ends in 10 minutes."
    • "Complete your setup with the matching charger at 15% off, this offer closes in 15 minutes." [L1412.035]
  • Downsell headline pattern: keep the same product but present a last-chance deeper discount after the first decline. This gives a lower-risk way to say yes without re-entering payment data. [L1412.036]
    • "One last chance, grab it now at 30% off before this page closes."
    • "Still thinking? Your extra unit is available at 30% off for the next few minutes." [L1412.036]
  • Upsell template: a limited-time discount at 25% that will not recur. Majority of customers (exceeding 75%) select this upgrade option. Exclusive access is granted for immediate purchase. Add an additional product at the stated price, with immediate savings shown. [L0291.008]

Holiday and promotion copy

  • Holiday deals and specific offers need different copy. For a specific offer, state the deal clearly in the ad. It has to look irresistible, be something the viewer does not see elsewhere, and be better than what competitors offer. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0799.005]
  • For a holiday deal, do not spell out the discount. Allude to a big, never-to-be-repeated sale that ends soon, such as a sitewide sale or a deal on specific products. If the deal is product-specific, feature that product in the image. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0799.006]
  • Some hype is acceptable for a holiday deal if it is justified, because Black Friday is often one of the biggest sales of the year. Keep the claim tied to the real size of the sale. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0799.009]
  • Holiday deal ad elements: (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0799.012]

    • "Dress Up Your Home for Less This Spring"
    • "Pack Your Cart Before the Summer Break Ends" [L0547.010]
  • Exclusivity formula: a "just for you" or early-access frame works only for a real segment, such as email subscribers or past buyers. Name that segment in the ad or landing page, because the reader feels selected. [L0547.011]

    • "Your Summer Preview Is Ready for Past Customers" [L0547.011]
  • Holiday promotion copy formula: state the offer and the time window first, then add a short hook tied to the holiday, then a clear call to action with the coupon code. The time limit creates urgency, and a themed hook helps the email or ad stand out. [L1115.085]
    • Example headline: "Spend the weekend in a jacket, save 15% with code WINTER15 until Sunday."

Consistency across ad, email and landing page

  • Keep the offer identical from ad headline to email subject to landing page, so the customer sees one consistent promise along the whole path. A mismatch between ad and email lowers trust and conversion. [L0547.013]
  • Keep the offer wording identical across emails, ads and website banners. [L1222.006]
  • Use the same offer, coupon code and time window across emails and remarketing ads for each promotion, so the message is consistent for the customer. [L1115.088]
  • Ad content must match the landing page. An ad that promises an offer or checkout route the store does not show creates a mismatch that hurts conversion. [L0704.008]
  • Ad copy should match the product description and the landing page. This consistency speeds up the click-through and confirms to the shopper that they landed in the right place. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L1169.018]
  • Mismatched ad copy and landing page confuse shoppers after they click. Do not promise one thing in the ad and show another on the page. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L1169.019]
  • Use the same message across emails and remarketing ads, so the same people see the same promotion in their inbox and on Facebook, Instagram and Google. The headline of each remarketing ad should match the email subject line of the same holiday. [L1223.023]
  • Assets required for each promotion, prepared before it starts: [L1222.004]
    1. Email copy for the full email sequence.
    2. Ad copy for the ads that run during the promotion.
    3. Images for both the emails and the ads.
    4. High-converting email subject lines and ad headlines.
  • Ad copy should be taken directly from the email sequence of the same promotion, so ads and emails carry the same message. [L1222.006]
  • Holiday asset workflow for each promotion: [L1224.014]
    1. Write the email sequence first, using the gain-logic-fear structure.
    2. Review every email for errors or invented facts (AI can hallucinate) before using it.
    3. Import the reviewed emails into Shopify Email or your email platform.
    4. Generate ad copy from the email sequence, not from scratch.
    5. Generate promotion-specific images that match the offer.
    6. Edit images in Canva if you need to add a logo or text, and resize them for each ad format.
  • Ad copy derived from the email sequence: [L1224.025]
    1. Ask for ad copy based on the email copy, for Meta and Google Display remarketing.
    2. For Meta, take the primary text and headline from each email, with the call to action set to Shop Now.
    3. For Google Display, take a headline and a description for each email stage, matched to the days it runs.
    4. Check all copy against the emails and the actual offer before publishing.

Answering objections

  • Use ads to answer objections that appear in comments, such as cheaper hacks people suggest. Read comments for price objections and reframe the product around its benefit. [L1225.047]
  • A Staged FAQ section addresses common objections and misconceptions, reframes the risks and provides facts to counter them. [L0196.030]

User-generated content and third-party voices

  • When an offer appears in third-party user-generated content, phrase it gently rather than as a direct discount. A direct "get 40% off today" works in brand-made ads. In UGC it looks like a sales pitch. A softer framing such as "two for one as it's their summer sale" fits better. [L1348.007]
  • Endorsements from a trusted person can strengthen an offer lead. [L0756.024]

Further details

  • Place testimonials in the offer between bonuses or after them, not in the credibility section. Case studies and testimonials are reserved for the offer section, while the credibility section uses authority signals such as TV appearances and track record. [L1356.061]

  • Write the recap in the offer as a rapid, bullet-driven list. Give each piece of the standard offer and each bonus its own bullet with a short description, assign a dollar value to each bullet to create a total, and then state the total value. Show that total as a large, prominent number, then state the price is far below it. [L1356.062]

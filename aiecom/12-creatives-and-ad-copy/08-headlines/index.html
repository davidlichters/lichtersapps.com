12.8 Headlines

Headline role and proportion

  • The headline has one job: stop the prospect and make them read the second sentence. It does not need to sell, name the product or state the main appeal, and asking it to do all of that wastes most of the budget. [L0330.014]
  • The headline takes only about 10% to 20% of the ad's space, so loading it with the full selling job leaves the rest of the ad empty of persuasion. The headline can carry a full selling job only when the prospect is actively looking for that exact brand and a special price reduction is on offer. [L0330.015]
  • Older advice (2021-22): a widely cited copywriting observation says about five times as many people read the headline as read the body copy. Headline work therefore deserves disproportionate time, and a weak headline makes the rest of the copy largely irrelevant. [L0416.002]
  • For editorials, about 60-80% of success comes from the headline, so most copywriting effort goes into the headline first. The 5x observation refers to general copy; the 60-80% figure refers to editorials. [L0578.014]

Headline formulas and structures

Core formula

  • Headline Formula: [Audience Identifier] + [Hook/Structure] + [Benefit Modifier].
    • Example structure: 'The [noun] Who Perfected The [topic], and Learn How It [Result + Timeframe].'
    • Alternative structures: 'A [Adjective] Approach to [Enticing Promise], And Why [Audience] Are Missing Out on [Outcome].' [L0196.005]

Structure-borrowing methods

Imitation Game procedure:

  1. Write a brief stating what you want the reader to do and what they should feel by the end.
  2. Visit trending sites (digg.com, reddit.com, news.google.com, medium.com, buzzfeed.com, cracked.com) and find headlines that attract attention, focusing on structure not content.
  3. Extract the structural pattern from trending headlines (e.g., 'The [noun] Who Perfected The [topic]').
  4. Analyze why the headline works: does it stoke curiosity, confirm a belief, open a loop, or challenge expectations?
  5. Adapt the structure to your own topic and audience.
  6. Expand with a benefit modifier: add the end result and time frame (e.g., '...and Learn How it 4x'd His Profits in 6 Months'). [L0196.001]

Amazon R&D Method procedure:

  1. Write a brief describing what you want readers to do.
  2. Go to amazon.com's book section, select your niche, and sort by bestsellers.
  3. Extract the subtitle structure from top books (e.g., 'A [adjective] Approach to [enticing promise]').
  4. Adapt the subtitle pattern to your own niche and expand it with benefits and modifiers. [L0196.002]

Problem-solution and persona formulas

  • Problem-Solution pattern: name the specific frustration or pain point your audience experiences, state that you have solved it, then introduce your product. It works because starting with the problem creates immediate relevance for people living with that pain, and stating the solution positions your product as the answer.
    • Example 1: 'Heavy workout gear slows you down. We fixed that. Introducing FlexMove.'
    • Example 2: 'Returns processing costs eat profit. We fixed that. Introducing ReturnLogic.' [L0321.041]
  • Persona pattern: a headline can combine the desire, the context where the problem happens, and the persona's specific situation, such as the place, the pet type or the trigger. Start with a plain outcome claim, add a stronger version such as 'with zero effort', then add a question or label that points to the specific persona. Finish with the product name and the action it performs.
    • Examples: 'Remove all the fur from your sofa with the FurFree Pro.' 'Is your Labrador shedding all over your bed every single morning?' [L0516.005]
  • Digital product formula: name the problem, add a specific timeframe or method, and tie it to a unique mechanism or angle that makes the buyer curious enough to click. A time-bound, mechanism-driven promise feels concrete and differentiated against generic offers.
    • Example 1 (own): Stop Scrolling Late at Night: A 14-Day Plan That Rebuilds Your Sleep.
    • Example 2 (own): The Real Reason Your Savings Never Grow Is Not Your Income. [L0513.015]

Awareness-matched headlines

Offers by audience temperature

  • Offer-focused headlines (limited-time discounts, percentage off) are most effective for warm audiences who already know the product or have seen previous campaigns. For them, the offer is a conversion trigger. For cold audiences unfamiliar with the brand, use attention-grabbing or benefit-driven headlines instead. [L0204.020]
  • Promotion ads: state the offer in the headline and use the description for supporting copy. The offer is what converts a clicked ad into a purchase, and the headline gets the most attention.
    • Example 1: 'Get 50% off your second item when you buy one.'
    • Example 2: 'Buy two, get a free shipping bonus on your third piece.'
    • Conflict: L0204.020 keeps offers out of cold-audience headlines, while this formula puts the offer first. Apply the promotion formula to warm or promotion-ready audiences and the benefit-led approach to cold ones. [L0642.028]

Product-aware prospects: seven tasks

When the prospect knows the product but has not yet bought it, the headline faces one of seven tasks. Choose the one that fits:

  1. Reinforce the desire for the product.
  2. Sharpen the image of how the product satisfies the desire.
  3. Extend the image to new places or times when the product satisfies the desire.
  4. Introduce new proof, detail or documentation of how well it works.
  5. Announce a new mechanism that satisfies the desire even better.
  6. Announce a new mechanism that removes a former limitation.
  7. Change the image or mechanism completely to separate the product from competitors claiming the same desire.

In every case, show the product name in the headline or in an equally large logo, use the rest of the headline to point out its superiority, then let the body of the ad elaborate with visuals, proof and mechanism until every strand of superiority is woven in. [L0330.021]

When the product name is shown, it is the exception to L0330.014: for a product-aware prospect, the headline names the product and points to its superiority. [L0330.014, L0330.021]

Patterns for product-aware prospects

  • Product name plus a symbol of a desired quality: attach a familiar symbol (prestige object, famous person or iconic phrase) to the product so the quality transfers. A familiar association reinforces the desire without any new argument.
    • Example A: Ship-shape hiking boots, the choice of mountain guides.
    • Example B: Coffee so rich, the baristas of the finest cafes reach for it first. [L0330.022]
  • Product name plus one concrete example: name the product and give one specific instance of its effect in a real situation. A specific instance makes an abstract benefit visible and believable.
    • Example A: The night a single cup of this tea let a tired teacher grade forty essays without falling asleep.
    • Example B: One pair of these headphones carried a full concert across a noisy airport without the bass turning to mush. [L0330.023]
  • Sensory language: describe the sensation through touch, taste, smell, sight or sound in a short phrase. Sensory language brings the desire closer to the surface and makes the product feel already owned.
    • Example A: Crisp apple bite that tastes like it was picked this morning.
    • Example B: Sheets that feel cool and soft the moment you slide in. [L0330.024]
  • Striking picture: lead with an arresting image of the product in use, so the image carries the claim and the headline or caption confirms it. It stops attention when the buyer already knows the product and its benefits. Use it for glamour, food, fashion, cosmetics and jewellery where the look sells.
    • Example A: A photo of a sun-soaked bowl of fruit on a linen table, with a caption naming the sauce.
    • Example B: A close-up of a watch face catching a sunset beside a single short line about the strap. [L0330.025]
  • Mechanism in vivid detail: describe the physical product or the mechanism that does the work so the reader pictures how it works. A clear picture of the mechanism makes the performance believable to a buyer who already knows the category.
    • Example A: At one hundred metres an hour the only sound inside this sedan is the clock on the dash.
    • Example B: The first thing you notice when the drill goes through the wall is that nothing shakes in your hand. [L0330.026]
  • Extend to a new place, season or moment: phrase a time or location claim that extends satisfaction to a context buyers did not associate with the product. It opens new usage occasions without changing the product.
    • Example A: Sunscreen that protects you in winter snow as well as summer beach days.
    • Example B: A morning drink that also works as your after-dinner treat. [L0330.027]
  • New proof: state a measured result, test or credible fact that adds detail to how well the product satisfies the desire. Fresh documentation revives belief in a claim buyers have heard before.
    • Example A: Four out of five people in a three-month home test reported fewer headaches on the new pillow.
    • Example B: After eight hundred hours of field testing, the new drill still held torque in the coldest workshop. [L0330.028]
  • New mechanism that satisfies better: name a new mechanism inside the product in the headline. A new mechanism gives a reason to switch when the market already knows the claim.
    • Example A: The first grill that cooks from both sides at once with no flipping.
    • Example B: A running shoe that returns energy with every step through a springy plate in the sole. [L0330.029]
  • New mechanism that removes a limitation: announce a mechanism that removes a downside buyers used to accept, such as dust, noise, weight, mess or wait time. Buyers switch for a product that gets rid of an old downside they had given up on.
    • Example A: A vacuum that keeps the room quiet enough for a baby to nap while it runs.
    • Example B: Paint that dries in minutes with no fumes, so you can finish the room before dinner. [L0330.030]

Problem-aware prospects: need-led headlines

  • Name the desire in one sharp sentence the reader recognises at a glance. Sharpen it in the lead, then satisfy and document it in the body while focusing it on the product. A new product must give vague desire a name before it can own it.
    • Example A: Learn to speak any language on your commute in three months.
    • Example B: Sleep through the whole night without swallowing a single pill. [L0330.032]
  • Name the need and dramatise it so the prospect feels how urgently they need a solution, then present the product as the inevitable answer. A prospect with a clear need is ready to accept a product that claims to solve it.
    • Example A: Heel pain that makes every first step of the morning a fight, and one insole that ends it.
    • Example B: Tangled wires behind the desk that ruin the room, and a clip-on tray that hides every cable. [L0330.033]
  • Need-led variants, chosen by how clearly the need is already felt:
    • (a) state only the problem in one word or short phrase;
    • (b) state the problem and its solution together;
    • (c) state the solution and the removal of the usual limitation together;
    • (d) state all three: the problem, its solution and the removal of the limit;
    • (e) offer a substitute for an unpleasant or expensive task;
    • (f) warn about a future problem and promise to prevent it;
    • (g) sharpen the problem by emphasising one word that makes the reader see himself;
    • (h) when a direct solution has lost force, add a twist to restore novelty.
    • Example A: Knee pain on stairs, gone in two weeks.
    • Example B: Never pay for a plumber again to clear a drain. [L0330.034]
  • Offer for problem-aware readers: name the concrete result, add a specific number, and remove one reason to hesitate such as time, skill or tools. A specific, quick outcome is easy to scan in a busy feed, while a vague promise is ignored.
    • Example 1: 'Fix Leaky Taps in One Afternoon With Tools You Already Own'
    • Example 2: '14 Back Stretches That Take Just 10 Minutes a Day' [L0348.036]
  • Prevention: a prevention headline sells only when it shows a problem happening to people the prospect cares about, such as children, a partner or family, not to the prospect himself. People will not picture disaster for themselves, but they will buy protection against it for the people around them. [L0330.058]

Solution-aware prospects: curiosity

  • Curiosity-gap headline: point to a surprising cause or hidden fact, withhold the answer, and leave an open question the reader wants to close. Readers who already tried other fixes stop scrolling when a familiar problem is framed with a cause they had not considered.
    • Example 1: 'The Reason Your Sourdough Stays Flat (It Is Not the Yeast)'
    • Example 2: 'Why One Cheap Drawer Liner Stops Mould Before It Starts' [L0348.037]
  • Curiosity headlines create a sense of mystery so the reader has to read on to resolve it. The best ones hint at a specific, surprising result without giving the answer away.
    • Examples: 'The one kitchen habit that quietly doubles the life of your fridge.' 'The cleaning mistake most people make without knowing it.' [L0516.032]

Transformation-focused prospects: identity

  • Identity-shift headline: address the reader as the person they want to be and show them already acting that way. People respond to a role they want to play, so the headline creates belonging instead of only describing the product.
    • Example 1: 'Be the Parent With a Plan When the Power Goes Out'
    • Example 2: 'Be the Friend Who Has the Fix Ready Before the Storm' [L0348.038]

Unaware prospects: hidden desire and story

  • Hidden dream story: connect the product to a dream by telling a short story of a person who reaches an aspiration through an everyday route, using a universal image as the entry point. A dream people already hold draws them in before they notice a sales message. Use it when a direct promise of the benefit would not attract enough people.
    • Example A: The bookkeeper who taught herself to paint in the evenings and now sells her canvases at a gallery across town.
    • Example B: The night-shift nurse who finished a degree one hour at a time from her kitchen table. [L0330.038]
  • Hidden fear: name a fear many people feel but do not admit, describe its symptoms and consequences in vivid steps, then offer the product as the way to relieve it. Fear that people quietly carry becomes urgent once put into words. Use it when the product's direct benefit has lost its pull.
    • Example A: The quiet worry that your savings will not last if you lose your job, and the one account that protects your cash.
    • Example B: The moment you realise your child is falling behind at school, and the program that gets them back on track. [L0330.039]
  • Accepted starting point: begin with an image or statement everyone accepts, lead gradually into a topic that would be rejected if stated bluntly, and only then introduce the product. A respected starting point gets the topic past the reader's resistance and often past publishers' rules.
    • Example A: Everyone agrees that a good night's sleep matters, so let us talk about the snoring that ruins it for the whole house.
    • Example B: Nobody enjoys the thought of a bad smell at work, and that includes the person who is unaware of it. [L0330.040]
  • Show, don't say: show a hidden desire through the people, situations and visuals that embody it. When a desire cannot be stated without sounding absurd or offensive, a strong image of people who already have it communicates it without words.
    • Example A: A confident rider crossing a desert at dusk, with no caption needed beyond the product logo.
    • Example B: A group of friends laughing at a rooftop party, all wearing the same watch. [L0330.041]
  • Shared resentment: find a common resentment or unspoken protest, then show that the product answers it. A shared grievance reaches a far larger audience than a direct promise of the solution, including people who do not think of themselves as the target. Show the facts behind the resentment, then present the product as the answer.
    • Example A: Why are you still paying a repair technician for something you can fix yourself in five minutes a week?
    • Example B: Why did the company that sold you the phone charge you for a battery you were never warned about? [L0330.042]
  • Triumph in the first person: describe the moment of triumph the prospect longs for, showing the reactions of others and the surprise they feel, then reveal the product as the source. Readers identify with the reward more than with the effort, so this reaches people who would be put off by a direct promise of skill.
    • Example A: Everyone laughed when I walked up to the presentation without notes, then the room went silent when I started speaking.
    • Example B: My coworkers thought I was joking when I said I would run the marathon, and then I crossed the finish line first in my age group. [L0330.043]
  • Outcome, not problem: describe the problem's result in a way that people who reject the problem's direct statement can still identify with. People accept a sharp description of an embarrassing outcome they recognise, even when they would reject a plain description of the cause. End a short story with the product solving it.
    • Example A: She always had the best outfit at the wedding, yet somehow she was never the one dancing with the groom.
    • Example B: He kept winning the pitches but never got the promotion, until a colleague quietly mentioned the one habit costing him the room. [L0330.044]
  • Reward first: show the result of an achievement, such as extra money, status or praise, so people scared off by the work are attracted by the outcome. It moves attention from effort to reward. Use a short dialogue or story where the reward is the central moment.
    • Example A: Mom, I got the raise, and the extra money is my treat for you, all thanks to the course I did at night.
    • Example B: You said you could never learn the piano, so here is the first song I played at the family dinner after six weeks. [L0330.045]

Matching the headline to the visual

  • Warm-up engagement headline: a short, one-sentence headline that points the viewer's attention to the animal in the photo and invites them to look or react. The headline matches the image directly, so the viewer understands the ad in a second and is more likely to start a message.
    • Example 1: 'Meet this sleepy little cat'
    • Example 2: 'Check out this happy rescue pup' [L0614.013]

Specificity and numbers

  • Specific-number headlines: replace vague promises with exact amounts, timeframes and counts that the offer really delivers. Turning a general claim into a precise, believable figure makes the copy more definite and memorable, as in the difference between 'earn more money' and a specific weekly income you can document. [L0340.068]
  • Numbers that show real performance are allowed and useful in headlines, provided you can support them with evidence. Do not make specific results promises you cannot back up. Older advice (2021-22). [L0416.013]
  • Quantified performance pattern: [specific number or multiplier] + [performance metric the buyer cares about] + [product name or model]. A precise, comparable number creates curiosity and invites reading on, while staying clear of unsupported health or income results. The number must be backed by evidence from the manufacturer or testing. Older (2021-22). Figures below are placeholders and need real evidence.
    • 'Cut Setup Time by 80% With the Tool-Free Shelf Kit'
    • 'Hold 3x More Gear With the Compact Travel Pack' [L0416.024]
  • Alternative angles when the primary claim is risky: if the primary problem cannot be used as a headline because of claim risk, use the secondary problems (for example convenience, time saved or money saved) or the biggest feature or unique mechanism. Use the one you can prove with evidence. Older (2021-22). [L0416.020]
  • Labeled numbered list: present a large, specific count of reasons, facts or flaws under a memorable label, then reuse that label across every channel so it becomes shorthand for your argument.
    • Formula: '[Number] [specific things] that [outcome], named [label].'
    • Examples: '27 Hidden Costs of Cheap Garden Hose, the Hose Audit' and '12 Overlooked Safety Checks in Kitchen Remodels, the Kitchen Check.' [L0340.088]
  • Credibility claim: a bold headline stating a specific, checkable claim such as a rating, a customer count, a ranking, or a 'number one' position in a named market. A precise number is more believable than a vague superlative, so the claim gains trust immediately.
    • New example 1: Rated 4.9 by 2,000+ owners in Canada, the back brace physiotherapists recommend most.
    • New example 2: Trusted by 5,000+ home cooks, the sharpest knife set under 60 USD. [L0648.062]

Audience-specific headlines

  • Two-audience headline: name each audience segment in capital letters, then ask a question that promises a specific, visible result for that segment.
    • Formula: 'SEGMENT A! Want [specific result]? SEGMENT B! Want [different specific result]?'
    • Examples: 'TRAIL HIKERS! Want boots that stay dry through a full day of rain? COMMUTERS! Want a bag that fits a 15-inch laptop and a lunch?' [L0340.083]
  • Audience-specific question: name the audience's exact pain point as a question, then give the specific benefit with a measurable detail.
    • Example 1: "Are you sick of dog hair on your bed? This microfiber brush lifts hair in half the time."
    • Example 2: "Tired of sweat in your running shoes? This mesh insole dries 2 times faster after every run." [L0552.034]
  • Older (2021-22): lead the headline with the main problem your business solves that the customer is likely experiencing, such as sizing, installation or delivery questions for products that often cause hesitation. Use other common pain points if they fit better. [L0488.008]
  • Older (2021-22): question headline naming one specific purchase hesitation, such as size, installation or delivery time, so the viewer sees their own doubt in the ad. Formula: a question about the hesitation, followed by body copy offering direct contact for an answer. An open doubt draws attention in a crowded feed and makes the call to action feel useful.
    • "Not Sure Which Size Fits Your Garden?"
    • "Wondering How Long Delivery Takes Before You Buy?" [L0488.017]

Schwartz-style headline tactics

Breakthrough Advertising tactics (template)

Breakthrough Advertising headline tactics (Eugene Schwartz), used to strengthen headlines. Write one headline per tactic that follows the tactic with your own product and benefit, keeping a number, comparison, or demonstration where the tactic calls for it.
1. Measure the size of the claim: put a number on the claim (e.g. '[Result] in just one [action]').
2. Measure the speed of the claim: state how fast the result comes (e.g. 'Get [result] in under [time]').
3. Compare the claim to an unnamed rival: state a comparison to other leading options (e.g. 'Collect twice as much [thing] as other leading brands').
4. Metamorphose the claim: turn the product into a vivid image (e.g. 'like a magnet').
5. Sensitize the claim: appeal to the senses (e.g. imagining running a hand over a clean surface).
6. Demonstrate the claim: show the result visually (e.g. 'See the pile of [thing] collected from a single cushion').
7. Dramatize the claim: contrast the bad outcome with the good one (e.g. 'Instead of a couch covered in [thing], find a clean space').
8. Remove limitations from the claim: say it works on all variants (e.g. all types of the item or all surfaces).
9. Associate the claim with values or people: connect it to what the audience values or who they are.
10. Show how much work the claim does in detail: explain the mechanism that delivers the result.
11. State the claim as a question: pose the problem as a question the reader answers yes to.
12. Offer information on how to accomplish the claim: give a clear how-to in the copy.
13. Tie authority to the claim: link to a respected publication, expert, or award.
14. Show before and after: display the transformation.
15. Stress how new the claim is: present the product as the latest technology.
16. Stress the exclusivity of the claim: be among the first to use it, so the reader feels status.

[L0515.019]

Tactic examples

  • Measure the size: state a specific, measurable result so the reader can judge it. The number makes the claim concrete and easier to believe.
    • Examples: 'Our brush removes 99% of pet hair in one sweep.' 'Cut your monthly grocery spend by 15% with one shared list.' [L0516.038]
  • Measure the speed: name a short time frame in which the result happens, so the reader sees how quickly the problem goes away.
    • Examples: 'Say goodbye to pet hair on your furniture in under five minutes.' 'Clear a clogged drain in under two minutes with no chemicals.' [L0516.039]
  • Metamorphose the claim: describe the product as a different, vivid object that performs the function in an exaggerated way, which makes the mechanism memorable.
    • Examples: 'This brush is a magnet for every hair on your bed.' 'This mop is a sponge that soaks up spills in a single pass.' [L0516.040]
  • Sensitize the claim: attach the reader's senses to the outcome, such as touch, sight or smell, so the benefit feels physical.
    • Examples: 'Imagine running your hand over your couch and feeling nothing but smooth fabric.' 'Picture a kitchen that smells fresh the moment you walk in.' [L0516.041]
  • Demonstrate the claim: show a concrete visual scene of the result in a small, specific amount of time, so the reader can picture the proof.
    • Examples: 'See a month of dust lifted from a single sofa cushion in one pass.' 'Watch one swipe collect all the crumbs from a car seat.' [L0516.042]
  • New mechanism question: name the unique mechanism, add curiosity, and tie it to the problem outcome in the form 'Could [new mechanism] really [outcome]?' Keep the mechanism in the headline so the reader sees what makes the product different.
    • Examples: 'Could this heated insole really end cold feet on early morning runs?' 'Could a single tap really lift every stain from your sofa?' [L0516.043]

Objection and benefit headlines

  • Turn the biggest objection into the headline: name it openly, then explain why it is a benefit.
    • Example one: Not the lightest bag on the market, because it's built to carry a laptop and a week of groceries without tearing.
    • Example two: Not cheap, and we're fine with that, because it lasts through years of daily use. [L0378.064]
  • Benefit-led headline: name the outcome the buyer wants in their own words, then the product quietly solves the underlying problem.
    • Example one: Get your old routine back, and stop wasting evenings on the wrong fix.
    • Example two: Get the weekend you were promised, and leave the chores to a system that already runs itself. [L0378.075]

The 38 ways to strengthen a headline

Thirty-eight ways to strengthen a headline by how the claim is worded, in the original order:

  1. Measure the size of the claim with an exact number.
  2. Measure the speed of the claim with a time figure.
  3. Compare the claim against a rival, a higher price or an older method.
  4. Express the claim as a metaphor.
  5. Make the reader feel, smell, taste, touch, see or hear the claim.
  6. Demonstrate the claim with one prime example.
  7. Dramatize the claim or its result as a short scene.
  8. State the claim as a paradox.
  9. Remove a usual limitation from the claim.
  10. Attach the claim to a value or a person the prospect wants to be like.
  11. Show in detail how much work the claim does.
  12. State the claim as a question.
  13. Offer information on how to get the result.
  14. Attach an authority to the claim.
  15. Use a before-and-after contrast.
  16. Stress that the claim is new.
  17. Stress that the claim is exclusive to you.
  18. Turn the claim into a challenge the reader can test.
  19. Present the claim as a short first-person case statement.
  20. Condense the claim by naming your product as the replacement for the old one.
  21. Symbolize the claim with a parallel image instead of a direct measure.
  22. Connect the mechanism to the claim inside the headline.
  23. Startle the reader by contradicting how he thinks the mechanism works.
  24. Connect the need and the claim in one line.
  25. Offer information inside the ad as the hook.
  26. Turn the claim or the need into a short case story.
  27. Name the problem or need precisely.
  28. Warn the reader about the risks of not using the product.
  29. Emphasize the claim through phrasing, by splitting it into two sentences or repeating part of it.
  30. Show the claim is easy by pointing to a limit everyone already accepts as overcome.
  31. State the difference that sets the product apart.
  32. Surprise the reader into seeing that a former limit no longer applies.
  33. Address the people who cannot buy the product.
  34. Address the prospect directly by the role or rank he holds.
  35. Dramatize how hard it was to make the product.
  36. Say the claim sounds too good, to meet the reader's doubt head-on.
  37. Challenge the prospect's limiting belief about himself.
  38. Turn the claim into a question and answer it.

Crowded markets and unique features

  • Crowded-market headline: name the unique feature in the headline when many similar products already exist. The feature is the hook.
    • Example: 'Unique microfiber static head removes more hair than any other brush' [L0353.041]
  • Name the unique mechanism or feature so the buyer can tell the product apart. The pattern names a unique mechanism, a concrete result and a short time frame.
    • Examples: 'Patented magnetic head pulls hair out of every fabric in one pass.' 'Dual-layer cleaning pad lifts pet fur from sofas in seconds.' [L0353.042]

Lead types

Choosing the lead: direct or indirect

  • The more aware and ready the prospect, the more direct the lead should be. The less aware or more skeptical the prospect, the more indirect the lead should be. Direct leads fail when the audience is skeptical or does not yet believe a solution exists. [L0756.006]
  • Direct approaches work well when the product is easy to understand, the promise is large but easily accepted, the deal or guarantee is exceptional, the customer already trusts the seller, or a product improvement was already awaited by the market. [L0756.007]
  • Indirect approaches work better when the customer knows little about the seller, the product needs explanation, the customer is jaded and skeptical, a timely news connection is strong, or the product is being reinvented or elevated. [L0756.008]
  • Being clever or coy is not the same as being indirect. A lead that entertains without ever connecting to the product often leaves the prospect unclear about what is being sold. [L0756.009]
  • A direct lead with a skeptical or highly aware-but-jaded prospect, such as a car buyer or someone who has heard too many similar offers, usually fails. Shift toward the indirect side to rebuild trust first. [L0756.010]

Promise lead

  • Use the Promise Lead for prospects who are mostly aware and almost ready to buy, and for prospects sitting on the fence. It opens with the product's biggest and most relevant claim and usually ties the claim to a core desire. [L0756.027]
  • A Promise Lead must target the prospect's core desire. The promise should be original, relevant, bold but believable, and supported by proof at least as strong as the claim. Oversized promises that cannot be proven make skeptics disbelieve the whole message. [L0756.028]
  • To find a strong promise: first check what competitors have already promised, then identify what the prospect expects, and finally identify what the product can truly deliver without overselling. Under-delivery from an over-sold product makes the product fail faster. [L0756.029]
  • When a promise seems too large and readers become skeptical, reduce the claim, turn it into a question that addresses the doubt, or shrink it to a believable size. Scaling a claim back can make it more credible, as a Sears battery demonstration with three or four cars was more believable than one with ten. [L0756.032]
  • Promise lead pattern: state the product's biggest benefit as the headline, add a specific time frame or result, then prove it.
    • Formula: '[Desired result] in [short time frame], without [common objection].'
    • New example: 'Clear, even skin in 14 days, without the redness that harsher serums cause.'
    • New example: 'Sleep through the night in one week, with no sleep aids or alarms needed.'
    • The time frame makes the promise concrete, and the second clause pre-empts the common objection. [L0756.033]
  • Promise leads usually fail with skeptics and highly unaware prospects, because a bare promise sounds like one more sales claim. They work best with people on the fence who just need a nudge. [L0756.034]

Problem-solution lead

  • Show empathy first, linger on the problem only long enough to earn a commitment to read on, then offer hope of a relevant solution. Lingering too long without offering hope makes the copy feel like it exploits fear. [L0756.036]
  • Name the deep emotional worry in one phrase (for example guilt, shame, fear of running out of money), then offer relief.
    • Formula: '[Painful situation]? Here is what ends it.'
    • New example: 'Worried your savings won't last past 75? Here is how to build a withdrawal plan that keeps paying out.'
    • New example: 'Tired of feeling like the only one who can't keep up at work? Here is a 10-minute routine that closes the gap.'
    • It works because it names the feeling and immediately gives a path to relief. [L0756.037]

Question and inversion leads

  • Questions that state a problem with an almost inevitable yes answer from the right audience are a strong way to start a conversation. The word 'these' in a question like 'Do You Make These Mistakes?' can make the problem feel like it applies to the reader personally. [L0756.040]
  • Inversion (solution-problem): promise the benefit of escaping or avoiding a situation before naming the problem.
    • Formula: 'Enjoy [benefit] without [problem].'
    • New example: 'Keep your savings growing without the stress of market swings.'
    • New example: 'Get the energy you need for your day without the afternoon crash.'
    • The reader senses the solution first, and the problem is framed as something they can avoid. [L0756.041]

Invented-term problem

  • Give the problem a new name that makes sense of the negative situation, then explain it so the reader learns what it means and what to do about it.
    • Formula: '[Coined term]: Is it costing you [specific loss]?'
    • New example: 'Pantry drift: Is it quietly adding $40 a month to your grocery bill?'
    • New example: 'Comparison fatigue: Is it making your savings feel smaller than they are?'
    • A new label feels like insider knowledge and creates curiosity about the cure. [L0756.042]

Invitation lead

  • Frame the offer as an exclusive membership or invitation so the prospect feels included, and reveal the deal details less openly. It works for products with a long tradition of quality, clubs, societies and luxury items, but only when the offer really has exclusive value.
    • Pattern: 'You are invited to join [exclusive group] and receive [valuable access] before anyone else.'
    • New example: 'You are invited to join the Alpine Pantry Circle, where members receive first access to limited-batch olive oil each season.'
    • Exclusion creates desire to be let in. [L0756.025]

Secret lead

  • Use a Secret Lead when a product has an element the customer does not know, or when its known qualities can be renamed or reframed to feel new. Start the secret in the headline and keep the teaser going through the copy. [L0756.043]
  • The secret must be both intriguing and beneficial, and tied to a major benefit of the product quickly enough that the reader understands why the secret matters. [L0756.044]
  • Do not disclose the secret during the lead. Revealing it too soon removes the curiosity that keeps the prospect reading. For many Secret Leads, the secret is held back until the prospect buys the product. [L0756.045]
  • Specificity overcomes the skepticism that secrets trigger. Exact figures, dates, named sources and worked examples make a teaser feel credible even when the secret is not yet explained. [L0756.046]
  • Provide clues as the copy progresses: restate the headline in different words, add dates, name credible figures or celebrities, or tell the reader what the secret is not. The prospect feels closer to the answer while the actual answer stays hidden. [L0756.047]
  • Secret lead pattern: a headline combining a credible claim with an unexplained term, a number, or a closed-then-reopened opportunity, with the secret deferred to the body copy.
    • Formula: '[Unfamiliar term] That [specific result] for [specific investment]'
    • New example: 'The Harvest Ledger Method That Turned a $200 Pantry Budget Into $1,800 in Savings.'
    • New example: 'The Quiet Lever Behind Consistent 9-Minute Morning Routines That Actually Stick.'
    • The reader must read on to learn what the term means and how the result was achieved. [L0756.048]
  • Transubstantiation means taking something ordinary and reframing it as something special, carrying over the emotional appeal of a more exciting example. Neologizing means giving a familiar concept a new name to create intrigue. Use one of these when the product's qualities are already known. [L0756.049]

Proclamation lead

  • A Proclamation Lead opens with a bold, emotionally compelling statement that is relevant and nearly unbelievable, usually in the headline. The copy then provides evidence that makes the implied promise believable, and the product is revealed only after the proof is shown. [L0756.050]
  • Use it for consumer products when the prospect is unaware of the product, and for business-to-business audiences already aware of the product who could be alarmed by a fact that threatens their current tools or practices. [L0756.051]
  • Proclamation pattern: an imperative or declarative headline implying a serious consequence, followed by a claim the reader wants to check.
    • Formula: '[Imperative] or [consequence]' or '[Outdated tool] Is Obsolete.'
    • New example: 'Stop Using Your Old Budget Sheet. It Is Costing You Up to 30% More Than You Realize.'
    • New example: 'Your 2019 Shipping Calculator Is Obsolete: Here Is What Changed in Carrier Rates.'
    • It startles, creates a worry about the status quo, and promises a clear way forward. [L0756.052]

Editorial and advertorial headlines

  • Editorial headlines should be benefit-driven, combining a news-style or attention-grabbing claim with a benefit or curiosity element. A plain product name or price in the headline weakens the editorial. [L0578.015]
  • Headline pattern: pair a surprising problem or bold claim with a benefit or curiosity hook, such as a benefit-driven news headline or an expert reveal. The headline decides whether the reader continues, so it must be interesting enough to keep them on the page.
    • Example 1: 'Why Millions of Commuters Wake Up Stiff and the Surprising Pillow Fix Experts Recommend'
    • Example 2: 'Vet Reveals the Simple Daily Habit That Keeps Dogs Moving Well Into Their Senior Years' [L0578.013]
  • Numbered advertorial headline: [number] reasons [target audience] [keeps failing at, or is missing out on, a specific problem], [promised fix or time frame]. The number signals a quick, scannable read, and naming a specific problem lets the reader see themselves in it before the product appears.
    • Example: 'Four reasons your sourdough keeps collapsing, and the fix takes one weekend.'
    • Example 2: 'Three mistakes most home office workers make with desk lighting, and how to fix each in minutes.' [L0552.049]

Meta titles and descriptions

  • The meta description and headline are the ad for an article in Google search results, so they must sell the click. Use persuasive sales copy in these fields, which is a different skill from writing article body text. [L0382.031]
  • Headline and meta description formula: name the exact topic people search for, add a concrete benefit or a question the searcher wants answered, then add a credibility cue such as the brand or a specific detail. Search results are ads that must win a click against competitors, so the searcher should see that the page answers their exact need.
    • Example 1 (new): Shopify vs. WooCommerce: Which Platform Fits a $5,000 Monthly Budget?
    • Example 2 (new): Should an Online Store Show a Phone Number? What Shoppers Expect in 2026 [L0382.033]

Other title patterns

  • Personalised gift-book titles: replace a generic title with a short phrase about the relationship between the giver and the recipient, such as what they share or how they feel about each other. Keep it short enough for the cover.
    • New examples: 'Two Hearts, One Shared Story' and 'Counting Every Reason, Twice' [L0333.006]

Testing headlines

  • Signs and headlines do not need to be artistic, but they must say the right thing. Test variations until a clear winner appears. [L0378.077]
  • A fruit stand split-tested signs and found that a short, direct line such as 'Picked Today' outperformed more generic phrases. Daily income rose from hundreds of dollars to more than $1,000 with the winning sign. [L0378.076]
  • Check each headline device against three jobs. A device should do at least one of these: strengthen the claim with more detail or measurement, make a familiar claim fresh by changing its angle, or pull the reader into the body copy by promising information or asking a question. A device that does none of these adds nothing. [L0330.097]

Headline Principles

  • A headline selects the people who could be interested and speaks to them directly, so the reader can tell at a glance whether the ad concerns them. Write each headline for one specific audience, not for everyone. [L1261.055]
  • Clever, blind, or amusing headlines may attract many more people, but most are not buyers, so the ad's true response falls. Headlines that hide the offer or product do not reach the right people. [L1261.056]
  • The headline has the largest effect on return. The same ad body run with different headlines can differ by five to ten times in response. Test several headlines against the same body copy and keep the one with the best cost per customer. [L1261.057]
  • One advertiser's keyed record for a single product included nearly two thousand headlines, and results varied enormously even when the body copy was almost identical. Headline testing is worth the effort. [L1261.058]
  • A headline counts as copy because its only job is to persuade the reader to continue with the rest of the content. The same headline principles apply to blog posts and similar content, although general educational content writing is a separate discipline. (Older: source 2021-22.) [L1460.009]
  • In a letter, headlines do most of the selling, so they must be as strong as a print ad headline. Place the headline above the salutation or between the salutation and body, in bold or a boxed panel, because it gets the reader past the first glance. [L1370.020]
  • The main headline of a page should be bold and clearly visible, at most one to two lines. It can be a USP, a bold claim about the product, or a benefit-driven statement placed in the first section of the page. [L0916.006]
  • Open a landing page with a large, bold headline that states the main benefit or desired outcome. Visitors move toward pleasant outcomes and away from painful ones, so the headline should show the gain first. [L1207.002]

The Four U's

  • A headline must carry a lot of information early, because most readers never read beyond it. Its purpose is to grab attention and convey the Four U's: Urgent, Unique, Useful, and Ultra-Specific. The four do not all need to be present, but if two or more are missing, the headline will likely fail to hold attention. [L1356.020]
  • Urgent: Usually conveyed by a date or time-limited prompt, such as Take action by December 21 or Sale ends this Friday, or indirectly through FOMO when the big emotion is strong. Urgency is the simplest U and can be dropped if the other three are present. [L1356.021]
  • Unique: Non-negotiable. The headline must offer something the reader has never seen before. To find a unique angle, pick one specific element of the product or offer and emphasize it, such as a feature competitors do not highlight or a fresh framing. The revolution is not required; the unique element can be small, as long as the reader has not seen it. [L1356.023]
  • Useful: The headline must promise something the audience values. Research the ideal prospect, what topics they are currently curious about, and which benefits would make them keep reading. A headline built only on intrigue is not useful and fails the "What's in it for me?" test. [L1356.024]
  • Useful, tied to a hot topic: Tie the useful promise to a topic top of mind for the audience, such as a major news event or government policy, then add a concrete, useful benefit such as a money figure. The topic creates interest and the benefit creates usefulness. [L1356.025]
  • Ultra-Specific: Use uncommon, oddly precise numbers rather than round figures, for example $10,251 instead of $10,000, 12.5 years younger instead of 10, or a 251% strength gain instead of 100%. Precise numbers imply the claim was tested and make it seem real. Be as specific as legally possible and do not lie. Ideally, the specific figure is backed by an example demonstrated in the lead or body. [L1356.022]

Specificity and Numbers

  • Specific measured claims beat general superlatives, because readers assume a precise figure reflects a real test. Formula: state the measured result, the test size or method behind it, and the concrete benefit. [L1261.059]
    • Stops pain in 30 seconds, measured on 400 users. [L1261.059]
    • Cuts drying time by 42 percent versus the leading brand. [L1261.059]
  • Pattern: a specific number from a real test or process turns a common claim into a fact that stands apart from rivals. Formula: name the common benefit, then add the exact figure that proves it. [L1261.080]
    • Lasts 9 hours on a single charge, tested across 60 days of use. [L1261.080]
    • Sets in 12 seconds, measured on 200 test batches. [L1261.080]
  • Solution-first property headline: How to Do Wonders With a Little Land! beat a conventional problem-solution headline by 40%. It also beat a flat promise headline, Two Acres and Security, by 75%. Start with the solution when the reader's problem is familiar and the product is clearly relevant. [L0756.071]
  • Problem-solution campaigns can also work at scale. In the Kimberly-Clark case below, they helped build a brand worth $1.6 billion, so the choice between problem-first and solution-first depends on whether the reader already knows the problem and sees the product as relevant. [L0756.067, L0756.071]

Finding the Angle

  • Concept headline from a production detail: a technical detail that explains why a product is special can become the headline concept. Ask the manufacturer how a feature is made and why competitors cannot copy it, since that answer often holds the strongest angle. Example: Laser Beam Digital Watch came from learning that the laser was the only way to seal the capsule. [L1366.083]
  • Curiosity from an unusual but true fact about how the product works invites the reader to learn more. Formula: state a surprising fact, then reveal the mechanism or benefit that explains it. [L1261.081]
    • Your morning coffee gives its kick two hours after you drink it. [L1261.081]
    • Each batch is filtered through a material that catches particles smaller than a human hair. [L1261.081]
  • Question about a surprising, specific unknown tied to the reader's trade or daily life. The reader must read the first line to get the answer. An unanswered question creates curiosity, and relevance to the reader's work keeps it from being mere entertainment. [L1369.117]
    • What is the one invoice error that quietly drains a small shop's cash every month? [L1369.117]
    • Why do some restaurants keep their regulars for ten years while others lose them after a single visit? [L1369.117]

Benefit Headline Formulas

  • Pattern: [desired outcome] + [novelty word] + [low-effort or fun word]. It promises the goal while removing the perceived effort and sacrifice. [L1207.004]
    • The simple new way to sleep through the night. [L1207.004]
    • The fun way to keep your dog active every day. [L1207.004]
  • Headline formula: combine the big desired outcome with a novelty word that signals something different from what the buyer already tried and failed, and an effort-reducing word that signals it is quick or easy. [L1209.003]
    • The fresh and easy way to build strength at home. [L1209.003]
    • The smarter way to learn a language in 10 minutes a day. [L1209.003]
  • Principle: novelty words such as "new" make an offer feel different from failed past attempts, which raises interest. Effort-reducing words such as "fun" or "easy" counter the common belief that reaching a goal is resource-intensive, slow, or unpleasant. Benefit headlines work best when they signal low effort. [L1207.003, L1209.004]
  • Pattern: [product] suitable for [broad group], [skill or experience] not required. It removes the fear of not being good enough. [L1207.012]

    • Works for every shoe size, no tools needed. [L1207.012]
  • Formula: [Specific measurable outcome] in [timeframe] [without the usual cost, effort, or risk]. A concrete outcome with a timeframe makes the big promise believable, and naming what is avoided removes the main objection. [L1365.014]

    • Earn a second income of $2,500 a month from a phone and a laptop, without building a website or holding inventory. [L1365.014]
  • Formula: Introduce a new [type of solution] that [unique claim] before [the point the market usually waits for]. The "new" element triggers attention, and the unique claim signals a breakthrough the reader has not seen before. [L1365.013]

    • A new breathing routine that lowers resting heart rate within ten days without setting foot in a gym. [L1365.013]
  • Product-page pattern: product name, then a USP or bold claim, then the emotional result the buyer gets. The page continues the feeling the ad created, so visitors do not lose the thread of their buying journey. [L0916.007]
    • Lightweight Standing Desk Mat: Stand for hours without the ache in your lower back. [L0916.007]
    • Compact Travel Blender: Fresh smoothies on the road, zero mess in your bag. [L0916.007]
  • Add a short semi-headline or benefit line directly after the product name that uses the same emotion as the ad creative. This keeps the visitor's experience consistent from ad to landing page. [L0916.008]

Product Image and Customer Language

Audience and Identification

Lead Types

  • Offer and promise elements can be combined with other lead types, because every sales piece needs them anyway. Most other combinations break the one-idea focus. A secret lead can be paired with a promise, for example a technique kept hidden while the headline promises a result, as long as one idea and one emotion drive the copy. [L0756.062]
  • Secret lead: In a typical secret lead, around 300 to 400 words go to illustrating the promise and building credibility before the secret is named. The secret itself stays withheld until the reader is emotionally ready to buy. [L0756.063]
  • Story lead: Testimonials, founder or expert bios, historical proof, and track records can all serve as the basis of a story lead. Story leads engage readers who do not know the seller or who distrust bold claims. Tell the story quickly and tie it back to the core promise of the promotion. A story lead can work directly or indirectly, depending on the audience. [L0756.064]
  • Indirect story opener: Describe two people who start from the same background and end up in different situations, with no product or offer mentioned until later. The reader keeps going to learn why the paths split, and the sale follows once interest and emotion are set.     • Two neighbors bought the same model of rain barrel; one has a full garden tank every summer, the other has a rusted lid and an empty yard. [L0756.065]
    • This works because the reader wants to know the cause of the difference, and the product becomes the answer. [L0756.065]
  • Underdog story headline: says someone was doubted for attempting something and then succeeded. It appeals to sympathy for the underdog and to the reader's own private doubts. [L1370.021]
    • They Said My Small Shop Would Never Last Past Winter, and It Just Hit Its Best Year. [L1370.021]
    • Everyone Laughed When I Asked for a Raise. Here Is What Happened Next. [L1370.021]
  • Who-else headline: a question or statement that implies many other people already know something the reader does not, creating curiosity and fear of missing out. [L1370.022]
    • Who Else Wants Their Kitchen Clean Without Scrubbing? [L1370.022]
    • Who Else Is Quietly Cutting Their Shipping Bill in Half? [L1370.022]
  • First-person story headline: starts with how something changed for the writer, followed by a dramatic result. It works best when the before and after are very different and the story has real stakes. [L1370.023]
    • How a Cheap Tripod Turned My Sales Around in One Month. [L1370.023]
    • How I Doubled My Repeat Customers Without Spending a Cent on Ads. [L1370.023]
  • Secrets, numbers, and warnings: "Secrets of" headlines promise insider knowledge, "N Ways to" headlines add a specific number to a how-to promise, and "Warning:" headlines grab attention for problem-solution copy. [L1370.026]
    • Secrets of Shops That Sell Out Every Monday. [L1370.026]
    • 17 Ways to Cut Your Packaging Costs. [L1370.026]
    • Warning: Your Current Supplier May Be Adding Hidden Fees. [L1370.026]
  • Promise and crowd headlines: "Give me X and I will Y" telegraphs a clear, short offer, and "Thousands now do X even though they Y" uses a crowd plus a surprising contrast to build belief. [L1370.027]
    • Give Me Ten Minutes a Day and I Will Show You How to Automate Your Orders. [L1370.027]
    • Thousands Now Sell Online Even Though They Never Studied Business. [L1370.027]
  • Promise-only check: remove the offer, price, and guarantee from the headline or opening promise, then read the bare promise. [L0756.073]
    1. Take the headline or opening promise and remove the offer, price, and guarantee.
    2. Read the bare promise and decide whether it still creates anticipation.
    3. If it does, use it as the opening for prospects who are on the fence or not ready for an offer yet.
    4. If it does not, make the promise more specific or more relevant to the core desire. [L0756.073]
  • Indirect leads carry specific risks: using an indirect angle when a direct offer would have worked, choosing an angle too subtle to connect back to the product, taking so long to reach the product that the reader gets bored, and writing something interesting that has nothing to do with the sale. Clever or coy copy that never ties to the product is not indirect selling and often leaves the prospect unclear about what is for sale. [L0756.074]

Offer and Sale Headlines

Testing and Adapting Headlines

  • Take a printed article, headline list, or product description. [L1343.036]
    1. Use a permanent marker to black out every word you do not want.
    2. Read the remaining words in order; they form a new headline, hook, or short piece of copy.

Case Studies

  • Kimberly-Clark and Kleenex: Kimberly-Clark had warehouses of cellucotton, a filter material originally made for gas masks, at the end of World War I. The company first sold Kleenex as a disposable replacement for towels used to remove cold cream. A 1925 magazine campaign promised no more dingy cold-cream towels, using celebrities to say it was how movie stars kept a pretty skin. Other ads tested uses such as handkerchiefs, coffee filters, and cleanup towels. A head researcher with chronic hay fever pushed the top copywriter to rewrite the ads with the handkerchief use up front, under headlines such as Don't Put a Cold In Your Pocket and No more sore noses.

12.10 Benefits, bullets and unique mechanism

Start from the Outcome the Customer Wants

  • Identify and emphasize the outcome the customer wants to achieve, not only the product's features or technical benefits. For a gaming keyboard, customers do not primarily want a wireless phone holder. They want to be sharper, beat competitors, keep a higher kill-to-death ratio, and outdo opponents. Build the ad's message around these desired outcomes first, then position the product as the solution. Outcome-focused ads drive desire and conversions; feature-focused ads do not. [L0067.045]
  • Lead with the main benefits of the product, meaning what the customer can do with it, rather than listing features first. [L0206.002]
  • Customers buy what a feature does for them, not the feature itself. A bare feature, such as a built-in charging station, has little meaning until the buyer sees the result it gives them. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0612.002]
  • Pair every relevant feature with a matching desire and state what it means for the customer in plain terms, such as saves time and effort or lets you stay relaxed. Focus on the few features that truly separate the product from competitors rather than listing everything. [L0516.003]
  • Each feature in a "what it does" list needs a matching meaning line that shows what changes in the customer's home, time, or comfort. A feature alone gives proof; the meaning line gives the reason to buy. Keep feature claims short and visible, and always state the effort saved, such as minimal effort or quick cleaning, because customers respond to less work as much as to results. [L0392.007]
  • Shoppers compare products by features, especially online with several tabs open, so features must still be listed. Omitting a real feature leads buyers to assume the product lacks it, which makes competitors look better. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0612.003]
  • Never leave the meaning of a feature for the reader to work out. Spell out exactly what it means for the buyer in a way they can picture. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0612.006]
  • Keep a technical term unchanged when it is the real name of the technology, but add a plain-language benefit after it. A technical name alone sounds impressive but means nothing to an uninformed buyer, so always follow it with its effect. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0612.007]
  • Quantified technical claims should show the result to the buyer. A carbon-ceramic low-EMF heater producing infrared heat 5x more effective than standard infrared sources is best written as: uses low-EMF carbon-ceramic heating that gives 5x more effective infrared heat than standard sources, so you spend less time in the sauna and more time on your day. The star footnote on a comparative claim such as 5x more effective than standard* must stay with the claim. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0612.008]
  • A benefit that only restates a quantified claim, such as five times more effective, is still too abstract. Add a power benefit that converts it into time, money, or daily life, because buyers often feel they lack time. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0612.009]
  • Non-unique features still belong in the list. Place unique features and the unique mechanism first, then include standard features that have real value, since buyers compare across products. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0612.010]

Feature-to-Benefit Patterns

  • Bullet formula: Feature + Benefit + Benefit of That Benefit. Example (Source C): KEEP THEM ON YOUR PAGES LONGER! [Feature: longer engagement] Bounce rates indicate engagement quality, boost engagement with these engineered questions to instantly increase conversion rates! [Benefit: more conversions] [L0196.006]
  • Interactive feature storytelling. Start with a benefit statement or what the product does, then describe features as vivid, interactive experiences the user will have. Why it works: it creates sensory imagery and shows the product in action, making it tangible and desirable.
    • These shoes learn your gait. Run, walk, or stand and watch them adjust cushioning automatically with every movement throughout your day.
    • This mug knows your temperature. Pour your coffee and feel it maintain the perfect warmth for hours, cooling just as you need it. [L0206.003]
  • Use case lead with feature checklist. Start with the primary benefit and emotional payoff, state relevant use cases, then list features with checkmarks. Why it works: it connects to customer needs immediately, then validates with clear, scannable proof.
    • Save 10 hours a week on admin work. Our scheduling software handles: ✅ Multi-timezone appointment sync ✅ Automated reminders and confirmations ✅ Integration with your calendar apps ✅ Client self-service booking.
    • Build muscle faster with precision. Our workout tracker includes: ✅ Form analysis with video feedback ✅ Personalized progression algorithms ✅ Recovery recommendations based on performance ✅ Offline workout access on mobile. [L0206.004]
  • Benefit-feature pairing with visual indicators. List each benefit with a supporting feature or proof point, using bullets or checkmarks, structured as benefit description + supporting detail. Why it works: scannable lists let different customers find their own reason to buy, and the pairing anchors abstract benefits in concrete features.
    • Instant relief + cooling technology; All-day comfort + invisible design
    • Reduces pain in 7 days + clinically tested; Works with your body + no medications required [L0313.003]
  • Effort-equivalence benefit pattern. Translate abstract benefits into concrete ones by comparing the effort or outcome of using the product with a familiar, quantifiable activity or baseline the audience already understands. Why it works: customers grasp benefits faster when they can imagine doing something they already know.
    • 20 minutes with our app saves the same time as a two-hour coffee meeting.
    • Using our template takes as much time as reading three emails. [L0321.042]
  • Dimensionalized benefit pattern: [feature] so that [practical benefit] and you can [emotional outcome]. It turns a feature into a practical result and then into a feeling the reader can picture, which is what triggers emotion. (Older source, 2021-22.)
    • Double-walled steel keeps lunch warm until noon so that you can skip the canteen queue and enjoy a relaxed break.
    • Lumbar support that adjusts to your posture so that your back stays comfortable after eight hours at the desk and you can leave work feeling fresh. [L0396.018]
  • Body copy order under a performance headline. Follow this fixed order: (1) a question that names what sets the product apart; (2) the specific material or technology behind it; (3) concrete equipment or spec details in plain language; (4) one practical convenience or comfort feature; (5) the control options. Every claim in the body copy must be backed by evidence, and health results are left out when the product is in a health or finance space. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0416.027]
    • Two new examples in the same pattern. All figures are placeholders that need real evidence.
    • What sets the Ridgeline 20V Drill apart from cheaper cordless drills? Its brushless motor and two-speed gearbox give steady torque on long runs. The 21-piece bit set and magnetic tray keep every job organized, and a battery indicator on the handle shows when to swap packs.
    • What makes the Lumen Desk Lamp different from other desk lights? Its adjustable 3,000 K to 6,500 K color range lets you match daylight at any hour. A touch dimmer and a USB-C port add convenience without extra setup. [L0416.027]

Power Bullets

  • Rule. State the feature first, then follow it with the benefit, and take the benefit further by describing a concrete picture of how it will be experienced in real terms. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0612.004]
  • Power bullet pattern. Name the feature, state the practical benefit it gives the buyer, then add a short emotional clause that addresses a likely fear or doubt (for example, reassurance for a buyer with no prior experience) and produces relief, security, or comfort. (Older source, 2021-22.)
    • Installs in about one hour, so you can enjoy it as soon as possible, even if you have never installed one before.
    • Heats up in 10 minutes, so you can start your session without waiting around while you get ready for your day. [L0612.005]
  • Steps to write a power bullet for a product feature. (Older source, 2021-22.)
    1. Write the feature exactly as the product has it, keeping the technical name if that is the real name of the technology.
    2. State the practical benefit that this feature gives the buyer.
    3. Describe in real terms what the buyer will be able to do or experience because of that benefit, so they can picture it.
    4. If the buyer may doubt or fear something about the feature, add a short clause that removes that doubt and gives relief, security, or comfort.
    5. Read the bullet and check that the buyer does not have to work out its meaning on their own. [L0612.013]
  • Example: reservations mode. A reservations mode that lets the buyer schedule the product to switch on up to 36 hours ahead can be written as a power bullet: it is ready at the time you choose, so you can step in after a long day without waiting for it to heat up, even if you plan the session a day and a half in advance. Keep the 36-hour figure exact, because a precise number makes the feature believable and lets the buyer picture the schedule. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0612.014]

Structuring the Feature List

  • Structure the feature list as bullets that each pair a feature with a benefit and an emotional image, not as bare feature statements. Keep the primary promise, story, and unique mechanism before the feature bullets. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0612.001]
  • A standard feature list that only names features, such as tablet controls, reservation modes, warranties, and installation time, gives the buyer nothing to picture and misses the chance to explain value. Avoid publishing bare feature lists on product pages and in ads. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0612.011]
  • A bulleted list is the preferred presentation for features and benefits because it separates them visually from body text and is easy to scan. Paragraphs with subheadings are an acceptable alternative if they suit the page. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0612.012]
  • Bullet points for technical products can be longer than usual. Break long bullets into shorter paragraphs inside the bullet so they remain readable; length itself is not a problem. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0461.011]
  • Extra features in a product copy's bullet list serve three jobs at once: they let the buyer compare the product with others on the market, they reinforce the power benefits, and they give the logical argument for the purchase. Choose each extra feature for at least one of these jobs. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0461.019]

Role of Bullets and Fascinations

  • The core function of bullets and fascinations is to keep prospects reading continuously through the entire message. If bullets bore the reader, they start skimming or stop reading, which directly causes lost sales. Weak links in bullet sections are often the deadliest vulnerability in copy because they are easy to spot and escape from. [L0068.001]
  • Bullets and fascinations do several jobs at once: they stimulate curiosity and intrigue, seduce the prospect to keep reading, build desire through revealed secrets, seize and hold attention, improve readability, add variety to prevent monotony, crush the headline block by generating dozens of headline possibilities, and create multiple selling angles on one piece of information. [L0068.002]
  • Dull, repetitive bullets or fascinations that lack intrigue or curiosity are a critical weak point. They cause the prospect to skim and scan instead of reading fully, which is one step away from the prospect putting the letter down or throwing it away. Systematically weed out weak links in bullet sections, because they cause loss of readership and ultimately loss of sales. [L0068.038]
  • Roughly 80% of promotions for certain products and markets are built almost entirely on strong bullets and fascinations rather than long body copy. This is particularly true for book promotions, large mail packages, and certain online promotions. [L0068.039]
  • One direct mail control featuring roughly 80% bullets and fascinations was mailed profitably for over 5 years, defeating previous controls, which indicates strong long-term performance of bullet-heavy copy. [L0068.040]
  • When you have a series of benefits, steps in a procedure, or other copy points, group them using bullets, numbers, letters, or other formatting. This adds clarity and impact to the message structure. Clayton often uses this approach to organize multiple related benefits or steps in a process. [L0068.043]

Research and Volume

  • Gene Schwartz research approach for bullet writing. (Source C)
    1. Read the source material multiple times, highlighting key points each time.
    2. Compile the notes into a comprehensive document (60-70 pages of distilled information is typical).
    3. Organize the research information thematically.
    4. Begin filling in the copy structure, creating bullets and fascinations as they naturally emerge from understanding the material. [L0068.034]
  • Parris Lampropoulos selection approach.
    1. Write approximately 700 fascinations covering every shocking, fascinating, and emotionally stirring piece of information in the research.
    2. Select only the strongest approximately 100 for the final package (a 7:1 ratio).
    3. This selection guarantees exceptional variety and quality in the bullet sections, preventing the reader from skimming due to mediocre bullets.
    4. Arrange the selected fascinations throughout the package in the most important positions: front cover, cover deck, and back cover teasers. [L0068.035]
  • Example of the 7:1 ratio. Parris Lampropoulos writes approximately 700 fascinations during research and writing, then selects approximately 100 for the final package. This ensures only exceptional material makes it into the final copy and creates substantial variety in the bullet sections, making them more intriguing and enjoyable to read throughout the promotion. [L0068.041]
  • Write many more bullets than you will use. The elite strategy is to write 7x more bullets than will appear in the final draft (for example, 700 written fascinations, selecting down to 100 for the promotion). This ensures only exceptional bullets make it into the final copy, selected from the cream of the crop rather than mixing exceptional and mediocre bullets. [L0068.032]
  • Archive what works. Create an archive of every bullet or fascination that performs well or generates strong response. When similar bullets are needed for different products, audiences, or platforms, check the archive first. High-performing bullets often work across applications when the core benefit remains relevant to the audience. [L0068.033]

Quality Checks for Each Bullet

  • The Makepeace Makeover: three-step bullet improvement.

    1. Add specificity by replacing vague claims with exact numbers, mechanisms, or credibility signals.
    2. Add intrigue and curiosity by choosing one of the 21 bullet formulas that creates tension, questions, or hidden information.
    3. Add benefit clarity by ensuring the reader immediately understands why this information matters to his life or business.

    Attack each piece of information from 2-3 different angles using different formulas, then select the strongest version. [L0068.031]

  • Specificity separates believable bullets from generic claims. Numbers, percentages, and specific mechanisms signal research and analysis. How to profit from stocks is flat and unconvincing. 307% profit from gold stocks using Source B Buffett's Bull Market X-Factor includes a mechanism (the named strategy), specificity (307%, not up to 500%), and credibility (a referenced expert). [L0068.024]

  • Name the mechanism in bullets. Revealing the precise mechanism by which the prospect will achieve the benefit (a proprietary system, a specific nutrient, a technology, a supplier relationship, or a process) is very powerful in saturated markets. It differentiates the claim by showing a unique means or system, and it introduces benefit, credibility, and intrigue at once. [L0068.025]

  • Emotional triggers allow less proof. When an emotional trigger aligns with what the prospect desperately wants (sexual desire, financial security, fear of missing out), a bullet can work with significantly less proof and specificity than normal, because desire overrides skepticism. Balance this with sufficient specificity in the other bullets of the same section. [L0068.027]

  • Find the emotional goldmines. Identify the prospect's core emotional desires or fears, such as financial security, sexual desire, or fear of missing out, and build bullets that target those emotional centers for maximum resonance. [L0068.047]

  • Gut-level filter. Use gut-level, greed-tickling impact as the filter when selecting bullets for the final copy. A bullet that creates an immediate visceral sense of desirability has significantly higher conversion power. [L0068.048]

  • Use colloquial language and strong verbs. Colloquial expressions and strong action verbs communicate more meaning in fewer words than formal language, which makes bullets more impactful and memorable. [L0068.046]

  • No exclamation marks. Exclamation marks signal hype and decrease response rates in bullet copy. Use other punctuation, such as colons, which boost response by 50% or more. [L0068.044]

Fascination Formulas

Each formula below appears with its pattern and examples. Use the formulas to write many variants of the same information, then select the strongest.

Curiosity and hidden knowledge

  • "How to" bullet. The bullet works because prospects are naturally curious about procedural information, especially when it is tied to their deepest desires or pain points. Open with the benefit-related action, not just a generic process.
    • How to source inventory without tying up working capital for 90 days, using the payment-term negotiation script that works with 8 out of 10 tier-one suppliers.
    • How to prevent competitor price undercutting by positioning your brand so customers ignore price-focused competitors entirely. [L0068.003]
  • "Secret to" bullet. Reveals non-obvious information positioned as a closely guarded secret. It works because prospects believe they have discovered something most people do not know. It must be based on genuinely uncommon information and varied carefully to avoid overuse.
    • The secret to 40% repeat orders from first-time buyers: it has nothing to do with product quality.
    • The little-known secret that separates $50K-per-month shops from $500K shops: it is one email sequence that reverses abandonment decisions. [L0068.004]
  • "Why" bullet. Builds intrigue by promising to reveal the reason or cause behind something, implying that understanding why will meaningfully change the prospect's life. Both the reason and the benefit of knowing it must be directly valuable to the prospect.
    • Why most dropshippers hit a $100K ceiling and never break through (and why understanding this single obstacle decides if you are stuck or scaling).
    • Why customers who receive tracking updates spend 35% more on their next order, and what you are missing if you do not tell them. [L0068.005]
  • "What" bullet. Works in two ways: it either gives specific action instructions to avoid a problem, or it reveals elusive valuable information not commonly shared. Both increase perceived value while teasing the answer.
    • What you must include in supplier contracts that protects you from the 12 most common vendor betrayals.
    • What your fulfillment partner doesn't want you to know about how they handle damage claims, and how knowing this saves you $3K-8K monthly. [L0068.006]
  • "Gimmick" bullet. Assigns a creative name to a specific idea extracted from research, combining a benefit with specificity and memorable framing. It makes a technique feel like a discovered thing with a name, which increases perceived sophistication.
    • The little-known supplier health score that predicts six months in advance which vendors will fail you or degrade quality.
    • The competitive pressure point: the exact moment when your competitor will need to raise prices due to supplier costs, giving you two weeks of market advantage. [L0068.013]
  • "Sneaky" bullet. Reveals something hidden, guarded, or deliberately obscured by a group, information that competitors or suppliers do not want revealed. Use it selectively so it does not seem contrived; it suggests a conspiracy element.
    • The sneaky way suppliers hide quality degradation in cost optimizations that your factory inspector never catches, until customers start returning product.
    • Sneaky fulfillment tricks most 3PLs use that make their performance metrics look better while customer satisfaction actually drops. [L0068.014]
  • "Single" bullet. Works when you have one benefit that exceeds all others and you have proof to back it. It positions one exclusive or most powerful possibility.
    • The single highest-impact metric for predicting which customers will buy again: customer acquisition cost in week 2 of purchase, which predicts repeat rate with 87% accuracy.
    • The single most important factor in supplier selection that determines 80% of your margin variance: it is not price but response speed on urgent issues. [L0068.023]
  • "Truth" bullet. Demystifies a controversial, confusing, or commonly misunderstood issue by offering a clear resolution. Prospects enjoy reading about contradictions being clarified.
    • The truth about influencer partnerships: micro-influencers (5K-50K followers) with 8-12% engagement deliver 3x the ROAS of mega-influencers claiming higher reach.
    • THE TRUTH ABOUT DROPSHIPPING PROFIT: most calculations hide fulfillment complexity. Here is how to measure real margin after every cost is included. [L0068.021]

Fear and danger

  • "What NEVER" bullet. Alerts the prospect to a mistake or danger, then promises information that protects them from making it. It builds curiosity about the hidden mistake while triggering fear-based motivation.
    • What NEVER to do with your best-performing product when your supplier suggests a minor update: one founder lost 60% of revenue by ignoring this warning.
    • What NOT to say to customers when shipping is delayed: the wrong approach loses 40% of repeat customers, while the right one loses only 8%. [L0068.007]
  • "WARNING" bullet. Alerts the prospect to a danger or threat, triggering fear, and must pair the threat with proof that you have the solution. Variations such as CAUTION, ALERT, or DANGER have the same effect.
    • WARNING: your supplier's quality standard changed last month, and your customers are seeing it in unboxing videos even though you have not noticed it yet.
    • ALERT: the shipping carrier you selected is about to raise rates 18% in Q2. Here is the exact timing and how to lock in today's pricing before the deadline. [L0068.011]

Questions and challenged assumptions

  • "Right...WRONG!" bullet. Plays on the prospect's assumptions about something they think they understand, then immediately debunks the assumption with the correct information. It works because everyone wants to know they have been wrong about something, with a strong benefit attached.
    • Most dropshippers think faster shipping guarantees loyalty? Wrong. Our data shows speed barely registers. Here is what actually determines repeat buys.
    • You think supplier payment terms don't matter for 30-day inventory cycles? Wrong. Here is how to turn terms into your biggest competitive advantage. [L0068.010]
  • "Are you...?" bullet. Asks the prospect about something you strongly believe he is already doing or experiencing, so he mentally answers yes. This builds momentum and agreement before you deliver the benefit, and it creates credibility because the prospect nods. Variations: Do you...?, Does your...?, Is your...?
    • Are you pricing like a commodity seller instead of a brand, and leaving 40% margin on every sale?
    • Are you using the same supplier for products in completely different categories when one small change could cut costs 25%? [L0068.012]
  • "Specific Question" bullet. Opens with an intriguing question of importance to the prospect, then offers the solution either in the bullet itself or in the product being sold. It must include credibility, benefit, intrigue, and curiosity.
    • Do you know that only 8% of dropshippers ever negotiate payment terms with suppliers, even though this decision predicts success more than product selection?
    • Have you discovered that the #1 reason for chargebacks isn't fraud but customer misalignment about product details, and this one email prevents 70% of chargebacks? [L0068.017]
  • "If...Then" bullet. Engages the prospect by inviting him to meet a simple requirement or asking whether he is experiencing something specific. He becomes interested in whether he meets it. The second half reveals the benefit that follows if he does.
    • If your supplier uses 30-day lead times, you are missing the first two weeks of every trend cycle, while competitors with 14-day vendors already have 200+ sales.
    • If you are losing customers at the cart stage, the problem isn't shipping cost. It is that you are revealing it too late in the buying journey. [L0068.018]

Direct, comparative, and benefit-first

  • "Statement of Interest + Benefit" bullet. Opens with an exciting or intriguing fact, then immediately follows with a strong benefit statement that explains why it matters. It often combines a factual lead with a how-to or why structure.
    • Your competitors are already using this supplier. Here is how to partner directly with them at their lowest-tier pricing.
    • Facebook's algorithm just changed how it prioritizes video content. Here is exactly how to structure your ads to capture the new traffic window. [L0068.015]
  • "Direct Benefit" bullet. Makes a powerful claim in the opening line (usually with an action verb) that instantly promises the benefit. The second line deepens, proves, or adds dimensions to that benefit.
    • ELIMINATE slow-moving inventory: here is how to validate demand before ordering a single unit.
    • STOP losing customers at the upsell stage: the one change to your offer sequence that increases order value 45%. [L0068.016]
  • "PLUS" bullet. Adds greed and momentum. It is one of the other formulas combined with PLUS at the beginning, promising additional benefits beyond the main claim. It works best at the end of a bullet list to finish with power and accumulated desire.
    • PLUS how to turn returns into repeat purchases that increase customer lifetime value by 120% instead of losing the customer.
    • PLUS my complete supplier database, already vetted for quality, communication, and margin, organized by category and minimum order. [L0068.008]
  • "Number" bullet. Groups multiple ways of doing something, multiple secrets, or multiple reasons why something will happen, condensing related information into one statement. The reader perceives more value when learning multiple solutions at once.
    • The 5 overlooked sources of repeat customer revenue that don't require building email lists.
    • Seven supplier negotiation leverage points that are in plain sight but never used, because most entrepreneurs don't know what to look for. [L0068.009]
  • "When" bullet. Promises a benefit at a very specific time, implying a guaranteed result if the prospect acts at that moment. The prospect wants to know exactly when he can expect the benefit.
    • When you update product photos on Friday evenings, the platform algorithm serves them with full power by Monday morning, capturing the week's traffic window.
    • When you negotiate supplier terms before inventory arrives, you control cash flow for 60 days. Waiting until after arrival costs you 30% of that control. [L0068.019]
  • "Quickest, Easiest" bullet. Reveals the fastest, easiest, or simplest way to achieve a benefit, tapping into the desire for instant gratification and minimal effort.
    • The easiest way to identify trending products in your niche without paying for expensive research: use your competitor's Shopify code and social proof.
    • The quickest way to recover abandoned carts without hiring email specialists: this single 48-hour sequence reverses 40% of abandonment decisions. [L0068.020]
  • "Better" bullet. Works when something is already perceived as good, but you offer something superior. It slips under the prospect's skepticism and introduces your benefit while building curiosity and momentum. Variations include leading with BEYOND.
    • BETTER THAN Facebook ads for cold traffic: TikTok organic reach costs zero and reaches your exact demographic six months earlier than you see it on Facebook.
    • BEYOND the standard 30-day return window: offer 60 days and watch repeat purchase rates increase 35% because customers trust you more. [L0068.022]
  • Pure benefit bullets. Used when there is no elusive information to hide. They work best for straightforward advantages and for guarantee presentations. They need strong action verbs, clear language, and emotional weight rather than curiosity-driven structure, and they must be made readable and interesting through word choice and specific detail. [L0068.028]

Combining, Sequencing, and Placing Bullets

  • Combine two or more formulas on the same information. Combining formulas gives greater effect than using one alone. For example, combine a PLUS bullet with a How-to bullet to multiply the impact. [L0068.049]
  • Do not overuse Sneaky or Secret-to bullets. Overuse makes copy feel contrived and causes excessive skepticism. When everything is presented as a secret, each secret loses its uniqueness and intrigue. [L0068.050]
  • Vary bullet length, structure, and formula. Variety prevents monotony and flat benefit syndrome, where bullets blur together. Mix long and short bullets, blind bullets with revealing bullets, and different formulas on the same topic. This creates a roller coaster feeling that keeps the prospect excited about each next bullet. [L0068.026]
  • Mix blind and revealing bullets. Mix bullets that tease unrevealed information with bullets that immediately reveal the benefit. This unpredictability prevents monotony and keeps readers excited about what comes next rather than skimming. [L0068.045]
  • Theme bullets to cover one topic from several angles. Grouping related bullets that hit one topic with different formulas makes the prospect feel he has comprehensive coverage. Varied themed bullets keep him engaged because he does not know which direction the next one will take. Themed bullets at the beginning (front cover or early deck copy) signal a thorough investigation inside. [L0068.029]
  • Position matters. The most powerful, intriguing fascinations belong in the most valuable real estate: the front and back cover, the opening headline area, and the final closing bullets. Opening bullets in promotional body copy should bribe the prospect into reading on. Closing bullets on the back cover should compel him to open the report to find the answers. [L0068.036]
  • Sequence bullets so they feed each other. Tightly and sequentially connected bullets reinforce each other rather than standing alone. Individual bullets need not do as much because the cumulative effect does the heavy lifting. Sequencing lets you introduce a benefit in one bullet and explain why it works in the next. [L0068.037]

Unique Mechanism

Purpose and placement

  • The unique mechanism is the part of product copy that explains the logic of how the product delivers its main promise and why it differs from competing products. It normally comes after the story or thought-command opening and before the detailed features and benefits. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0465.001]
  • Buyers decide emotionally but need a logical reason to confirm the decision, and the unique mechanism is where that logic enters the copy. Without it, the copy only makes promises. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0465.002]
  • Skeptical buyers who have been over-promised before need to see how a product works before they believe a headline promise. Explain the mechanism right after the primary promise. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0465.003]
  • Buyers want something different from what they have tried before, so the unique mechanism also separates the product from alternatives on the market. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0465.004]
  • The unique mechanism of your product is the strongest single benefit to highlight in ad copy, because uniqueness encourages clicks through the promise of learning something different. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0241.006]
  • Brands print their unique mechanism prominently on packaging and use it in offline and online sales alike. Once you know the concept, study competitor packaging and product listings to spot the mechanisms they highlight. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0465.028]
  • The length of the mechanism section depends on the product: simple products may need only three or four short paragraphs, while technical products need more space. Explain as much as needed so the mechanism is understood. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0465.027]

Writing the mechanism sentence

  • Before writing the mechanism, list the concerns buyers have before purchase, for example never having used this type of product. Then connect the mechanism to resolving that concern, which adds a benefit to the logic. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0465.015]
  • Formula for a benefit-linked mechanism sentence: name the mechanism, state a measurable advantage over the previous or competing version, then state the benefit that answers a specific buyer doubt. The name creates uniqueness, the number makes the difference believable, and the benefit removes the reason to hesitate. (Older source, 2021-22.)
    • Our 3-layer Thermo-Lock lid keeps coffee hot for up to 6 hours, 40% longer than our previous model, so first-time buyers can brew at 6 am without making a second cup.
    • TrailGrip sole technology holds grip on wet stone 2x better than standard rubber soles, so hikers new to trail running stop worrying about slipping. [L0465.014]
  • State directly what the reader should conclude, for example that they need all components of the mechanism. Do not assume the reader will reach the conclusion you want on their own. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0465.026]
  • Do not leave technical terms unexplained. Jargon confuses buyers and drops them from the copy. Explain each term in short, plain paragraphs, but keep it short for simple products, since they need less explanation. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0465.023]

Technical mechanism

  • Formula for a technical mechanism section. Open with the trademarked technology name and a measurable output compared with the closest competitor, then add a second attribute that goes beyond raw power, then explain each component in simple terms, and close with a firm statement of what the buyer needs. The number proves superiority, the second attribute widens the advantage, and the plain explanation makes the technology understandable. (Older source, 2021-22.)

    • Our TerraSeal boot uses a 4-zone membrane technology that keeps water out 3x longer than the closest competitor. It is not just about waterproofing; the same membrane lets moisture escape, so your feet stay dry on long days. You need both functions, because a boot that only blocks water traps sweat.
    • The AeroFlow kettle heats water with a 2200 W element, 30% faster than the closest competitor. It is not just about speed; the sensor shuts it off automatically, so it is safe to leave unattended. You need both the speed and the auto shut-off to get a fast, safe kettle. [L0465.018]

  • Case: infrared sauna, first claim. The trademarked True Wave heaters produce 1000 W, which the copy states is 25x more than the closest competitor, so the same benefits come in less time. The claim was supported by a test showing higher output than competitors. The session takes about 15 minutes after the sauna preheats, and a reservation mode can preset the sauna to switch on up to 36 hours in advance. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0465.019]

  • Case: infrared sauna, second attribute. The heating is full spectrum, meaning it uses near, mid, and far infrared together.

    • Near infrared is absorbed in shallow skin tissue and linked to sweating.
    • Mid infrared reaches deeper soft tissue and is linked to oxygen and circulation.
    • Far infrared reaches the deepest and is linked to releasing stored substances from muscle and fat.

    The copy then says you need all three wavelengths, not just one or two as some competing saunas offer. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0465.020]

Verifying mechanism claims

  • Case: wax strip pack. The named gel technology is the first and most visible claim on the front. The claims below it state that:

    • the gel flows around and grips the shortest hairs;
    • it works on hairs from 1.5 mm and is easier than the previous formula;
    • it removes hairs from the root for up to 28 days of smoothness;
    • the pack holds 20 strips and 2 finishing wipes;
    • a sensitive-skin version contains almond oil and vitamin E.

    The narration gave 20 days for the smoothness claim, while the pack prints 28 days. Use the printed claim only after verifying it. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0465.017]

Intensification

Principles

  • Intensification is the process of building desire by showing new images of how the product satisfies it. It works in two directions: horizontally, by reaching more people, and vertically, by making the existing desire stronger. (Older source, 2021-22.) [L0330.066]
  • Where space is limited, as in catalogs, small print, radio, and television, use two techniques: compression, where images are boiled down to a few key words, and campaign repetition, where those key words repeat across ads with changing embellishments. In long-form media such as large print ads and direct mail, the length is limited only by how many fresh ways you can present the image without boredom. [L0330.067]
  • The reader will remember one main image from an ad. Every new and different way of presenting that image adds detail, sharpens it, and builds emotional weight, so walk around the product and show it at each point where it enters the buyer's life. [L0330.068]
  • Do not repeat a description; reinforce it with a new viewpoint. Each new setting for the same basic promise strengthens the descriptions before it and makes the reader more eager to take part. If the new viewpoint is not different or dramatic enough to renew interest, leave it out. [L0330.069]

Techniques

  • Technique 1: describe the satisfaction in complete detail. Use the result's specific numbers, such as counts, sizes, colours, and time spans, so the reader can picture the outcome before reading on. [L0330.070]

  • Technique 2: put the product in action. Walk the reader step by step through how it does what it does, including what he sees and feels at each step, so he can imagine using it. [L0330.071]

  • Technique 3: place the reader inside the story. Describe what will happen to him on the first day he owns the product, in concrete scenes such as a moment with family, a traffic light, or a shop counter. [L0330.072]

  • Technique 4: turn the demonstration into a test the reader can run himself. Give clear steps to measure the result, such as checking a number before and after, or completing a task on the first evening, so the claim is proven in his own hands. [L0330.073]

  • Technique 5: stretch the benefits over days, weeks, and months. Show the product working in stages over time, so the reader sees a continuing flow of benefits instead of a single event. [L0330.074]

  • Technique 8: compare side by side. Compare the product with the old way or with competitors, with the specific price or cost difference shown in parallel sentences, so the advantage stands out sharply. [L0330.077]

  • Technique 9: show the negative side. Paint the problem or the inadequate old product in full, dark detail, then give the parallel solution item by item. The reader feels the push away from the problem and the pull toward the product together. [L0330.078]

  • Technique 10: show how easy the product is to use. Stress how little effort, skill, or time it takes, and set that against the large benefits it delivers, so the reader sees a short effort for a long-lasting return. [L0330.079]

  • Technique 11: use metaphor, analogy, and imagination. Use them to make facts more dramatic when a literal description is dull or unbelievable. Personify the product or the method if that helps the reader grasp it. [L0330.080]

  • Technique 12: summarise before closing, using a catalog. There are two kinds.

    1. Horizontal catalog: list all applications and buyer types in one-line items to widen the market. It is a shotgun approach that hedges the choice of one dominant appeal.
    2. Vertical catalog: pile up the desires in summary to deepen the desire the ad has built.

    Use short sentences, one per item, with no further explanation. [L0330.081]

  • Technique 13: end with the guarantee as the climax. State the terms in clear numbered points, each with its condition and refund or replacement promise, and repeat the product's main performances in the process. The close becomes a summary of the product's benefits, made safe by the guarantee. [L0330.082]

Research benefits before writing copy

  1. Read Amazon reviews of the product and of similar products, focusing on the five-star reviews that describe the experience after use. [L0916.010]
  2. Note the life changes, enhancements and positive outcomes buyers describe, since similar products often solve the same problem. [L0916.010]
  3. Search Google for "benefits of [product name]" and collect articles and blogs that contain benefit points and data. [L0916.010]
  4. Collect all points in one document and use them in the page copy. [L0916.010]

Interest copy should describe the end experience, such as comfort, relief or a better daily routine, rather than technical specs and features, because most buyers care more about how the product will make them feel than about volume, functions or technical details. [L0916.011]

Turn features into benefits

  • Identify the product benefits and the desired outcome from the features before writing any copy, because features are the proof and benefits are what the customer buys. [L0917.015]
  • Write each feature on its own card, translate it into the benefit it gives the buyer, and sort the cards by importance. People buy what a product does for them, not what it is, so every feature must be converted into a benefit. [L1370.005]
  • Look beyond the obvious benefit for a hidden benefit that the buyer cares about deeply but rarely states, and build the message around it. Example: a product that saves work time can be pitched around the free time the buyer gets back for leisure, which the buyer values more than the savings alone. [L1370.006]
  • Do not write landing page copy that lists features without linking them to a customer benefit, because the customer cannot see why the feature matters. Follow each feature with a "why is it there" answer that names the result the customer gets. [L0917.010]
  • Benefit bullet pattern: translate each feature into the outcome the customer gets, so the feature explains the benefit. Pattern: [Feature] keeps [surface or person] [protected outcome] without [unwanted side effect]. Examples: Non-slip base keeps your rug in place without damaging your floor. and Quick-release buckle keeps your strap secure without the hassle of fiddly fastening. [L0917.007]
  • Building ad or landing page copy around features (for example waterproof, quick-drying, smooth writing, no ink leakage) misses what buyers want. Write about the end benefit the buyer gets, such as fun time with kids, less screen time, or children's creativity. [L1183.026]
  • Manufacturers write specifications for technical buyers. A store owner should rewrite them into customer benefits, such as easy to adjust, no tools needed instead of a thread specification. [L1293.023]
  • Write product descriptions as benefits, not features. Features are technical specs (for example CDMA and GSM, or 110 to 240 volt switching) that customers do not understand. Benefits state what the product does for them (for example works in Canada, the UK and Asia or plug it in anywhere in the world). [L1293.022]
  • Where sources differ on how much feature content to keep, both apply in different places:
    • Product descriptions: lead with benefits and keep features as supporting detail. [L1293.022]
    • Ads: benefits carry more weight, but features must still appear, because a feature describes the product part and the benefit is the outcome that part produces. [L1425.013]

Lead with the outcome and the problem

  • Sell the desired outcome first, then list the features that support it. Features act as proof that the outcome is reachable, not as the main pitch. [L1207.026, L1209.018]

  • The buyer wants to know how the outcome is achieved, so present the outcome first and then the features, with each feature serving as proof that the outcome is possible. [L1209.018]

  • Describe the outcome with a vivid, dramatized metaphor to make the promise memorable, for example melt inches off your waist instead of lose inches. Keep the tone fun and positive so exercise feels less like a chore. [L1207.025, L1209.017]

  • Make the big benefit claim either the unique selling point or the strongest benefit of the product, and state it boldly. A vivid metaphor can make it memorable, but the claim must still match the actual product benefit. [L0917.014]

  • Persuasive benefit copy describes the customer's desired outcome and, where useful, first surfaces the customer's problem and makes it feel worse before presenting the product as the solution. Frame the solution as fast, instant or convenient where that is true, and emphasize benefits more than features. [L0918.009]

  • Benefit copy structure:

    1. Name the problem.
    2. State the solution.
    3. State the concrete benefit in a short form.

    Not every page needs to surface a problem, but the benefits must always be emphasized clearly. [L0918.010]

  • Present benefits by showing the reader the life they could have, rather than only naming the benefit. Use the word Imagine followed by a vivid scene, or drop it and state the benefit directly, for example You'll speak with confidence after attending this public speaking class. [L1356.040]

Use occasions and lower the entry barrier

  • Occasion pattern: state the moment the product is used, then the emotional result for the buyer's loved ones. Formula: occasion + product's role + result for others. [L0773.066]
Occasion Product's role Result for others Example line
Cold evenings Soft fleece blanket Whole family stays warm Keep the whole family warm on cold evenings with this soft fleece blanket. [L0773.066]
Birthday dinner Personalized cocktail glasses Dinner feels special Make every birthday dinner special with these personalized cocktail glasses. [L0773.066]
  • Emotional benefit pattern: replace a feature statement such as high quality item, ships in two days with a use case that connects the product to a shared moment and the customer's loved ones. Formula: person-centered situation + the product's role in it. Examples: Make your weekend hikes easier with this lightweight backpack and Give your kids a calmer bedtime with this soft nightlight. [L0773.020]
  • A line such as suitable for all ages or no experience required lowers the entry barrier. It makes the product seem usable by anyone, which removes the feeling that the buyer has to be skilled first. [L1207.011]

Make benefits instant with analogies

  • Formula: describe the product by comparing its effect to an experience or object the reader already knows, such as a common event, a sport or a household task. Why it works: the reader already has a clear picture to attach the new idea to, so they understand the benefit at once. [L1369.055]
    • Example one: Like finding a parking spot right at the front door on a busy Saturday, the new loading ramp saves the dock crew twenty minutes per shift. [L1369.055]
    • Example two: Like having a second pair of hands on the bench, the clamp holds the frame steady while the glue sets. [L1369.055]

Stack benefits and make claims specific

  • Benefit stacking: list the ingredients and benefits one after another in a single run, each tied to a result the viewer wants, such as firmer, brighter, less volume loss or fewer lines. The ad in the lesson stacked about ten benefits in a row. Stacking makes each claim feel more convincing than the last, but each benefit must still match the product's real ingredients. [L0707.015]
  • Name ingredients, such as a molecule that supports elasticity or a compound that works against overproduction of pigment, to add specificity that makes benefits believable. Link each ingredient to one concrete skin result. [L0707.016]
  • A generic claim of lowest prices was replaced with a specific net profit figure of 3 percent, which proved more convincing because it implied prices were already near the minimum possible. Specific financial facts make a claim credible. [L1261.077]
  • A generic purity claim was replaced with specific production details, such as a count of experiments or a measured process, which created a far stronger impression. Turn general quality statements into exact, verifiable process facts. [L1261.079]
  • An itemized breakdown of hidden costs, such as 735 dollars of hidden costs on a 1,500 dollar car, supports a profit-margin claim better than the margin figure alone. Readers accept a concrete cost breakdown more readily than a bare percentage. [L1261.141]

Build the unique mechanism

  • A unique mechanism explains what makes the product different from everything the reader has tried, without naming competitors. The reader infers that older solutions failed because they lacked that specific ingredient, method or technology. [L0772.021]

  • Formula: name the one specific technology, ingredient or method that sets your product apart, then imply that older solutions lacked it, without naming any competitor. It works because the reader connects their past failures to the missing element.

    • Example 1: Most serums stop at the surface. Ours uses a sealed-release carrier that reaches the layer where the damage sits. [L0772.022]
    • Example 2: Shampoo rinses away before the scalp absorbs much. This formula stays on the follicle for the full wash. [L0772.022]

  • The real problem is the reason the prospect's past solutions failed. Find it by reverse-engineering the answer to the uniqueness question. Once revealed, the reader sees that old approaches never addressed it and that your new mechanism does, which gives them hope. [L1365.019]

  • Explain the mechanism by linking it to a belief the audience already holds, which makes an unusual claim feel obvious. The explanation and its proof are also copy, so they must be engaging and wrapped in the story structure. [L1365.031]

  • Mechanism formula, in order:

    1. Existing belief the audience already holds.
    2. Your mechanism follows logically from that belief.
    3. Credible third-party proof confirms the result.

    Why it works: readers accept a mechanism faster when it extends something they already believe, and outside sources make the claim harder to dismiss. [L1365.032]

    • Example 1: Most people already know consistent sleep drives recovery; our routine schedules the light and temperature cues that make that consistency happen, and an independent sleep lab measured the effect. [L1365.032]
    • Example 2: Shoppers already believe fresh produce keeps its nutrients when frozen right after harvest; our delivery cold chain applies exactly that, and independent testing measured the vitamin retention. [L1365.032]

  • Give proof on the first page without revealing the mechanism yet. A reference such as a patent filing number or a named publication shows the opportunity is real, while the explanation of how it works comes later in the letter. [L1365.047]

Proof elements

  • Each promise must be backed by proof at least as strong as the claim. Useful proof includes testimonials, short anecdotes, clear metaphors, case studies, charts and specific statistics. Promises that are not proven make skeptics doubt the whole message. [L0756.072]
  • Proof should come in threes. One or two pieces leave the claim feeling unresolved, while three is the standard target and more is better. Common proof types are case studies, demonstrations, testimonials, quotes from authorities, charts and graphs, official documents, and articles or headlines. [L1356.037]
  • When a claim is too high to prove with three examples, build proof from a sequence of smaller examples that add up to the claim.
    1. Show one example worth $5,000.
    2. Show a second example worth $7,500.
    3. Show a third example worth $10,000.
    4. State the total claim of $10,000 as proven. [L1356.038]
  • Show proof visually where possible (charts, screenshots) and reframe each proof point two or three times in different forms. A reframe shows the same information from a new angle, for example turning a one-time $10,000 gain into a yearly income of six figures, or comparing it to a familiar benchmark such as an S&P 500 return over a year. [L1356.039]
  • Proof answers the question "how do I know this is real" and must back up the one belief. Initial proof can be historical charts, testimonials or third-party quotes. In competitive markets, where skepticism is high, it should come right after the bold promise. [L1365.015]
  • Testimonials should mimic the target reader as closely as possible:
    • Show images of people who look like the ideal customer and reflect their economic and life status.
    • Use royalty-free or commercially licensed images, never random images from a search engine.
    • Make testimonials specific (amounts, timelines, steps followed, family reactions), since specificity conveys Big, Fast, Easy, Safe and Anybody. [L1356.059]
  • For mailings that sell a product for purchase now:
    • Use testimonials from happy users, which matter more than anything else for sales.
    • Use photographs rather than drawings.
    • Prove the product is easy to use through copy, photos or testimonials. [L1370.033]
  • For mailings that sell professional services, factual credibility such as years in business, clients served and sample client lists supports the offer. Real customer experiences, benefits achieved and doubts resolved are more persuasive. Make intangible benefits tangible with before and after photos, case histories and examples. Consider offering a free first consultation or free information to break down skepticism. [L1370.034]
  • Historical example (not current data): in ordinary homes of that period, about two-thirds of the heat from the furnace was wasted, with one-third lost through windows and doors and another third through uninsulated walls and roofs. A product that stops that waste could be argued with these figures. [L1369.051]

Close with a bottom line

  • Close the benefits section with a bottom line of three short, parallel adjectives that sum up the product, for example fun, simple, effective. Three words are easy to remember and repeat, and the rhythm makes the claims feel complete and confident. [L1209.035]
    • Example 1: Fast, quiet, and built to last. [L1209.035]
    • Example 2: Light, clean, and easy to store. [L1209.035]

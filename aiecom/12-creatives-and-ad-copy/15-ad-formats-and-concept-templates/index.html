12.15 Ad formats and concept templates

Format, concept and how to choose

  • An ad format is the medium an ad uses, such as video, static image or carousel. A concept is the type of piece made in that format, for example a VSL, a listicle, an explainer, an unboxing, a before-and-after or a text-only black screen. [L0374.009]
  • Choose the format by the product and the audience, then test. A format that wins for one product can underperform for another, and some products will not fit a layout at all. [L0341.003, L0343.011]
  • Test a mixture of formats to find the winner: lifestyle images, offer graphics with features and reviews, and "this or that" benefit layouts. [L0067.044]
  • Use the best assets available, and clip or repurpose competitor videos if needed. [L0640.030]

Testing system: swipe file and test rules

  • Keep a small swipe file of proven ad creative templates, so several products or offers can be tested quickly without waiting for slow or unreliable outsourced creative. Fast testing shows which product and angle respond best before you invest in polished assets. [L0341.001, L0343.001]
  • A testimonial-style Facebook ad run at a £20-30 daily spend produced a strong early result, so social-proof formats are worth testing first. [L0341.002]
  • Test each template against each product rather than assuming it transfers. [L0341.003, L0341.042]
  • Keep templates editable in Canva, so product photos, colours and text can be swapped in minutes. [L0341.042]
  • Track which template and offer combination gets the best click-through rate and spend efficiency before you scale it. [L0341.042]
  • Vary creative formats within one set: one with text on the image, one with no text, and one short video with a slowly moving background. Winners differ by audience, so test all of them. [L0649.034]

Image, video and carousel: where sources differ

Sources disagree on the best format. Use both values, depending on the product:

  • Video first: Video creatives significantly outperform image-only ads, so use video whenever possible. [L0280.019]
  • Video often wins: Video often outperforms static images for engagement, but image ads can also work well. [L0640.030]
  • Video not always: Video creatives do not always beat static images. A product suited to video can still perform better with pictures, so test both formats rather than assuming video wins. [L0690.032]
  • Image cheaper, sometimes better: Image ads can be cheaper and convert well, sometimes better than video, especially for products that are not obvious problem solvers. Video usually converts better overall but costs more to run. [L0692.013]
  • Image for self-selling products: Image ads can suit visually appealing products that sell themselves, such as home decor, even though video is usually expected to convert better for problem-solving products. [L0692.053]
  • Demonstration needs video: Video is the better format for products that need demonstration, such as a scent, a texture or a visible use. Image ads can perform well for self-explanatory products. Jewellery usually needs close-ups and in-context shots, so test both. [L0524.050]
  • Case: picture ads won on traffic: A clothing brand hired a photographer for a product video and ran both video ads and picture ads. The picture ads drove more traffic to the store, so the video moved to retargeting to keep the product in front of people who had already seen the brand. Video ads were also more expensive per thousand impressions than picture ads. [L0642.055]
  • Static jewellery in context: Static images of products such as jewellery can work when shown in context, such as worn with an outfit or in an elegant setting. Video or UGC adds more story and is recommended when images do not tell the story well. [L0995.034]
  • Reframing with UGC: Reframing a product through a different audience angle, such as a gift from a partner or a personal story, can turn a flooded market into a new segment, because the ad speaks to a specific person in a specific situation. Customer testimonials in a natural voice (UGC) are often more convincing than a static product image. [L0995.033]

Carousel and multi-image rules:

  • When using image creatives instead of video, use the carousel format so customers can view multiple angles and understand the product from different perspectives. [L0280.018]
  • In one fashion test, three formats were used: a single picture ad, a carousel ad and a slideshow ad with images changing every few seconds. The carousel performed best in the lesson. [L0661.038]
  • Carousel ads work well for products with a lot of lifestyle imagery, such as fashion. Single image ads can also be strong. Natural lifestyle image ads should be included in testing alongside designed graphics. [L0343.017]
  • Carousel ads can show several images in one unit, which helps vary the message within a single ad. [L0649.039]
  • Carousel ads that show several products and point to a collection page, rather than a single product page, work well, especially for a buy two, get the third free offer. [L0388.040]

Format combinations:

  • Split-screen layout with an endcart display alongside vertical video: the endcart carries static product information, while the video adds dynamic engagement, so both formats sit in one creative unit. [L0258.028]
  • Founder videos filmed to camera are a common workhorse format if the founder is comfortable on camera. Each video can carry a different message, idea or angle at low cost. [L0483.014]
  • A product demonstration filmed with a camera on a desk, showing how the product works, is a common workhorse format. It needs little setup and can be repeated for different products or angles. [L0483.015]
  • Other UGC formats include before-and-after and testimonial videos, but the problem-solution framework is a reliable default structure for most products. [L0561.032]
  • A user-generated style clip, where a buyer describes receiving a product as a gift, makes the message feel personal and credible. Static images can work for products whose appeal is in the look, but a story in context often gives more. [L0999.042]

Same message, different styles

  • The same message can be delivered in different styles: large text on a static image, a person showing the problem in a video, a founder ad or a versus ad. Hooks can differ within one style, for example a scientific diagram versus a real-life demonstration, and they attract different kinds of viewers. [L0485.003]
  • For an interview-style ad, the visuals should look like a real interview, the copy should mention the interview with an expert, and the audio should clearly show a question followed by an answer. Every lever makes the format obvious. [L0485.009]
  • Storytelling suits simple gadgets such as security cameras, because creators can invent scenarios that show the product being useful. Use it only if you can tell a convincing story, and keep the claims honest. [L0372.041]

Testimonial ads

Core formula

  • Pain-to-relief transformation: the customer states the original problem or emotional state, then describes the moment of change, then ends with a specific result with no negatives. This works because it shows transformation through experience rather than claims, and the absence of drawbacks ("no meds") is as persuasive as the benefit. [L0313.001]
    • Example 1: "Dealt with afternoon fatigue for years, now I have steady energy all day without jitters." [L0313.001]
    • Example 2: "Couldn't focus during meetings, this changed my work life and I'm actually productive again." [L0313.001]
  • Keep testimonials short. Testimonials under 20 words outperform long narratives because social media users scan rather than read. Each testimonial should name one specific transformation ("life back", "control again", "migraines stopped"). Negative comparison (what the product is NOT: "no meds", "no side effects") increases credibility. [L0313.023]
  • Avoid generic testimonials such as "Amazing product!", "Great quality" or "5 stars". They provide no social proof, because any competitor could claim the same. Specific testimonials tied to the exact problem and transformation ("Finally, real relief without meds", "Gave me my life back") work because they are hard to fake. [L0313.009]

Testimonial concept template

Build a testimonial ad by pairing a customer's face with a direct quote about their experience, such as "I tried three things before this and nothing worked until I found it." Display the product clearly in the creative, ideally showing the customer actively using or holding it. This establishes credibility by demonstrating how a real person achieved success with the product.

[L0316.005]

Targeting skeptics

  • Testimonial ads for skeptics and proof-driven buyers follow: past struggle → tried other solutions → discovered your product → current success. [L0315.005]
    • Example 1: "Tested four competitors until finding this one-finally solved the problem." [L0315.005]
    • Example 2: "Spent weeks searching for alternatives before discovering ours works better." [L0315.005]
    • This works because skeptics require peer validation from someone relatable before trusting. [L0315.005]

Testimonial creative pattern

  • Formula: volume of happy customers + believable, varied reviews + two trust badges. Buyers trust visible proof from other people more than brand claims. [L0343.006]
    • Example: "Over 12,000 happy homes" with three different reviews and a free returns badge. [L0343.006]
    • Example: "Trusted by 5,000 runners" with a 30-day fit guarantee badge. [L0343.006]
  • Use several strong testimonials and set the colour scheme to your brand colours. Do not use testimonials from one reviewer only, because it looks unrealistic. [L0341.008]
  • Use testimonials from different people, because a set that all comes from one reviewer looks fake. Adapt the brand colours and the font to your own branding, and keep the background open for colour changes. [L0343.007, L0341.008]

Trust signals

  • Place trust signals at the bottom of the ad, such as a 60-day money-back guarantee and free shipping for life. They reduce purchase hesitation in the ad itself. Use only guarantees you can actually honour. [L0341.009, L0343.008]
  • Trust signals at the bottom of a testimonial creative: use them to reduce purchase hesitation in the ad itself. [L0343.008]

Video testimonials

  • Video testimonials are an affordable way to share the message and generate many sales. In the example, the best video testimonial ad produced a couple hundred thousand dollars in revenue. Reviews were reinforced by sending the reviewer a brief covering the required topics. [L0334.035]
  • A client testimonial video can send viewers straight to the product page, followed by retargeting of everyone who watched it. [L0643.057]
  • A testimonial video ad can send viewers straight to a marketplace listing through an affiliate link, and the people who watched can then be retargeted. The lesson's example used an Amazon affiliate listing as the destination. [L0643.066]

Proof-heavy layouts

  • In print, a flyer with a headline, a photo of the seller and dozens of participant quotes arranged in columns feels overwhelming in its proof. A numbered list of 22 reasons to buy, or a brochure with 101 customer stories, reads as convincing even before the reader checks the claims. [L0340.058]

Social proof ads

  • Social proof ads target herd-followers and bandwagon buyers using: customer volume + satisfaction rating + credible third-party mention. These buyers trust decisions validated by large customer bases combined with respected authorities. [L0315.008]
    • Example 1: "150,000+ customers. 4.8 stars. Featured in Forbes and TechCrunch." [L0315.008]
    • Example 2: "98,000+ users. 4.9 rating. Recommended by Wall Street Journal." [L0315.008]

Social proof concept template

Social proof concept: "50,000+ customers. 4.8 stars. Featured in Vogue and Men's Health." Use numbers, ratings, and media mentions to signal credibility and show that others trust the brand.

[L0316.008]

  • Social proof ad: a short customer quote with a five-star rating and the store name, linked to the product or store. It works because buyers trust other buyers more than the brand itself. [L0649.066]
    • Example 1: "Five stars. The fit was perfect and delivery took three days, says a happy customer." [L0649.066]
    • Example 2: "Five stars for the quality, says a repeat buyer. Shop the store today." [L0649.066]
  • Stacking emotion and credibility: merge a customer testimonial with social proof stats in one concept. [L0316.046]
    • Example: "I had back pain every day by 3pm. After 2 weeks with Posture Pro, it's totally gone. 50,000+ relieved users. 4.8 stars. Featured in Vogue and Men's Health." This stacks emotional trust (personal story) with credibility (numbers, ratings, media mentions). [L0316.046]

Us vs them and comparison ads

  • Us-vs-them comparison: place your product on one side and the competitor's on the other. Grade the competitor's background down so your side looks like the clear winner. Name specific points where you are better, such as seam quality or protective features, so the viewer sees the difference in one glance. A light pattern and a white gradient overlay add texture and keep the layout from looking flat. [L0341.004]
    • Example: "Our jacket has double-stitched seams and reinforced zips, while theirs has single stitching and exposed zip edges." [L0341.005]
    • Example: "Our kettle boils in 90 seconds with auto shut-off, while theirs takes 4 minutes and has no safety cut-off." [L0341.006]
  • Them-vs-us creative: competitor product on the left, your product on the right, with a short list of what makes yours better. Formula: the competitor side shows the weakness, your side shows the strength, and both sides stay visually simple. It frames the comparison in one glance without long copy. [L0343.003]
    • Example: "Our zipper outlasts their plastic clasp", with the old brand dimmed on the left. [L0343.003]
    • Example: "Their bottle leaks, ours seals in 3 seconds", with a matching split layout. [L0343.003]
  • Comparative framework ("We vs the Others"):
    1. Use a split-screen layout.
    2. Display your product's unique selling proposition and advantages versus what competitors lack.
    3. Include a clear call to action. [L0258.011]
  • Comparison video pattern: show a well-known product the audience already knows, then introduce your product as the better alternative. The hook says the audience has seen the known product and asks whether they have seen yours. It works best when the compared product is familiar to viewers. [L0795.014]
    • Example hook: "Everyone has tried this popular kitchen gadget, but have you seen the one that actually cuts prep time in half?" [L0795.014]
    • Example hook: "You know the classic desk lamp everyone owns, now meet the one that adjusts itself to your workday." [L0795.014]
  • Trend comparison pattern: use a trending audio where a speaker compares a competitor with their own product, then overlay on-screen text that lists the product's benefits in quotation marks. Comparisons and POV formats also perform well. [L0795.031]
    • Example: a kitchen tool shows a messy competitor result, then its own clean result with on-screen text such as "Cleaner edges, no sticky residue". [L0795.031]

Benefit and feature callouts

Benefit concept template

Benefit concept: Place a headline at the top (e.g., "Sleep through the night, wake without back pain. Every morning."), list 3-4 concrete benefits below (e.g., "Relieve back pain in as little as 7 days," "Improve posture and boost confidence"), put the product image in the middle, and add a discount or CTA at the bottom. Keep it simple and outcome-focused.

[L0316.007]

  • Benefit ad pattern for outcome-first and problem-aware buyers: problem state → transformation promised → concrete result. These buyers already recognise their problem and respond to specific outcome promises. [L0315.007]
    • Example 1: "Stop dealing with [pain point]. Achieve [desired state] daily." [L0315.007]
    • Example 2: "Never again experience [problem]. Get [benefit] consistently." [L0315.007]
  • Worked example, posture corrector: headline "Wake Up Pain-Free. Every Single Morning." Bullet points: "Relieve back and neck pain in as little as 7 days," "Improve posture and boost confidence," "Lightweight, comfortable and invisible under clothes." Product image centred. CTA "SHOP NOW" with discount "20% OFF Today, Offer ends Sunday" and the regular price crossed out. This structure shows benefit first, proof second, urgency third. [L0316.038]
  • Feature callout pattern: one large benefit as the headline, a few features pointing to specific parts of a cut-out product placed in the centre, and a simple step-by-step line at the bottom. It links one clear promise to visible product details. It works best for products that can be cut out and placed centrally, and does not fit every product. [L0341.012]
    • Example: "Big benefit: Sleep through the night. Features: quiet motor, blackout cover, 8-hour timer, with arrows pointing to each part." [L0341.013]
  • Benefit callout creative: one large headline states the main benefit, and several short feature lines with arrows point to product parts that support it. Formula: headline benefit + two to four supporting features + a simple call-to-action step at the bottom. One clear promise is easier to grasp than a list of features. [L0343.010]
    • Example: "Sleep deeper in minutes", with arrows to a weighted lining and a cooling layer. [L0343.010]
    • Example: "Clean floors without bending", with arrows to a telescopic handle and a swivel head. [L0343.010]
  • A benefit callout creative works best for products that can be cut out and placed centrally, with features written around them. Products that do not fit this layout may still work with a different structure, so test each product. [L0343.011]
  • Feature point-out framework:
    1. Start with a close-up image of the product.
    2. Add 4-5 text-based advantages surrounding the product.
    3. Use Canva or Photoshop to enable easy creation of variations and iterations. [L0258.013]
  • Single big-benefit creative: one benefit in a large headline with a small social-proof badge, such as a count of happy customers. Formula: one benefit + one badge + short direct language, with no feature list. A single promise reads at a glance and the badge adds trust. [L0341.014, L0343.012]
    • Example: "Stops leaks in one wipe", with a badge reading "Thousands of happy customers". [L0343.012]
    • Example: "Dries in 90 seconds", with a badge reading "4,000 five-star reviews". [L0343.012]
  • One clear benefit headline with a badge reading "Thousands of happy customers" avoids the bad outcome. [L0341.015]
  • Test the single benefit callout against feature-led layouts, since results vary by product. [L0341.014]

Offer, free gift and urgency ads

Free gift concept template

Free gift concept: Lead with "If you order today in the next 24 hours, we'll throw in [companion product or resource] free with your order." This creates urgency by setting a 24-hour deadline and adds perceived value. Use as primary text and headline.

[L0316.006]

  • Free gift ad pattern for fence-sitters and value-focused buyers: core product + bonus offer + time anchor. These buyers decide on total perceived value relative to price, and bonuses shift that ratio favourably. [L0315.006]
    • Example 1: "Order this month and get the [accessory] free." [L0315.006]
    • Example 2: "Buy now and receive [complementary product] included." [L0315.006]
  • Offer creative: product image and name at the top, the top three benefits, a big discount or free-gift element with the price difference shown, and a clear call to action. It answers what the product is, why it is worth buying and what the deal is, in one image. Use it to test offers quickly before building video. [L0341.021]
  • Offer test creative: product image, three core benefits and one clear offer, such as buy one get one free, a free gift or 40 percent off. Formula: product + three benefits + one offer in a single static layout. It isolates which offer a market responds to, faster than a full video. [L0343.019]
    • Example: "Buy one, get the second free", with three benefits and a product shot. [L0343.019]
    • Example: "Free travel pouch with every order", with three benefits and a price-anchored gift image. [L0343.019]
  • Offer images that display "choose this or that" options for a free gift, list multiple benefits and showcase the main product prominently are particularly effective. [L0067.044]
  • Offer graphics highlight the promotion prominently, with features called out and customer reviews displayed. [L0067.044]
  • Promotion ad: name the event and the offer clearly, then add a call to action. A named deadline and a concrete offer make people act during the sale window. [L0649.067]
    • Example: "Cyber Monday: buy one desk lamp, get the second free, tap to claim." [L0649.067]
    • Example: "Summer clearance: 30% off all bags this weekend only, shop the deal now." [L0649.067]

Urgency concept template

Urgency concept: "Only 45 left in stock. Sale ends Sunday at midnight." Combine scarcity (limited inventory) with a deadline (specific day and time) to push procrastinators over the line.

[L0316.009]

  • Urgency ads for procrastinators and almost-sold buyers use scarcity indicator + specific deadline. Procrastinators need both a reason to act now (time limit) and proof of consequence (scarcity) to overcome delay patterns. [L0315.009]
    • Example 1: "Only 22 left. Sale ends Friday midnight." [L0315.009]
    • Example 2: "Stock running low: 17 remaining. Offer closes Thursday." [L0315.009]

Giveaway ads

  • Headline pattern: start with WIN, state the prize count as 1 of N, name the product, and add a clear call to action such as Enter Now. The pattern works because a specific prize and a small win ratio create curiosity and produce cheap clicks. A disclaimer about the win ratio must still appear on the ad. [L0797.012]
    • Example: "WIN 1 of 3 Limited-Edition Hoodies: Enter Now." [L0797.012]
    • Example: "WIN 1 of 5 Summer Bundles, Enter Below." [L0797.012]
  • Static image ads work well for giveaway funnels. Run several variations and test them. Use the giveaway for new product drops, because it generates a large amount of awareness at very low CPM. [L0797.013]
  • The example giveaway ad carries two calls to action: an entry button linking to the giveaway landing page, and a separate Shop now button linking to the store. Using both serves entrants and buyers at the same time. [L0797.028]

Meme-style and native-looking ads

  • Meme-style image ads look native to the Instagram feed and feel less like ads, so they tend to earn high attention and click-through rates. Replace the text with a provocative hook or a major product benefit, then swap the image to test new products quickly. [L0341.016, L0343.013]
  • Meme ads can hold attention and generate clicks, but in one case they still sold less than user-generated content. Use them as a testing format and scale the ones that respond well. [L0343.014]
  • Meme-style video ads produced an attention rate around 60% and a click-through rate around 4% in one case. They were kept running because they improved Google Ads performance, even though they did not show direct Facebook sales. This was observed once and should be checked before relying on it. [L0995.036]
  • Static meme-style image ads perform well on Facebook. Build them in Canva by placing the product image with a short line of text and an emoji on top. Meme-style image ads performed well in the lesson's test. [L0773.040, L0773.058, L0774.041]
  • Simple image ads: the product photo with a short caption that gives the reason to buy, placed on top of the image. [L0774.042]
    • Example 1: "The shoe you wanted, now in your size." [L0774.042]
    • Example 2: "Ready for winter nights? Get yours today." [L0774.042]
  • Jokes that rely on outdated trends or references lose their effect quickly. Check whether a reference is still current before building a video around it. [L0372.038]
  • Trend-format pattern: adapt a popular format in which people list what they like about a subject, replace the subject with your product, then name its main benefits. Viewers already understand the pattern and can focus on the benefits. [L0372.039]
    • Example 1: "Things I love about my cordless vacuum: it fits under the couch, it charges in a minute, it finally keeps the stairs clean." [L0372.039]
    • Example 2: "Why my commute got easier: the foldable backpack fits a laptop, it dries fast in the rain, and it has a USB port." [L0372.039]
  • Story hook pattern: open with a relatable incident, such as a robbery or a theft caught on camera, then cut to the product as the solution. Viewers want to see how the story ends. [L0372.040]
    • Example 1: "Someone walked into the garage at 2 am and the owner's first clip was caught by a light bulb camera." [L0372.040]
    • Example 2: "A package disappeared from the porch, so the homeowner set up a hidden camera that shows the whole story." [L0372.040]
  • Visual pump-and-dump product: a plain product showcase with short overlay text and no listicle or advertorial can be enough, since the product itself is the message. Short clips of about 15 to 20 seconds with simple text worked as winners in the example. [L0347.029]

Listicle, advertorial and editorial formats

  • Three editorial styles are common: listicle (a numbered list of benefits), long-form blog-style article, and news-article style with bold claims, big numbers and research data. Choose the style that fits the product and the audience's problem. [L0578.009]
  • Listicle pattern: a numbered list of benefits, often 7 or 10 points, about a trending or little-known product. It is framed as why it is taking over or why people should own it, and the product is pitched only at the end. The reader meets neutral content instead of an ad, and the trend framing makes an unknown product worth checking. [L0578.010]
    • Example 1: "7 Reasons Compact Ergonomic Mice Are Taking Over Home Offices". [L0578.010]
    • Example 2: "10 Things Physiotherapists Wish Their Patients Knew About Morning Back Pain". [L0578.010]
  • Blog-style pattern: a long-form article that opens with a relatable character's problem, agitates it, explains why common fixes fail, introduces a new approach, and only then presents the product in the last part. Trust grows when the author byline shows an expert, such as a doctor or vet, and when photos break up the text. [L0578.011]
    • Example 1: "Why Your Sleep Keeps Breaking at 3 AM: What a Sleep Specialist Found in Her Clinic". [L0578.011]
    • Example 2: "The Kitchen Habit That Dulls Knives in Weeks: How One Chef Changed Her Routine". [L0578.011]
  • News-style pattern: the layout and headline imitate a news article, using a bold claim, large numbers and cited research data, with the product pitched after the story. The reader trusts the apparent reporting and sees the problem as urgent and proven. [L0578.012]
    • Example 1: "Study Finds 6 in 10 Remote Workers Lose Focus Before Lunch, and a New Fix Is Gaining Ground". [L0578.012]
    • Example 2: "Data From 4,000 Home Gym Owners Shows Why Most Resistance Bands Snap Within a Year". [L0578.012]
  • Advertorials also come in a listicle format: a numbered list of reasons, such as eight or five reasons a product solves a problem. It works like a story-based advertorial: it builds interest first, then bridges the visitor to the product page. Problem-solving products, such as toys, suit this format best. [L0691.031]
  • Listicle advertorial headline: "Here are [number] reasons why [product] is on [audience]'s top wish lists", followed by a numbered list of points and then a sales page. The format promises a quick, ordered read that makes the product feel chosen by people like the reader. Test headline variants, because the first line decides whether visitors keep reading. [L0995.055]
    • Example 1: "Here are 7 reasons why this compact pet-hair roller is on busy dog owners' top wish lists". [L0995.055]
    • Example 2: "Here are 5 reasons why this personal alarm keychain is on night commuters' top wish lists". [L0995.055]
  • Cold audiences: send paid clicks first to editorial-style advertorial pages that look like articles, to warm the audience up before they reach the sales landing page. Then run the sales page with messaging matched to the same audience. Use this when a product needs more explanation or trust than a short ad can give. [L0918.034]
  • Physical products are more visual. An image, a short product video or a product explainer that shows the feature, benefit and outcome often converts better than a long sales letter. A long-form sales letter fits products that need more explanation, and the right format depends on the market. [L0643.056]
  • Dialogue-style videos, where the creator responds to a viewer's comment or promises to feature the top-liked comment, drive strong engagement and follower growth, especially on new accounts. They usually convert less well than hooks that name a viewer problem and present a product solution right away. [L0795.037]
  • A warehouse or behind-the-scenes video showing the brand's own logo on packaging adds proof and trust. [L0950.046]
    • Example: "See our packing team ship every order in its branded box." [L0950.046]
    • Example: "Watch how we pack your order before it leaves our warehouse." [L0950.046]
  • Winner-and-envious-rival scene: show the buyer succeeding in a visible moment while a peer watches with envy, and let the product be the implied reason without making a claim. [L0340.084]
    • Formula: "[Buyer] using [product] in a moment of success, [rival] in the background watching or waiting." [L0340.084]
    • Example: "A landscaper unloads a truck with the company name visible while a rival crew sits in a jam behind them." [L0340.084]
    • Example: "A home cook serves a tray of bread to friends while a neighbour stands at the door with store-bought loaves." [L0340.084]
  • Long-running story case: a Facebook video story of about nine minutes, far beyond the length usually recommended for that platform, was posted with a $187 spend in the first week, then paid promotion stopped. The video was a personal story about a pet and a hard time, filmed on location, with no sales pitch. It reached 2.4 million views, 59,000 likes, 31,000 shares and over 4,000 comments, many of them long personal replies. The creator later noted that deeply emotional storytelling built lasting loyalty that a shorter, safer clip could not match. The same story opened the email sequence to build a bond with new subscribers. [L0378.078]

Image angles for demand generation

Demand Gen image angles:

Informational Create curiosity without solving the problem Problem-focused (everyday struggle); Clinical/Editorial (anatomy or article style); Curiosity-driven (unusual or intriguing)

  • Informational images that worked as headline partners include a medical-textbook style anatomy illustration, an X-ray style image, a doctor reviewing anatomy, and a pain-point lifestyle photo that looks like stock imagery. Retargeting ads

  • Reminder retargeting ad: a short question that points at what the visitor viewed, followed by a reason to return, such as free shipping. It reminds the visitor of their interest instead of pitching cold. [L0649.065]
    • Example 1: "Still thinking it over? Your saved items are waiting, with free shipping today." [L0649.065]
    • Example 2: "Left something in your cart? Come back and check out in one click." [L0649.065]
  • Informant ad (older advice, 2021-2022): a retargeting ad that promotes an existing, valuable article instead of the product directly. Formula: the article's feature image, a headline in the form "Learn how to [main outcome the article delivers]", body copy that names the main problem the reader faces and says the article solves it, and a CTA such as "Read now" for text or "Watch now" / "View now" for video. It works because the ad speaks to a problem the viewer already feels and promises a useful answer rather than a sale. [L0415.002]
    • Own examples: "Learn how to stop a squeaky office chair in five minutes" (body: squeaks break your focus at work and the guide shows the three quick fixes; CTA "Read now"). [L0415.002]
    • Own examples: "Learn how to pick a hiking backpack that fits your torso" (body: most packs sit wrong and cause shoulder pain; CTA "Learn more"). [L0415.002]
  • Informant ad steps (older advice, 2021-2022):
    1. Choose the existing content piece the ad will lead to.
    2. Use that content's feature image, checking it is high quality and directly matches the content on the page.
    3. Write a headline that names the main thing the reader will learn from the content.
    4. Write body copy that describes the primary problem the content addresses, so the viewer feels the connection.
    5. Pick the CTA: "Read now" for text content, "Watch now" or "View now" for video, "Learn more" as a general alternative.
    6. Keep the same image, headline and CTA across all placements, changing only the copy length and format per network.
    7. Set up the ad in the ad platform's interface once the audiences exist. [L0415.003]
  • The feature image should show people who look like the target audience and be directly relevant to the main problem of the content, so it stands out in a feed. A generic or off-topic image weakens the ad, because it does not signal that the content solves the viewer's problem. [L0415.004]
  • Since the content already exists, most of the work is done and the ad takes little time to produce. In practice, one such ad takes around 10 minutes to create, and once the initial audiences are set up the process is quick. [L0415.005]

Lead magnet ads

  • Lead magnet ad: name a useful free resource in the headline, add a short benefit line, and end with a button such as "Learn more". The formula sells the free item instead of the product. [L0649.064]
    • Example 1: "Free weekly meal plan for busy parents, grab yours here." [L0649.064]
    • Example 2: "Free fit guide for trail runners, get it now." [L0649.064]

Introduction and owner-led ads (older advice, 2021-2022)

  • Showing the business owner in an introduction ad builds trust and a personal connection that many e-commerce stores lack, which helps them stand out from competitors. It works best when the owner introduces themselves in their own voice, rather than using a professionally produced ad with no personal link to the business. [L0488.002]
  • Avoid phrasing such as "thanks for visiting our site" in retargeting ads, because it feels creepy to visitors. Instead, have the owner introduce themselves and invite people to get in touch. [L0488.003]

Introduction ad layout

Ad layout for an introduction ad, listing what each field must contain. Image or video: show yourself on camera. If you choose video, say who you are, describe your business and how you assist people, then add a call to action asking the viewer to phone your business. Headline: address the customer in a manner that catches their eye; this can be quite open-ended. Body copy: urge the customer to reach out to your business. With a video ad, simply restate the video's main message. With more room, add a short background story and cover the business, your unique selling point and the way you help people. Call to action: test wording such as "Call Us", "Get In Touch" or "Call Me".

[L0488.004]

  • If you are dropshipping and cannot film yourself with the products you sell, use a plain image of yourself alone instead of product images. A video with the owner showing the products is even better when possible. [L0488.005]
  • Use the body copy to encourage the viewer to contact the business and build the relationship. Experiment with call-to-action wording such as "Call us", "Get in touch" and "Call me". [L0488.009]
  • Placements for an introduction ad concept:
    • A static Google Display Network remarketing banner with a short text and a call-to-action button.
    • A Facebook news feed video ad.
    • A Google Display leaderboard banner (the wide banner shown at the top of a website).
    • An Instagram video ad with a Contact Us button. The same image and text are reused across placements. [L0488.014]

Concept briefs for new-customer ads

Ad concept requirements (applies to at least 5 concepts aimed at new customers who have never heard of the brand):

  • Each concept must contain: a name; positioning strategy (new mechanism, superior product or identity marketing); format (static image, video, carousel or UGC); primary avatar; key desire or pain point; 5-7 hook variations using proven patterns; visual concept (benefit, problem, product or product in action); ad structure; full script; visual proof elements; call to action; urgency element if appropriate; handling of the top 2-3 objections; and an explanation of why it works, citing research findings. [L0613.019]
  • Each ad concept should focus on one desire or problem only. It must show understanding of the viewer's desires, problems and identity, use language found in the research, pre-empt objections, use hooks proven in the niche, and create belief through visual proof. It should also stand out from competitor creative. [L0613.021]

Concept architecture (for at least 5 concepts):

  1. Name the concept and target avatar with distress level.
  2. Opening emotion (0-3 seconds) and whether it narrows or broadens attention, with the reason.
  3. Agitation emotions, intensity and duration.
  4. Transition trigger, permission language and bridge emotion.
  5. Solution emotions and the identity transformation promised.
  6. Action emotion (urgency or possibility) and the final push.
  7. 5-7 hook variations, each tagged with its emotion, narrowing or broadening, and the distress level it suits.
  8. Negative label addressed and positive label offered.
  9. Mirror moment within 3 seconds.
  10. Emotional permission granted.
  11. For high distress: how positive impairment is handled, how long to stay negative, and what validation comes first.
  12. Full script with [NARROWING] and [BROADENING] tags and a clearly marked transition.
  13. Visual concept supporting each phase.
  14. Emotional objections and fears addressed.
  15. Why it works, citing research findings and the avatar's profile.

[L0613.033]

Brief per avatar, in this order:

  1. Concept name.
  2. Emotional architecture: opening emotion (narrowing or broadening), agitation emotions, transition trigger, solution emotions, action emotion.
  3. Five hook options, each tagged with emotion and type.
  4. Identity strategy: negative label addressed, aspirational label offered, transition presentation.
  5. Mirror moment within 3 seconds.
  6. For high distress: how long to stay in negative territory, what validation is given and what permission is granted.
  7. Objections pre-empted.
  8. Best format and best time from the data.
  9. Script timing:
    • 0-3 s: hook (emotion)
    • 3-15 s: agitation (emotional development)
    • 15-25 s: transition (permission to hope)
    • 25-45 s: solution (positive emotion building)
    • 45-60 s: CTA (action emotion)

Choosing the format: static, image and video

  • Static formats include static image ads, catalog ads, advertorials, and listicles. Static image ads are the simplest starting point before custom video production. [L1195.034]
  • Test a book with several ad formats: long-form copywriting, native ads, catalyst ads, image ads and AI UGC ads. Each format needs heavy customization before the test reaches a usable stage. [L1175.037]
  • Video works best for products that need storytelling, use-case demonstration or an emotional or spiritual connection, while simple attractive products can work well with carousel or image ads. [L1158.030]
  • Image ads with the offer printed on them can be sufficient for some products, and they were the strongest creative in the lesson's own example. Whether a product needs video depends on the product itself. [L1219.032]
  • Fashion items can usually be tested with picture ads only, because they do not need video to sell. Compare CTR and store reaction before adding video. [L1219.033]
  • For fashion products targeting an audience aged 45 and over, picture ads (single image, carousel, or slideshow) often outperform video ads. This was observed on fashion products in Australia, so it should be tested rather than assumed. [L1212.011]
  • Test social-proof creatives alongside value-proposition creatives instead of relying on one format. [L1016.027]
  • Show the product in use in short video, because motion and experience carry the emotional part of the message. [L1016.028]

Testimonial and review ads

Structure and length

  • A workable testimonial ad states the core benefits first and then adds testimonials as proof. Placing the testimonials after the benefit claims lets social proof back up what the ad has already promised. [L1348.022]
  • Use no more than three testimonials in a single ad. A short slideshow of three brief testimonials works well, but more than three wastes viewer time. For long, full video testimonials, use only one per ad so the viewer is not bored. [L1348.014]
  • Testimonial length determines how many to show: short one-line testimonials can be stacked, while a long story-style testimonial (a customer describing their full journey) should appear only once in an ad. [L1348.015]
  • Testimonial ads can run as static images or as video, and both formats performed fairly well in testing. Animated testimonial sequences, where one quote rolls into the next with subtle movement such as a slight breathing motion, were reported to work well. [L1348.023]
  • A pop-up social proof format shows many comments or reviews appearing one after another (pop, pop, pop) until the screen fills. This displays a lot of social proof without boring the viewer. Build it with real or designed comment cards and sound effects in a video editor. [L1348.016]

Sourcing and testing

  • Real customer comments taken from your own store or product page are a lower-risk option than invented comments, because the feedback is genuine. Recreating a comment card by hand takes more design effort, so real feedback is the simpler starting point. [L1348.029]
  • Run customer reviews and testimonials as ad creatives on Meta, even when there are only around 11 reviews. Split test two or three review or UGC creatives inside one ad set. [L1225.050]
  • Testimonial and review-style videos work particularly well for retargeting, because viewers have already seen the ads but not acted. Pair them with an offer in the ad copy, headline, or description. [L1335.022]

Procedure: learn to produce a testimonial ad

  1. Find examples from large established brands known to run testimonial ads. [L1348.012]
  2. Search for "testimonial ads" in Foreplay and Google. [L1348.012]
  3. Study which testimonial ads are clean and well spaced. [L1348.012]
  4. Build your own version from these references instead of starting from scratch. [L1348.012]

Low-cost creatives

  • Two low-cost ad creatives need no production team: a screenshot of the checkout page showing the discount or savings, and a screen recording of browsing the site with a voiceover from the customer's point of view. Both can run alongside other creatives in the same test. [L1225.094]

Problem and demonstration ads

  • A problem-scene creative shows the target buyer in a situation where the product's problem occurs, such as a rider whose headphones do not fit under a helmet, and then shows the product solving it. Name the specific problem in the ad copy as well, so the viewer recognises the situation quickly. [L1225.095]
  • Ads that open with a relatable before-and-after story about a specific pet problem attract owners who are in a similar situation. Emotional storytelling was used to make people feel the problem before the advertorial. [L1131.023]
  • A lifestyle video in which a creator presents a product as their own new tech and shows it in daily use performed strongly organically. The video showed the product as a solution in the creator's own routine rather than as an advert. [L1444.034]

Us-versus-them and comparison ads

  • An us-versus-them ad positions the product against a clearly weaker alternative, such as an inferior method or a poor competitor, so the product looks better by comparison. This works through anchoring, because the viewer judges the product against a low benchmark. [L1348.024]
    • Example: "Our 3-layer hiking socks vs. cotton socks that soak up sweat" [L1348.024]
    • Example: "Our cordless mop vs. a bucket and rag that just spreads grime." [L1348.024]
  • Comparison ads can work well: start by comparing against an everyday alternative, such as a café coffee at about $6 a cup, rather than attacking an established brand directly. [L1016.025]
  • A before-and-after comparison can show the time and effort of a competitor's option, such as a barista coffee bought on the run versus a quicker product, matching the time-saving value lever. [L1016.024]
  • Direct brand-on-brand comparison carries pushback risk and is best used once the brand has enough traction, and only when the brand is ready to respond to a counterattack. [L1016.026]

Listicle and advertorial ads

  • List-style advertorial headline. Formula:
    1. Promise a specific count of reasons the product matters. [L0999.080]
    2. Tie it to a status signal the reader wants. [L0999.080]
    3. Present the points as a numbered list before the sales page. [L0999.080]
  • This works because a numbered promise sets clear expectations, the status tie makes the reader want to see if they belong, and the list makes reading easy so the sales page feels earned. [L0999.080]
  • Examples: "7 reasons home bakers are switching to a magnetic spice rack" and "5 reasons outdoor runners keep a reflective vest in their car." [L0999.080]

Print and mail formats

  • Postcards suit consumer mailings with simple offers and existing relationships, but are weaker in business-to-business mail where gatekeepers discard B-pile mail. Large fold-out self-mailers that mail flat without an envelope, and roadmap postcards that unfold to about eight pages, are useful alternatives for longer copy. [L1370.035]
  • Tear-sheet or advertorial mailing: a piece made to look like a page torn from a newspaper or magazine, with a handwritten sticky note from a friend. Because people read articles more than ads, such mailings can start with 30 to 50 percent of recipients reading them, compared with about 10 percent for a standard sales letter. [L1370.036]
    • Example: a sheet styled as a magazine article with a sticky note reading "Thought you would want to see this, J." [L1370.036]

Lead magnet and reward ads

  • Ad copy for a lead magnet offers a free, related resource instead of a discount or a product. Formula: free + specific related topic + a short call to action such as "get your copy." [L1076.006]
    • Example: "Free guide: how to choose your first dog harness. Get your copy." [L1076.006]
    • Example: "Free checklist: the history of camping stoves. Grab yours." [L1076.006]
  • Offer a reward item in the ad that the prospect claims by returning a request form with qualifying questions. The answers show which products each responder is likely to want and build a targeted list for follow-up. [L1261.147]

Giveaways

  • Giveaway contests attract people who want free items rather than buyers, so avoid them when the goal is paying customers. [L1016.064]

Social proof and post-engagement ads

  • Formula for a post-engagement ad that asks for social proof: say that the customer loves the product, invite them to comment with a photo or description, and mention a small reward. Keep the request simple for older audiences by asking only for a written comment. [L1243.040]
    • Example 1: "Enjoying your new mug? Tell us what you think in the comments and get a free sticker pack." [L1243.040]
    • Example 2: "Got a photo with your jacket? Post it in the comments and we will send you a discount code." [L1243.040]
  • Post-engagement ads are cheap to run and can be linked to Minichat for an automatic reward. Successful page posts can be reused in other campaigns as layered social proof, showing comments and photos to cold audiences who are asked to buy. [L1243.043]

Trusted advisor and review headline ads (older advice, 2021-2022)

  • Reuse the product image from the product reminder ad in the trusted advisor ad, since the shopper already knows it from the product page. Change the headline, copy and review so the ad feels new. [L1398.003]
  • Formula for the opening line of a review-based ad: name a group of happy buyers, state what they say about the product, and name the product in the same line. It works because the first line is the only copy most viewers see, and a clear summary with a named product creates enough curiosity to tap more. [L1398.009]
    • Example 1: "Home cooks are raving about the Alpine cast-iron skillet, and it cooks evenly on any stove." [L1398.009]
    • Example 2: "Dog owners say the Cloud-soft bed is worth every penny for older pets." [L1398.009]
  • Key attributes of a trusted advisor ad: [L1398.010]

Creator and whitelist (native) ads

  • Pitch the same product in two voices. The brand voice is a direct, company-led call to buy, while the whitelist voice is a first-person usage story that includes a time frame and a result. It works because the brand voice informs, while the personal voice reads as a recommendation. [L1474.011]
    • Example 1, brand: "Get the new 1 L steel bottle, now in stock." [L1474.011]
    • Example 1, whitelist: "I took this 1 L bottle on three 10 km runs this month and it has not leaked once." [L1474.011]
    • Example 2, brand: "Our new collagen drink is here, order today." [L1474.011]
    • Example 2, whitelist: "I added this collagen drink to my morning routine for 21 days, and my energy stays steady until lunch." [L1474.011]
  • Formula for ads run from a character page: open with a personal aside that sounds like a real confession or a repeated question the character gets, then promise a specific number of tips or steps, then name the one tool or product the character uses. Show the character as the sponsor with a Sponsored label, a destination domain under the creative, a headline naming the concrete outcome, and a Learn More call to action. [L1475.018]
    • It works because it reads as content from a person first and an offer second, with a concrete promise that invites clicks. [L1475.018]
    • Example 1: "Honest confession: my knee pain was ignored for two years until I changed one habit with my running shoes. Here are the three checks I run before buying any pair now, plus the insole I still swear by." Headline: "3 Checks Before Buying Running Shoes". [L1475.018]
    • Example 2: "Every week someone asks me how I keep my kitchen grease-free without harsh chemicals. Here are four cheap habits that changed how often I clean, plus the one spray I keep on the counter." Headline: "4 Kitchen Habits That Cut My Cleaning Time". [L1475.018]
  • Case: a fictional character page started at zero followers. Ads ran from the page for one month, from March to April. The page reached about 1,000 followers organically through the ads, and people chose to follow via the ad itself, without buying followers. Zero followers did not stop people from buying. [L1476.024]
  • Native ads must always run from a whitelist page and should not be split-tested against a brand page. Running native ads from a branded page ruins the ad, because native ads only work when they look like an ordinary post by a person. [L1476.025]
  • Whitelist ad copy is written in an organic, personal voice, not a brand voice. Brand copy uses direct selling such as "buy today, 50% off, see the reviews", while whitelist copy opens with a personal hook, names a question the character is often asked, promises a short concrete takeaway and mentions the product in passing. Keep the static ad layout the same and swap only the brand name and logo for the character's face. [L1476.031]
    • Example 1: "Real talk: I used to wake up exhausted every single day. Here are the 3 wind-down steps I still follow, and the one thing that made the biggest difference." [L1476.031]
    • Example 2: "I get DMs about my knife every week, so I finally wrote down why I stopped buying cheap ones. Here is what to check before you spend a dime." [L1476.031]

Further details

  • Post-it Notes Hook Framework: (1) Create a handwritten hook on a physical post-it note. (2) Record a 15-30 second video showing the post-it with the product and its benefits. (3) Leverage the personal appeal of handwriting and the uncommon format to stand out. [L0258.014]

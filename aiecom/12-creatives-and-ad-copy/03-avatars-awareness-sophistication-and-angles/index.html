12.3 Avatars, awareness, sophistication and angles

Awareness stages: the five levels

Schwartz's awareness scale has five stages, counted from unaware upward:

  • Stage 1, Unaware: does not know they have a problem.
  • Stage 2, Problem-Aware: knows the problem, does not know that solutions exist.
  • Stage 3, Solution-Aware: knows solutions exist, but not all options.
  • Stage 4, Product-Aware: knows your product, unsure whether it is right.
  • Stage 5, Most Aware: knows your product, may be skeptical or comparing.

Default to Stage 2 for cold traffic with new products unless research clearly indicates otherwise. [L0039.015]

Source C counts the same five levels from most aware downward:

  • Knows the product and wants it (most aware). [L0330.018]
  • Knows the product but does not want it yet (product-aware). [L0330.018]
  • Knows the desire but not the product (solution-aware). [L0330.018]
  • Knows the need but not the solution (problem-aware). [L0330.018]
  • Completely unaware of the desire (unaware). [L0330.018]
  • Each level is separated from the next by a psychological wall between indifference and strong interest. [L0330.018]

The same levels are described by what the prospect knows and what lead type suits them:

  • Most aware: knows the product and only needs the deal. [L0756.005]
  • Product-aware: knows the product but doubts it fits. [L0756.005]
  • Solution-aware: wants the result but does not know the product delivers it. [L0756.005]
  • Problem-aware: feels the problem but not that a solution exists. [L0756.005]
  • Completely unaware: no knowledge of the problem, product or category. [L0756.005]
  • The awareness level decides the lead type and how direct the copy should be. [L0756.005]

Source D describes the stages by what each reader knows:

  • Unaware readers do not see the problem. [L0772.037]
  • Problem-aware readers know the problem but not that a fix exists. [L0772.037]
  • Solution-aware readers know fixes exist but not your product. [L0772.037]
  • Product-aware readers know your product but are not yet convinced. [L0772.037]
  • Most-aware readers only need a reason to buy now. [L0772.037]

Each ad should be matched to one of the five stages. Each stage needs a different opening, because a reader who does not yet know they have a problem needs a different start from someone already looking for your product. [L0772.019]

Judging the stage and the copy implication

  • Classify each customer on Schwartz's five stages. Failed-method and competitor-name language signals later stages, while pure "struggling" language signals Problem-Aware. [L0039.031]
  • Immediately after classifying, extract the copy implication: one strategic directive that reshapes how the voice and approach must work for that stage. For example, for a Problem-Aware audience the voice must sound like a person describing how the world operates, not a polished advertiser trying to heal broken belief. [L0039.035]
  • To judge how unaware a prospect is, ask how new the product is in the market and whether similar products already exist. If similar products exist, the prospect has a frame of reference and is not fully unaware, so a more direct lead can be used. [L0756.057]
  • Use the stage to choose the ad angle. Buyers who do not yet recognize the problem respond better to ads that surface a need or desire. Buyers already comparing options respond better to comparison ads such as us-versus-them. Do not use one formula for every audience. [L1348.025]
  • Unaware customers do not know they have a problem, so the copy must first educate them about its consequences, for example by asking whether they have noticed a specific risk or a specific symptom. Once they recognize the problem, position the product as the solution. [L0516.006]
  • Problem-aware customers know the problem but not that a solution exists, so use problem-agitate-solution: state the problem, make it feel worse, then introduce the product. Use vivid, specific wording of the frustration to make it land. [L0516.007]
  • Solution-aware customers already know alternatives such as lint rollers or scrapers exist, so surface the problem and then show a unique feature that solves it in a newer or better way than those alternatives. Do not just repeat that a solution exists. [L0516.008]
  • Product-aware customers know your product but have not bought it, so highlight features that make it better than other products and address their common objections to ease the purchase decision. [L0516.009]
  • Match the copy to market awareness: an audience unaware of the problem needs education, an audience aware of the problem needs the solution, and an audience aware of solutions needs a clear reason to choose your product. A crowded market needs a unique mechanism or angle. [L0524.058]
  • Buyers who have tried many methods and now feel skeptical are usually problem-aware and leaning toward solution-aware. Lead with empathy for the methods that failed, not with a polished product pitch. [L1140.018]
  • Before writing, identify where the prospect sits on Schwartz's five levels. The level decides the lead type and how direct the copy should be. [L0756.005]
  • For problem-aware prospects, find the point of maximum anxiety: the frustration, desperation, fear or anger the problem causes. Opening with that emotion earns a connection before any product is named. [L0756.059]
  • The most aware buyers usually sit on the house list and among multi-buyers, the customers who have bought before and come back. These groups are the easiest to sell to, so the offer can be direct and short for them. [L0756.058]
  • Problem-aware prospects need the problem explained. Solution-aware prospects know several solutions exist and compare approaches. Product-aware prospects compare specific products or providers and need differentiators. [L0643.003]
  • Buyers sit at different points of awareness, from knowing they have a problem but not that solutions exist, to knowing a solution exists but not which one to choose. Copy works best when it speaks to the stage the reader is actually in, because that makes the seller seem to understand them. (older) [L1157.009]
  • When a buyer feels that the seller understands their problem better than they do, the buyer assumes the seller also has the solution. Copy that speaks at the buyer's current awareness level builds this trust and makes selling easier. (older) [L1157.006]

Writing for each stage: the three-text pattern

Use three primary texts, one for each of three awareness stages. The problem-aware text opens on the pain and hints that a solution exists. The solution-aware text admits that other options fell short and introduces the mechanism with proof. The transformation text shows life after the product, lists what is included and makes the offer clear. It works because each reader meets the message at the stage they are actually in. [L0348.039]

  • Problem-aware example, in italics: Tired of cold draughts creeping under the door every winter? There is a simpler way to seal them this weekend. [L0348.039]
  • Transformation example, in italics: Picture the first winter evening your living room stays warm without touching the thermostat. The full sealing kit arrives in one box. [L0348.039]

The product-journey ladder (a different model)

This is a second five-stage ladder about how familiar the buyer is with the product and the purchase. It is not Schwartz's awareness scale and should not be mixed with it.

  • Five stages run from not knowing the product exists, to considering it, to planning a purchase and needing details, to buying, and finally to habitual repeat use. Ads can cover all five stages in a single piece of copy, or a series of ads can move the prospect from one stage to the next over time. [L0340.028]
  • Start copy at the stage where the target audience is, not where the product is. Early-stage prospects need an explanation of what the product is, while late-stage buyers need reasons to choose it. [L0340.029]

Market sophistication: what the market has already heard

  • Market sophistication describes how exposed a market is to a claim, and it sets the type of headline. [L0515.004]
  • Market sophistication describes how many competing products and claims the audience has already seen. If you are first to market, state the basic claim. If you are second but the product is new to the audience, enlarge the claim the first seller made, for example from "cleans hair" to "cleans hair quicker than ever before." [L0516.011]
  • When the audience has heard many similar claims, lead with a new mechanism, meaning a unique feature that solves the problem in a better, more sophisticated way. When competitors copy that mechanism, exaggerate and amplify it further rather than inventing something unrelated. [L0516.012]

Source C's stage-by-stage headline types:

Sophistication stage Market situation Headline approach
Stage 1 First to market State the need or claim simply and directly. [L0515.004]
Stage 2 Claim already seen, product new Enlarge the claim and outbid competitors on the big benefit. [L0515.004]
Stage 3 Competitors have claimed everything Add a new mechanism or feature to the headline that solves the problem in a new, unique way. [L0515.004]
Stage 4 Competitors copy the mechanism Embellish the mechanism and solve more of the problem, or find a new feature that is more believable. [L0515.004]
Stage 5 Market no longer believes claims Shift from desire to identification, speaking to who the customer is and who they aspire to be, as large commodity brands do. [L0515.004]

Assessing sophistication for a product

  1. Pick the product and define the problem it solves. [L0515.005]
  2. Count the competitors selling the same product. [L0515.005]
  3. List the competitors solving the same problem with a different product (for example lint rollers or hard brushes). [L0515.005]
  4. Decide the sophistication stage from these counts and the claims already made, then choose the headline approach for that stage. [L0515.005]

[L0515.005]

Headlines at sophistication stage 2

Formula: state the core claim, then add a measured enlargement of its size, speed or count, with each new competitor trying to promise more than the last. Why it works: when the market has not yet seen the bigger number, a larger claim wins attention. The limit is reached when the claim becomes unbelievable. [L0330.052]

  • Lose up to 30 pounds in 6 weeks or get your money back. [L0330.052]
  • Get 60 hours of battery life, then 90, then 120 hours on the same device. [L0330.052]

Headlines at sophistication stage 3

Procedure for a market that has heard every claim and every extreme:

  1. Accept that repeating or exaggerating the old claim will no longer work. [L0330.054]
  2. Introduce a new mechanism that explains how the old promise is achieved now. [L0330.054]
  3. Move the emphasis from what the product does to how it works. [L0330.054]
  4. Feature the mechanism in the headline, reducing the old claim to a single short word or phrase that points to it. [L0330.054]
  5. Once the reader accepts that a new chance exists, restate the full claim in the lead paragraph to show everything they get. [L0330.054]

[L0330.054]

  • Formula: mechanism first, claim second. The headline names the new way the product works, in a short, striking phrase. The lead paragraph restates the full promise and the benefits it brings. Why it works: a familiar claim becomes fresh and believable again when it is explained through a new process. [L0330.055]
    • Melts the stubborn sweat patches out of your shirts before they set, the first laundry booster with a cooling enzyme. Lead: fresh shirts after a full day of heat, in one wash, with no scrubbing. [L0330.055]
    • The first electric kettle that heats from the bottom plate. Lead: the full promise of boiling a litre in under two minutes. [L0330.055]
  • Solution-aware markets: the headline should name the unique feature or mechanism instead of only the problem. [L0353.002, L0353.051]
    • Market awareness (Schwartz's stages) decides what the ad leads with. [L0353.002]
    • Schwartz's scale places a solution-aware market at stage three: buyers know the problem and know that competing solutions exist. The ad should not only restate the problem but show a unique feature or mechanism that sets the product apart from the alternatives. [L0353.051]

Headlines at sophistication stage 4

  • In the fourth stage, a competitor has just introduced a new mechanism that is selling. Respond by elaborating on that mechanism: make it easier, quicker or surer, let it solve more of the problem, overcome old limitations or promise extra benefits. This copy eventually loses believability, so the next move is a new mechanism that the market accepts. [L0330.056]
  • When a rival launches a new mechanism for the same claim and it sells, counter by making the approach easier, quicker and surer, letting it solve more of the problem, overcoming its old limits and promising extra benefits. This keeps you competitive in markets where promises keep escalating. [L1365.018]

Headlines for unaware and resistant markets

  • Introducing a new product to a prospect who wants what it does but does not yet know that the product exists:
    1. Name the desire or its solution in the headline, pinpointing the vague desire that is spreading. [L0330.031]
    2. Prove that the solution can actually be achieved. [L0330.031]
    3. Show that the mechanism that achieves it is contained in the product. [L0330.031]
    • Execution matters more than mechanics here. Success needs analysis of the right market, intuition to sense a trend early and to see when it becomes profitable, and verbal creativity to give a name to what people feel but have not yet expressed. [L0330.031]
  • When the market is completely unaware or resistant, first eliminate three headline options: do not mention the price or any price reduction; do not put the product name in the headline or prominent display type; do not state directly what the product does, what desire it satisfies or what problem it solves. Each of these is either meaningless or will fail at that stage. [L0330.035]
  • At the most unaware stage, the headline must be an identification headline. It calls the market together around an emotion, attitude or situation it already feels, sells nothing directly, and exists only to get the reader into the next paragraph. Each paragraph then pulls the reader into the next, moving from identification to awareness of the problem or desire, to the realisation that a solution exists, and finally to the product. [L0330.036]
  • An identification headline must be the prospect's own headline, describing their problem and their state of mind right now. It should attract only the logical prospects and repel everyone else. It must not be a cute, funny or shocking line with no real headline behind it. [L0330.037]
  • Before writing the headline, answer three questions: what mass desire drives the market, how much the market already knows about how the product satisfies it (state of awareness), and how many similar products the market has already seen (state of sophistication). The first answer gives the force. The second and third give the position of the market relative to the product and therefore the content of the headline. [L0330.017]
  • A headline that works at one awareness level usually fails at another, and a headline that worked for a market will stop working once that market moves to a new level. Re-check the awareness level before reusing any headline. [L0330.019]

Avatar document

The avatar document has six required analysis sections. Ground every claim in verbatim research data. [L0039.031, L0039.026]

Section What to write
(1) Awareness stage Classify on Schwartz's 5 stages using the rule that failed-method and competitor-name language signals later stages while pure "struggling" language signals Problem-Aware, then state the copy implication. [L0039.031]
(2) Core fear / fear direction The single dominant fear underneath the surface complaint, which direction it points (future dread vs. present shame), quoted from data. [L0039.031]
(3) Failed methods What customers tried that didn't work, phrased as "this will never work" reasoning that copy can lean on. [L0039.031]
(4) Emotional drivers and trigger moments The specific moment or circumstance that makes them buy. [L0039.031]
(5) Their own words Verbatim swipe list of exact phrases customers use for the problem and desired outcome, to be echoed in copy without replacing them with marketing language. [L0039.031]
(6) Angle map Distinct angles (each = fear + mechanism), ranked by data support. [L0039.031]
  • In the avatar, ground every claim about the customer in verbatim data from your research. If the research is thin on a point, state that explicitly instead of inferring or inventing customer psychology. [L0039.026]
  • Give the avatar a name, an age and a photo, then write ad copy and emails as if speaking directly to that one person. A concrete, named person makes copy more specific than a broad demographic label. [L1243.014]

Desires, emotions and the reason behind them

  • Each desire should be paired with the emotional reason behind it, such as fear of being embarrassed, feeling unhygienic or ashamed of the home. The reason ("why") is what gives the copy its tension, so a desire without its why produces weaker messages. [L0516.002]
  • Sell what the buyer wants, then deliver what they need. Buyers rarely identify their needs, but they describe their wants clearly. [L0378.074]

Wants and needs sort

  1. Draw two columns on a sheet of paper. Label one "Wants" and the other "Needs". [L0378.073]
  2. List everything the buyer says they want in the first column, and everything they actually need in the second. [L0378.073]
  3. Write your sales message around the wants, then deliver the needs as the actual solution. [L0378.073]

[L0378.073]

  • A strong ad angle combines three parts: a feeling the buyer has right now (for example present self-blame rather than fear of a future problem), a mechanism that explains why earlier attempts failed without blaming the buyer, and a stated reason the angle stands apart from competitors. Before using the feeling, confirm it appears across many survey responses in the buyers' own words, otherwise it is only the seller's assumption. [L1140.033]
  • Competitors often speak to a symptom, such as a child's behavior. An angle about how the buyer privately judges themselves, for example feeling like they failed, can stand apart if buyers repeat that feeling in the survey. [L1140.017]

Angle research and angle banks

  • An angle is a separate layer from format and concept: it is the argument or approach the ad takes to the viewer. Do not confuse angle, concept and format when reading a competitor's data. [L0374.010]
  • Mark any angle that is well-supported by avatar data but already saturated in the market. These angles have emotional validity but need a fresh mechanism or differentiated positioning to stand out against existing competitor ads. Saturation does not eliminate an angle; it signals the need for innovation within that emotional framework. [L0039.037]
  • When a niche's main angle becomes saturated, reframe the same product for a different buyer, such as a gift bought for a partner instead of a purchase for oneself. Test that new angle as its own ad set before dropping the product. [L1315.051]
  • Compare the buyer breakdown in the ad account (for example by gender) with the people shown in the creative. If the actual buyers differ from the people in the ad, make a new set of creatives aimed at the real buyer group. [L1315.052]
  • Build a unique marketing angle by attacking the problem from different perspectives, so the same product can be marketed to different audiences or platforms. For example, Facebook for one group and TikTok for a younger group affected by the same problem. [L0513.016]
  • Create ad angles that match the problem: one shows the product as protection from the cold, another shows use with coffee and friends, and a third shows the product as a gift. Men also buy it, so the audience can stay open to both genders. [L0492.045]
  • Marketing angles generated for zodiac jewelry included showing off a star sign, a birthday or holiday gift, self-expression, and compatibility and connection between signs. A lead-generation quiz could return each buyer's matches as a one-page PDF and unlock a discount after completion. [L0549.035]
  • Also target a secondary problem identified in the product map, such as convenience of using the product at home or saving time, within the same picture. (older) [L0580.013]
  • Turn the research into concepts: from the collected data, list five ad angles (for example comfort, sustainability, more confidence, better sleep). Combine research with consumer-behavior principles such as survival, enjoyment of life, life extension, enjoyment of food and drink, envy, jealousy, curiosity, expression of beauty and style, economy, profit, convenience and efficiency to refine each angle. [L0902.013]
  • Pick the most promising angles by checking what customers repeat in Amazon and Reddit reviews. [L0646.063]

Angle selection procedure

  1. List the marketing angles for the product. [L0646.063]
  2. Check what customers repeat in Amazon and Reddit reviews to see which angle is most likely to land. [L0646.063]
  3. Ask ChatGPT which audience is more likely to respond, keeping in mind its data may only go back to 2021, and judge its answer against the review evidence. [L0646.063]
  4. Produce one concept per angle, or at least the most promising one if time is short. [L0646.063]

[L0646.063]

  • A video that mentions a product's flaw as a joke or confession can attract many comments and views, but views alone do not show buying intent. Test whether the audience actually buys before scaling a honesty-style angle, because it can work well or fail badly depending on the product. [L0655.001]
  • A video with 100,000 views that converts well can be worth more than a video with one million views that attracts an unrelated audience, because conversion depends on the viewers' fit with the product. [L0795.028]

Building one angle

  1. Choose the problem type and write one overview line naming the product, the problem theme and the target customer. [L0392.002]
  2. Write the problem or hook: the visible symptom the customer sees first. [L0392.002]
  3. Write two to three agitate lines that make the problem practical and concrete (what it does to breathing, cleanliness, time or social life). [L0392.002]
  4. Write one or two feeling lines describing how the customer feels because of the problem. [L0392.002]
  5. Write the solution in one line, naming the product category and its main claim. [L0392.002]
  6. List the features in the "what it does" row, each as a short claim with a visible result. [L0392.002]
  7. For each key feature, write a meaning line describing what this changes in the customer's home or day. [L0392.002]
  8. Write the outcome: the emotional result the customer reaches after using the product. [L0392.002]
  9. Write the avoid-negative-outcome row: what happens if the customer does not buy. [L0392.002]
  10. Add social proof, such as customer testimonials that match this angle's problem. [L0392.002]
  11. Write the call to action with a concrete offer. [L0392.002]
  12. Start a new column for each different problem type, and keep columns separate so each one can be tested as its own angle. [L0392.002]

Angle patterns for specific audiences

  • Gift-based angle: frame the product as a gift from a partner or friend and describe the feeling it creates, so the customer sees a reason to buy for someone else or for themselves. Example for a ring, in italics: My partner surprised me with this ring and it makes me feel seen every time I wear it. Example for a keychain, in italics: My sister gave me this so I always feel safe walking home at night. [L0999.043]
  • Cultural or identity-based angle: name a specific group or identity the product speaks to, and show how the product fits their life, which makes the creative more intimate and easier to target. Example: a jewelry piece for couples in a specific culture, described as worn for a special family occasion. Example: a hair brush for owners of large dogs that shed onto beds, shown with a before and after result. [L0999.044]
  • Pattern for a niche angle: take a product that a broad audience sees as generic, name a specific situation in which it is used, and let the copy describe that situation. This works because the viewer can imagine using the product right away, which sells more than a general claim.
    • Example 1: a stainless steel travel mug for early-morning commutes on cold days. [L1161.037]
    • Example 2: a desk lamp for late-night study sessions in a shared room. [L1161.037]
  • For products that appeal mainly to men, combine the product with a concrete situation where it is used. This narrows the message and helps a weak conversion rate. [L1161.038]
  • A broad ad for a watch case asks whether you want to keep your watches in a box, which speaks to everyone and stands out less. A segment-specific ad speaks to the traveler's situation of choosing the right watch for each outfit on a trip abroad. [L0998.029]
  • Models in an ad should look like the person the viewer wants to become, because viewers are more attracted to the product when the person in the ad matches their aspiration. [L1184.044]
  • For ads targeting older audiences, use a younger person speaking to the older audience, because older viewers want to look young and do not identify with ads showing people their own age. [L1184.045]

Angle patterns for products

  • Show the target audience using the product in the real setting, such as a cyclist or snowboarder, and name the specific problem it solves. Avoid generic product shots on plain backgrounds for cold traffic. [L1225.046]
  • Use a gifting angle with a product that is a gift, such as showing the item as the thing a buyer's family member would want. Keep the copy simple and clear about what the product is. [L1225.084]
  • The same product can be marketed with different angles, such as a gift for couples, a memorial keepsake or a pregnancy milestone. Using different angles keeps the product fresh and gives you unique content. [L1171.041]
  • Same product, different angle: take a product that already sells broadly and promote it to a specific audience with its own interest or problem, then brand the product page to match.
    • Example: a foldable mat promoted to yoga teachers for studio use. [L1185.028]
    • Example: a phone stand promoted to home bakers for recipe videos. [L1185.028]
  • Angle for children's toys: position the toy as a way to reduce a child's screen time by offering a hands-on or educational activity instead.
    • Example of your own: a building kit that replaces tablet time with a 20-minute task. [L1176.037]
    • Example of your own: a drawing set pitched as a screen-free weekend activity for kids. [L1176.037]
  • Family-benefit angle for educational toys: frame the product around reducing children's screen time and creating shared quality time with parents or grandparents.
    • Example: a wooden building kit that replaces an hour of tablet time. [L1185.030]
    • Example: a puzzle set designed for weekend time with grandchildren. [L1185.030]
  • When a product has a technology feature, sell it as a classic or traditional look that keeps the buyer from falling behind on modern function. The formula is to name the timeless style the buyer already wants, then add the modern capability they fear missing. It works because it narrows the message to one specific audience and ties the product to status and belonging.
    • Example 1, in italics: Keep the warm look of a wooden desk lamp without working under dim, outdated light. [L0997.030]
    • Example 2, in italics: Carry a classic leather wallet that still works with every new payment app. [L0997.030]

Naming angles and concepts

  • An angle is the argument or approach the ad takes to the viewer. It is separate from the concept and the format. [L0374.010]
  • Naming: for each distinct angle, give a clear name. [L0039.009]

Angle-naming prompt

Combine the fears and angles discovered in customer research with the gaps between what competitors currently advertise and what the market isn't hearing. For each distinct angle, provide a clear name, state the underlying fear and mechanism in one line, cite the supporting evidence from customer data and market gaps, assess whether competitors are already saturating this angle or whether it remains open territory, and rank by expected strength, with well-supported customer fears that competitors ignore rising to the top.

[L0039.009]

  • To give a name to what people feel but have not yet expressed, use verbal creativity. This is part of introducing a new product to a market that does not yet know it. [L0330.031]
  • Renaming an old product can create a new market appeal. The South Beach Diet was initially named "Modified Carbohydrate Diet" and failed until it was renamed. Apple's iPod was marketed as "1,000 songs in your pocket" though it was not the first MP3 player. [L1356.055]

Angle-choice prompt

From the angles you listed, choose the most unique one, meaning one that most competitors in this niche do not use. Explain why it is unique, how often buyers mention the underlying feeling, and which emotion and desired outcome it connects. Keep the answer as a short paragraph.

Planning a new ad (concept development)

  1. State the problem in plain words, for example that you want to sell this product. [L1366.086]
  2. Restate the problem in a different way, for example that you want to introduce the unique aspects of the product to the prospect. [L1366.086]
  3. Restate it again in terms of the buyer's experience, for example that the product should be easy to buy and seem fun. [L1366.086]
  4. Choose the restatement that feels closest to the ad you want to write. [L1366.086]
  5. List several big ideas for that restatement and pick the one or two that make the most sense. [L1366.086]
  6. Visualize how the concept fits into the ad and check it against the restatement. [L1366.086]
  7. Stop and incubate. [L1366.086]
  8. Write the headline, then the subheadline, then the first sentence. [L1366.086]
  9. Block-diagram the finished ad to check its flow. [L1366.086]

Unique selling points and value propositions

  • Value propositions can be functional (solve a technical or practical need, typical for tech or electronics) or emotional (typical for kids' products and beauty products). Define one value proposition per customer archetype based on which reason drives the purchase. [L0902.010]
  • List the unique selling points of best-selling products, such as manufacturing process, eco-friendly features, press coverage or charity donations, because these can serve as credible marketing angles. [L0902.012]
  • Compare products by a single value-based claim that rivals cannot easily copy, because copied general claims become commonplace and lose value. Look for a unique, specific, verifiable advantage. [L1261.082]
  • The USP formula from Rosser Reeves has three parts: the product must actually do what the copy says it does, the benefit must be original compared with competitors, and the promise must target a core desire the prospect already has. Desire cannot be created, only awakened. [L0756.030]
  • The unique selling proposition in the offer must make the service different, typically through the phrasing "The only service that...". A USP can be created in three ways:
    1. By genuinely unique features like a proprietary system or patented technology. [L1356.053]
    2. By labeling a common feature as unique because competitors do not talk about it. [L1356.053]
    3. By giving an old thing a new name. [L1356.053]
  • Labeling a common feature as unique works by highlighting something every competitor also has but no one mentions. For example, a manufacturer's gold-plated connectors that all rivals also use but never advertised. The reader treats the newly highlighted detail as distinctive. [L1356.054]

Models, presenters and identity in creatives

  • Ads should use a wide variety of avatars, including different genders, ages and ethnicities, to promote the same product. This variety allows testing which audience responds best. [L1187.021]
  • Models in an ad should look like the person the viewer wants to become. [L1184.044]
  • A well-known person can be used as an opponent or challenge rather than an endorser, because a challenge creates a story and an endorsement only adds a name. Build the ad around the contest and make the product the thing being challenged. Get written permission before publishing any name, since an unauthorized use caused a legal dispute in the original case. [L1366.084]

Copy flow across stages on multi-page stores (older)

Units in this group come from the 2021-22 course and are marked older.

  • Long-form sales pages make it easier to test different messages and to address different awareness segments. Traffic sources can also be used to send each awareness segment to a different message. (older) [L0904.011]
  • When a store has many product pages, write the copy so it starts at the Problem Aware stage instead of writing separate copy for each stage. Copy that starts at Problem Aware works for visitors at that stage and at every stage above it up to purchase. (older) [L0904.012]
  • Copy that starts at a later stage such as Product Aware does not work for visitors who are at an earlier stage. Copy that starts at Unaware works for all stages, but it is harder to write well. (older) [L0904.013]
  • People who are at Problem Aware or above are easy to target because they already know their pain and what they want to solve. Using one copy flow that starts with the pain is simpler than writing different copy for each awareness stage. (older) [L0904.015]

Copy flow procedure (older)

  1. Name the customer's pain or problem first, since Problem Aware visitors recognize it. [L0904.014]
  2. Build the desire for a solution by showing how the solution addresses that problem. [L0904.014]
  3. Lead the reader through the options to the point of purchase within the same copy. [L0904.014]

[L0904.014]

  • Example for a surfboard store selling to beginners (older): the Problem Aware stage is the realization that they have no board and cannot surf without one. The Solution Aware stage is choosing between renting and buying. The Product Aware stage is comparing beginner boards by budget, shape, style and brand. The Ninja Aware stage is choosing a specific brand, shape and style from a specific seller. This course adds Ninja Aware after Product Aware, which is not part of Schwartz's five stages. [L0904.016]
  • At the Ninja Aware stage, the solution itself creates the desire. Once a buyer understands how a solution works, they want the products that deliver it, so copy for late-stage buyers should connect the product to the solution the buyer has already accepted. (older) [L0904.020]

Testing, planning and checklists

  • Ads aimed at product-aware audiences differ from ads aimed at unaware audiences, a framework covered in the classic book Breakthrough Advertising. Creating creatives for unaware audiences is a way to grow a product that has already reached its aware market. [L1127.029]
  • Ads that run a long time can keep working even after a product is obsolete, as long as the core need and buyer motivation are unchanged. Work out the buyer's underlying need so the copy stays valid across product cycles. [L1366.070]

Pre-launch ad checklist

  1. Does it tap into a desire the audience already has? [L1348.026]
  2. Does it show the desirable outcome the customer can reach with the product? [L1348.026]

Research before writing

  • Research the buyer before writing any copy, because the copy only works if the writer truly understands the buyer. [L1370.002]
  • Visualise a typical recipient's whole day, from arrival at work to how and when their mail reaches them. Note what else occupies their attention at that moment. [L1370.002]
  • Study the reader before the product. Find the features that match the reader's needs and build the letter around them. A letter that starts from the buyer's reactions and presents only the matching product features is almost always effective. [L1369.039]
  • Let the buyer's priorities drive the copy, not the seller's. A sales letter gets no live feedback to correct a misunderstanding. [L1370.004]
  • A corporate buyer weighing a donation or purchase typically asks three things: [L1370.004]
    1. What benefit justifies the cost?
    2. Who else already chose this?
    3. Where will the money come from in the budget? [L1370.004]

Ad angles and concept building

  • Define the ad angle before the script. An ad angle is a specific take on a problem for a target audience. [L1425.027]
  • Not every product solves a problem. Some products fulfil a need, such as self-expression, identity, or a hobby. For these, connect the ad emotionally to the desired feeling rather than to a problem. [L1426.019]

Lateral thinking exercise for a stuck ad concept:

  1. Pick a random source of words, such as a shuffled set of words or a dictionary. [L1366.087]
  2. Pull out the first three words you see. [L1366.087]
  3. Force a connection between those three words and the product you are selling. [L1366.087]
  4. Build an ad concept from the connection, keeping the selling goal in mind. [L1366.087]

Awareness stage and funnel messaging

  • For audiences who do not yet know a product category exists, or do not realise they have a problem it solves, do not push a purchase. Open with the symptom or desire they already feel, such as tiredness. Explain the benefits and present the product as a possible answer, so the first step is awareness, not a sale. [L1386.018]
  • Showing a buy-now offer to people who are not ready to buy wastes ad spend. It also drives away prospects who would have converted later. Serve the right message at each funnel stage to keep those prospects in the funnel instead of losing them. [L1386.019]

On-screen models

  • Use one consistent on-screen model across all videos. In competitor videos, a consistent model increased sales, especially when the product targeted women. Using her in every video can build recognition. [L1444.032]

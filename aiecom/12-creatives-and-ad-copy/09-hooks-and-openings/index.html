12.9 Hooks and openings

What the opening has to do

  • A hook is the opening line in the first 1-3 seconds of a video. It stops the scroll and starts a full watch. It works by triggering one of seven psychological patterns, and it decides whether the algorithm sees a watch completion or an exit. [L0160.001]
  • The seven triggers are: direct problem relevance, comparison against current solutions, personal authority from experience, curiosity about outcomes, fear of missing out, daily necessity, and removal of cost barriers. [L0160.001]
  • The hook is arguably the most important ad element. It must stop the scroll and keep the viewer watching, and it should highlight a fact, a problem, the key benefit or the unique selling point. [L1425.006]
  • The first 3 to 5 seconds decide whether a viewer keeps watching. If the hook fails, nobody sees the rest of the ad, however good the offer. [L0707.007, L0777.004]
  • The first seconds must stop the scroll and show what the product does right away. Shocking, unexpected or user-generated content (UGC) can achieve this. [L0777.004]
  • Attention is only the first step. The opening must also win interest, or attention fades and is much harder to win back. [L1369.040]
  • A strong opening drives the click, not only views beyond the hook. [L0950.040]
  • A strong ad calls out a problem or a big benefit in the first seconds, so viewers are already qualified buyers. Attention-grabbing content that has no relation to the product attracts viewers who will not buy. [L0640.020]
  • A hook should relate to the product, the video and the viewer's own situation, and appeal to emotion. Statements viewers can instantly relate to hold attention longer and improve conversion. [L0764.008]
  • Name the audience directly in hooks and copy, for example the profession or life situation. People outside the group keep scrolling, people inside it qualify themselves, and clicks are filtered toward likely buyers. [L1426.044]
  • The first three seconds should target one specific audience and show either the pain point or the relief. The right people stay and others skip the ad. [L0871.062]
  • Simple copy with a clear hook in the first seconds matters most, because the opening seconds decide whether viewers keep watching. [L1176.038]

Timing windows

Sources give different time values. Each value applies to a different element of the opening.

  • The hook must appear within the first 2 to 3 seconds of the video. [L0764.005]
  • A hook lasts 1 to 3 seconds, ideally 1.5 to 2 seconds. [L0764.006]
  • The first 3 to 5 seconds are the hook. Test that window separately from the rest of the video. [L0902.018]
  • The first 3 to 5 seconds should show the pain point, the face or the problem, and then introduce the product. [L0638.035]
  • Reach the solution within about 3 to 5 seconds. A product introduction that takes around 10 seconds loses viewers before the benefit appears. [L0640.048]
  • If an ad takes 10 seconds or more to introduce the product, jump straight to demonstrating the benefit (the action) within 3 to 5 seconds, then introduce the product. [L0067.021]
  • Show the product in the first five seconds. Videos that delay the product tend to underperform unless they are strong unboxings. This applies to custom and sourced content. [L1377.021]
  • On TikTok, show the offer within the first three seconds, because the audience needs to recognise a product is being sold, otherwise attention drops. [L1177.027]
  • For video ads, the first three seconds must show how the product works as the attention grabber. After those three seconds, show how it is installed or used. [L0562.032]

Problem first or product first

Sources disagree on what the first frame should show.

  • Problem first: Show the problem the product solves, not its features or specifications. Do not lead with features such as "battery lasts 3 hours" or "rechargeable". Show the dirty lawn first, then the clean lawn, so customers understand how the product helps. [L0280.016, L0280.017]
  • Problem first, for problem-solving products: Bold claims and pain points are the most useful for product-led ads. The first seconds should show the audience's problem, the face, or the problem before the product appears. [L0398.010, L0638.035]
  • Result or demonstration first: Show the working product in the first three seconds (video ads), or show what the product does right away. [L0562.032, L0777.004]
  • Product in first five seconds: Delayed products tend to underperform unless the video is a strong unboxing. [L1377.021]
  • Use the problem or the demonstration: Both are valid. Apply the problem-first rule to features-heavy or problem-led products, and the demonstration rule when the video is a product demo. The sources give no further conditions beyond the 10-second rule in L0067.021 and the unboxing exception in L1377.021. [L0280.017, L0562.032, L1377.021, L0067.021]

Hook categories and choosing one

  • Three common hook categories work for Facebook video: [L0398.010]
Category What it does Best use
Bold claim A direct, specific statement about the product (long proven) Product-led ads
Pain point Shows the problem the audience has Problem-solving products
Suspense Builds curiosity and entertains the right audience at once (TikTok-style) Short-form feeds
  • Bold claims and pain points are the most useful for product-led ads. Suspense is strong for short-form feeds. [L0398.010]
  • Classify each hook by the 13-pattern set. [L0613.012]
    1. Claim size (for example an exact monthly income figure)
    2. Claim speed (a fast result)
    3. Authority figure
    4. Before-and-after
    5. Comparison to rivals
    6. Removal of limitations (for example "even if you have no experience")
    7. Question
    8. Information offer
    9. Newness
    10. Exclusivity
    11. Belief challenge
    12. Direct call-out to a specific person
    13. Identity-based hook [L0613.012]

Problem and pain hooks

  • Problem identification and solution: State the specific pain point your audience experiences, then introduce the product as the fix. Formula: "[If you / Stop scrolling if] you [specific pain]," then offer what fixes it. It works because viewers who have that exact problem recognise themselves and stop to see the solution. [L0160.002]
    • "Stop scrolling if you're tired of planners you never actually use."
    • "If you waste 30 minutes every morning choosing an outfit, this changes everything." [L0160.002]
  • Problem-first hook formula: Name the viewer's exact problem in the first sentence, using the words the target customer uses. Add "this is for you" or an equivalent so the right person keeps watching. The viewer identifies with the problem within a second and stops scrolling. [L0392.004]
    • "If your kitchen floor is covered in crumbs every single morning, then this is for you."
    • "If your car smells like wet dog every time you drive, then this is for you." [L0392.004]
  • Problem-first, product as direct fix: Name the customer's everyday problem in the first line, then present the product as the direct fix. The viewer recognises their situation and understands the benefit at once. [L0562.020]
    • "Tired of cold feet at your desk? Our heated foot warmer keeps them toasty all day."
    • "Sick of your car windows freezing at 7 am? This quick-defrost cover clears the glass in seconds." [L0562.020]
  • Problem plus benefit with a visible symptom: Start with a question or statement that names a specific problem and promises a benefit for a named body area or use case, then show the product doing that job immediately. [L0657.011]
    • "Still waking up with puffy eyes? Watch this gel reduce swelling in 10 seconds."
    • "Worried about slipping on wet tile? See how these shower slippers grip the floor." [L0657.011]
  • Symptom-first (Source A): Name the visible problem the buyer already notices, state what it costs them in time, money or appearance, then close with a question that invites them to recognise it. Buyers recognise symptoms faster than root causes. [L0701.042]
    • "Running shoes soaked before the first mile? Stop buying new pairs every month."
    • "Jackets stained after every commute? Your clothes deserve a fix that lasts." [L0701.042]
  • Problem-solution opening (Source D): Lead with a direct question about the customer's pain ("Sick of the [specific problem]?"), then position the brand as the answer with an adjective that appeals to customer psychology, such as "wholistic", "calming" or "natural". It works because it names the problem state, triggers recognition and offers an escape path at once. [L0313.002]
    • "Tired of restless nights? This is your natural sleep solution."
    • "Frustrated with back tension? Meet your pain-free workday." [L0313.002]
  • Contrast question (Source D): Open with a question or contrast that names the viewer's exact problem, then point to a different path than the one they are already on. The hook is judged by how fast it makes the viewer feel seen. [L0374.030]
    • "Are you still getting breakouts on your back after every workout? Here is the two-minute routine that finally stopped mine."
    • "If your running shoes still hurt after 5 km, don't buy another pair yet. Check this first." [L0374.030]
  • Frustration and "that is me" statement: Name a frustration the target viewer already has, connect it to the product as the fix, and phrase it as a short statement that makes the viewer think "that is me". [L0764.009]
    • "Still scrubbing grease off the stovetop every night? Watch what happens in two seconds."
    • "Tired of your car smelling like wet gym bags after every rainy trip?" [L0764.009]
  • Specific everyday problem, number and action: Open with one specific everyday problem the buyer already notices, state a fix with a number, then close with one action. Naming the exact situation makes the product feel relevant instead of generic. [L0794.056]
    • "Chargers dying by week three? Our braided cable is rated for 10,000 bends, so order a pack of two today."
    • "Boots soaking through on every wet walk? Our membrane liner keeps feet dry for 8 hours, so get the pair before the weekend." [L0794.056]
  • Relatable-problem hook: Open with a question or statement about a problem the viewer experiences. Within seconds, show the product solving that problem on screen. Viewers who recognise themselves stay to watch. [L0795.026]
    • "Tired of wiping muddy paw prints off the floor after every walk?"
    • "Hate scrubbing burnt cheese off your pan every night?" [L0795.026]
  • Problem-specific creative in the customer's words: Name the exact problem the audience has in their own words, as found in reviews, then offer the product as the fix. [L0999.045]
    • "Dog hair on your bed every morning? This brush lifts it out of wiry fur in one pass."
    • "Still stuck with cat hair on the sofa? This brush pulls it out before it spreads." [L0999.045]
  • Owner-group formula: Name a specific owner group, describe the everyday annoyance they recognise, point to the visible symptom, and add a direct call to act. The viewer sees their own situation in the first line. [L0993.023]
    • "Cat owners with a sofa full of fur, this roller lifts it off in one pass."
    • "Border Collie owners whose car seats are covered in hair, this attachment cleans them in minutes." [L0993.023]
  • Pain-point hook: Open with a relatable daily struggle, name the feeling it causes, then reveal the product as the fix in one sentence. [L1427.008]
    • "Dreading the wait for your kettle while you still need a second cup? Here is a quick fix that works in any mug."
    • "Tired of brewing bitter coffee that tastes flat every morning? This ready-to-use coffee gives you a smooth cup in seconds." [L1427.008]
  • Reassure, explain, act (Source D): Open with a relatable pain point framed as a question, reassure the viewer that the problem is not their fault, name one mechanism of the product in plain words, and end with a simple call to action. The viewer recognises the problem first, so the product reads as a solution, not a pitch. [L1479.018]
    • "Stuck in an afternoon slump again? You're not lazy, your energy may just need support. One daily capsule helps keep focus steady."
    • "Dog still pulling on every walk? It's not you. This harness shifts pulling tension to the chest so walks feel calm." [L1479.018]
  • Precise customer words: Open the hook with the exact words your target customers use to describe the problem in forums, reviews or comments. Only someone with that problem feels addressed. The reader sees their own inner voice in the first line. [L0772.018]
    • "My heel cracks every winter even after three creams, and I am sick of it."
    • "Every shirt is soaked by lunch, and nothing I have bought has fixed it." [L0772.018]
  • Pain-point hook over free stock footage: Find a free stock clip that shows the frustration, convert it to grayscale, and overlay a caption that names the pain point, plus a cross mark over the clip. Viewers recognise their own problem within seconds. [L0398.011]
    • Grayscale clip of a dripping tap, caption: "Still mopping up the same puddle every morning?"
    • Grayscale clip of a cluttered desk, caption: "Losing 20 minutes a day hunting for chargers?" [L0398.011]
  • Warning hook: Warn the viewer off a product, or ask them to check one detail before they act. It makes the viewer feel protected from a mistake, which holds attention longer than a plain product claim. [L1164.062]
    • "Hold off on buying [product] until you check the ingredient list on the back."
    • "Stop reordering [product] before you read the renewal terms in the checkout." [L1164.062]
  • Desire-result hook: Name a result the buyer wants, then name the product or ingredient as the way to get it, in the form "if you want [result] you need [product]". The viewer sees the benefit in the first seconds. [L0901.017]
    • "If you want deeper sleep without feeling groggy, you need magnesium glycinate at night."
    • "If your knees feel stiff every morning, you need this collagen drink." [L0901.017]
  • Problem and relief in three seconds: Start with the viewer's specific problem and show the relief within the first three seconds. Viewers with the same problem recognise it and keep watching, and others leave, so the ad reaches a qualified audience. [L0871.070]
    • "Tired of waking up with a stiff neck? Watch what happens after 60 seconds on this."
    • "Working from a laptop all day and feeling that back ache again? Here is the stretch that gives relief." [L0871.070]
  • Relief sound or reaction: A relief sound or reaction while using the product works as a recurring hook pattern. Keep the same voice and actions across ads and change only the camera angle. [L0871.063]
  • Testimonials in the problem hook (Pain/Agitate/Solve): In the problem description hook, use customer testimonies or direct quotes. This gives social proof at the opening, before the product enters as the solution. [L0258.026]

Claim, benefit and transformation hooks

  • Challenge the standard, offer a better alternative: State what people currently believe or do, then immediately introduce a different or better way. It creates doubt about the current approach and positions the product as the smarter choice. [L0160.003]
    • Formula: "Everyone says [common belief], but [your approach is better]" or "Don't [current solution], [do this instead]."
    • "Everyone says drink more water for energy, but here's what actually works."
    • "Don't buy those expensive noise-canceling headphones-try this instead for a quarter of the price." [L0160.003]
  • Outcome or hidden insight: Promise a specific result or reveal a non-obvious reason, but do not explain it in the hook. Formula: "Here's [the secret / the real reason / what I discovered] about [topic]" or "How I [achieve specific result]," then pause. Curiosity and the wish to know HOW keep the viewer watching. [L0160.005]
    • "Here's the real reason your productivity hack isn't working."
    • "How I sleep 2 hours less and wake up more rested (and it's not what you think)." [L0160.005]
  • Transformation hook: State that the product changed the speaker's space or health, then name the result. The viewer sees a before and after before the product is explained. A visible result creates curiosity and gives proof before any claim is made. [L0392.005]
    • "This is how my car went from covered in dust to spotless in one week."
    • "This is how a single bottle changed my morning routine and gave me back my energy." [L0392.005]
  • Bold claim: Open with one specific, strong statement about the product result, so the right viewer relates at once. A direct claim filters the audience in the first second. [L0398.012]
    • "This pan fries eggs without a drop of oil."
    • "This charger refills a phone in the time it takes to make coffee." [L0398.012]
  • Bold claim with a payoff later: Open with a bold claim or surprising statement that makes viewers expect a payoff, such as a shocking reveal or a question they need answered. The payoff must come later in the video, which keeps viewers to the end. [L1384.015]
    • Example A: a tiny change in the kitchen that saved a family a surprising amount each month.
    • Example B: nobody expected this result until the final second of the test. [L1384.015]
  • Surprising cost or loss: Name a surprising cost or loss tied to an everyday situation, then present the product as the fix. It grabs attention with a concrete problem the viewer recognises. [L0638.033]
    • "Commuters lose hours each month to tangled cables. This organiser keeps every charger in one place."
    • "Gym goers waste money on bottles that leak. This sealed bottle stays dry in any bag." [L0638.033]
  • Benefit-led hook: The hook states the target audience and problem, then creates curiosity, and the product benefit follows.
    • Ergonomic mouse: "Office workers, never ignore these three signs of wrist strain. Wrist pain often starts small, and most people only notice it when it's too late. Our curved mouse keeps your hand relaxed through an entire workday."
    • Sleep mask: "Light sleepers, never ignore these three signs of a bad night's rest. Most sleep problems start with a stray light you barely notice. Our blackout mask blocks 100% of light so you wake up rested." [L1425.025]
  • Budget-conscious solution: Acknowledge the cost barrier directly, then position the product as solving both the need and the price concern. [L0160.008]
    • "Want luxury skincare without the luxury price tag? Here's how."
    • "Designer jeans energy on a thrift store budget." [L0160.008]
  • Daily use or essential status: Highlight frequency or category status to show the product is proven and essential, not a trend. Daily use suggests reliability and builds confidence in real value. [L0160.007]
    • Formula: "[Number] products I [use daily / cannot live without / use every morning]" or "My must-have [product category] I use without fail."
    • "Three tools I use before 9 AM that actually change my day."
    • "The one item in my skincare routine I've used for five years straight." [L0160.007]
  • Authority through extensive testing: Share your personal journey or quantify the scope of your testing (every, all, tested X number, daily, without fail). Testing builds trust, and viewers believe you have done due diligence. [L0160.004]
    • Formula: "I tried [number or scope], and here's what I kept" or "One [product] I use [frequency] is."
    • "I tested 23 coffee makers this year and kept only one."
    • "The one desk lamp I use every single workday and will never replace." [L0160.004]
  • Trusted source and numbered list: A hook that credits a trusted source, such as a viral trend or a group of people, with a purchase decision adds social proof in the first seconds. A hook that promises a numbered list of reasons sets a clear, quick-to-scan promise. [L1335.053]
    • "Busy parents keep switching to this, here is why."
    • "Five reasons your kitchen needs this before the weekend." [L1335.053]

Curiosity, suspense and pattern breaks

  • Suspense: Start with a curiosity gap and delay the reveal of the product until after the first clip. Curiosity keeps people watching long enough to reach the product intro. [L0398.013]
    • "I almost threw this out before I tried it on my kitchen counter."
    • "The one thing my gym instructor never told me until I bought this." [L0398.013]
  • Pattern break: Ads must interrupt what people are doing, not blend in. Short headlines of two to three words, paired with an unusual image, stop a reader mid-scroll long enough to click. The goal is a pattern break that moves attention to the offer. [L0523.032]
  • Familiar word, unexpected format: Open by speaking directly to the target buyer and pair a familiar, high-intent product word with a format the buyer does not expect. The curiosity from the mismatch stops the scroll, and the buyer identifies with the speaker at once. [L0707.008]
    • "Sunscreen in a stick? Here is why it leaves no white cast and why my daily routine changed."
    • "Espresso-style foam in a 30-second jar? I cannot believe this replaced my morning cafe run." [L0707.008]
  • Unusual question or event: Open with a specific, unusual question or event the reader cares about, then bridge to the offer as the way to understand or solve it. Curiosity holds attention long enough for the reader to reach the offer. [L1369.053]
    • "Why do some bakeries sell out by nine in the morning while the one across the street throws away its bread every night?"
    • "What is the one check that tells a warehouse manager a shipment will be late before the truck leaves the dock?" [L1369.053]
  • Offbeat news hook: Find an unusual article or statistic and use it to open the ad, then link it to the product through one clear step. Keep a file of such clippings so they are ready when a product needs a surprising opening. [L1366.062, L1366.076]
    • "A survey shows that many people reuse their water bottles for days without washing them, so here is a bottle that cleans itself."
    • "A new report finds that most office chairs are sat on for more than eight hours, which is why this chair was designed around the spine."
    • "A survey finds many people reuse water bottles for days without washing them, so this bottle cleans itself."
    • "Most office chairs are sat on for over eight hours a day, which is why this one is built around the spine." [L1366.062, L1366.076]
  • Educational hook: Open with a useful fact or warning that names a problem the audience cares about, then reveal the product as the solution. Viewers accept the lesson first and the sale second. [L0524.044]
    • "Three everyday habits that quietly dry out your skin and what to use instead."
    • "Two cleaning products that leave a film on your floors, and the simple swap that fixes it." [L0524.044]
  • Educational reveal: Ads that reveal a problem the viewer did not know about tend to perform well, for example "did you know your dog could suffer from...". They pull the viewer into the problem. [L1425.010]
  • Question about owning the product: Open with a question that asks the viewer to picture owning or using the product, then cut to text-to-speech listing the reasons to buy in a few sentences, and end with a short closing line. [L1383.022]
    • "Do you see yourself keeping this on your desk every morning?" followed by benefits and a closing line about the daily routine.
    • "Do you see yourself giving this as a gift this winter?" followed by reasons it suits gifting and a closing line urging a purchase. [L1383.022]
  • Case: "Do you see yourself needing this?": A jewelry store used the hook "Do you see yourself needing this?" and reportedly reached about 2.5 million views with it. The speaker cited this as evidence that the hook can perform well across different products. [L1383.023]
  • Dialogue hook: Start with an emoji of a person and a text-to-speech question such as "Why did you give me this free product?". Reply in character that the product is a gift, change the wording slightly each time, and have the other person say they would like to see more videos and ask whether they won. Finish by confirming they won. [L1383.024]
    • "Why did you give me this free bike?" answered by a gift reply and a follow-up asking whether they won.
    • "Why did you send me this free candle set?" answered by a thank-you and a question asking whether they can join the giveaway. [L1383.024]
  • Case: dialogue hook performance: The dialogue hook "Why did you give me this free product?" was uploaded about three to six times before one version got traction. That version reached about 370,000 views and brought about 5,000 followers. Earlier uploads of the same format got barely any views. [L1383.025]
  • Text-story hook: Use on-screen text that takes longer to read than the video length, for example 15 seconds of reading in a 7-second video. This forces viewers to replay, which raises average watch time, but the text must be engaging enough to keep them reading. [L1384.020]
    • Example A: a short story where the outcome is revealed only in the final frame.
    • Example B: a running dialogue where each line shows a small surprise. [L1384.020]
  • Story opening: Begin with a short exchange in which someone with a real problem asks a respected person for advice. Let the answer introduce a principle that leads to the product. A concrete person and a question make the reader listen, and the answer frames the product as the practical result of wise advice. [L1369.118]
    • "A plumber with a full schedule asked his most experienced mentor how to stop losing jobs to cheaper rivals, and the answer was to sell the result the customer will see first."
    • "A new mother asked her pediatrician what matters most in a stroller, and the reply began with the one feature that parents regret not checking." [L1369.118]

Audience, POV and social-proof hooks

  • Niche-targeting hook: Address only a specific group in the first line, so non-members scroll past and members engage. Engagement from that group signals TikTok to show the video to similar users, which can give better-converting reach than a large but broad view count. [L0795.027]
    • "Every runner needs this free route tracker."
    • "Calling all home bakers: this tool fixes sticky dough in seconds." [L0795.027]
  • Niche hook with a cold or problem: Name a specific audience, name the cold or problem they face in their activity, then give the product as the direct fix. The hook works because it addresses one identifiable person at the moment of the problem. [L1316.030]
    • "For every football fan at winter games: cold feet ruin the match, so wear these insulated socks all game long."
    • "For the dog walkers stuck in the rain: the waterproof jacket that keeps you dry on every walk." [L1316.030]
  • Hobby hook, boredom and free time: Name the boredom at home, then show how the product changed the way the viewer spends free time. [L1426.021]
    • "Tired of scrolling every evening? Here is the one thing that filled my weekends."
    • "Your free hours deserve more than the couch. Try this build." [L1426.021]
  • POV hook: Place the viewer in a first-person situation in the first second. This makes the scene feel personal and encourages them to keep watching. POV works well when the scene leads straight into a comparison or product demonstration. [L0795.038]
    • "POV: you finally find a charger that survives a full week of travel."
    • "POV: your kitchen floor stays clean after the first week with this mop." [L0795.038]
  • Shopping-finds hook: Frame the product as something people discover on TikTok, Amazon or shopping feeds. This leverages curiosity about what others buy, and viewers trust products that appear socially trending. [L0398.014]
    • "The kitchen tool everyone on my feed keeps buying."
    • "Three reasons I stopped paying for the expensive version." [L0398.014]
  • Fear of missing out and insider knowledge: Suggest the knowledge or product is unknown or underused, and position yourself as the one sharing the secret. FOMO triggers curiosity and urgency, and viewers do not want to be left behind. [L0160.006]
    • Formula: "Why is nobody talking about [product]," "Calling all [audience] tired of [problem]," or "If you're not [using X / doing X], you're missing out."
    • "Calling all runners tired of blisters-why isn't anyone using this?"
    • "If you're not using this app for your business, you're doing it the hard way." [L0160.006]
  • Follow-for-giveaway hook: It grows followers quickly ("follow my account and I'll give you a giveaway"), but has become less effective for conversion because many sellers use the same style. Use it sparingly and do not rely on it as the main sales driver. [L0657.014]

Copy openings (written ads and landing copy)

  • Open with "Even if...": Start copy with "Even if..." to address the main objection the reader might have. This jugular objection, the single most common one, must be crushed first or you will lose readers. Illusory superiority means most people think they are better than average, so they assume "it might work for them but not for me". [L0196.011]
  • Mistake: skipping the jugular objection: Not addressing the common main objection at the start of copy is a mistake. Start with "Even if..." to pre-empt what readers are thinking, especially given illusory superiority ("it might work for them, but not for me"). [L0196.063]
  • Open with customer language: Effective ad copy opens with customer language. State their pain point directly, reference their insecurity or deepest desire, or make a bold benefit claim that resonates immediately. [L0204.004]
  • Short first sentence of body copy: The first sentence should be very short, nearly incomplete, and easy to read, with no long or multisyllable words. Its only job is to make the reader read the second sentence. [L1366.003]
  • Headline-to-first-sentence pull (print): Every element of a print ad (headline, subheadline, photo, caption, logo, layout) is judged by one question: does it get the reader to read the first sentence? The first body sentence should be very short, almost incomplete, with no long words. [L1366.072]
    • "Your inbox is a mess. Here is how to clear it in ten minutes."
    • "Your back hurts by noon. Four changes can stop that before lunch." [L1366.072]
  • Real-life scenario: Open with the buyer inside a stressful everyday moment, describe the feelings and pressure, and only then reveal how the product resolves it. Keep technical specs for later so the scene does the emotional work first. [L1366.061, L1366.075]
    • "You are stuck in a hotel lobby, the bill is waiting, and the card reader fails; this portable terminal fixes the moment."
    • "You are on a crowded train with a phone that is about to die; this solar pack keeps you connected until you get home."
    • "The card reader fails and the line is growing behind you. This portable terminal fixes that moment."
    • "The phone is at 3 percent on a crowded train. This solar pack keeps you connected until you get home." [L1366.061, L1366.075]
  • Familiar scene from daily life: Open with a scene the reader recognises, then move to the problem the product solves within the same scene. A familiar setting makes the reader picture himself, so the offer feels relevant. [L1369.054]
    • "After you finish the morning school run and drop the car keys on the counter, you still have three invoices waiting to be paid."
    • "On the first cold morning of the year, the water in the shower takes nine minutes to warm, and your kids are already late for the bus." [L1369.054]
  • News angle tied to the reader: A news angle or an unusual, specific detail draws attention and can be bridged to the offer. Attention must tie to the reader's own interest in the business or life, not stand alone as a shock. [L1369.067]
  • Newsworthy first, then their affairs: Businesspeople compete with many distractions. Give the reader something newsworthy first, then connect it to their own affairs. Once attention is diverted, bring it back by showing how the offer serves those affairs. [L1369.068]
  • Simple first line that is relevant: The first sentence or headline must instantly say something important, valuable and useful to the reader, because an unwanted letter is treated as a pest until it proves otherwise. Gimmicks such as fake hazard warnings tend to fail. A genuinely relevant headline usually succeeds. [L1370.019]
  • Sensational shout (mistake): A sensational shout, such as an alarming word with no link to the offer, draws the wrong response. Tie every attention-grabbing element to a reason the reader cares about the offer. [L1369.041]
  • Find the point of contact: Use this procedure to build an opening.
    1. List the reader's dominant thoughts, such as income, family, comfort, status, promotion, business survival or a current worry.
    2. Pick the thought most closely tied to what you sell.
    3. Open with a sentence about that thought and bring in the offer as its answer. [L1369.042]

On-screen text, sound and visual hooks

  • On-screen text carries much of the effect: The on-screen text and the hook have a large share of a video's effect. The hosts estimated the text alone at about 30% of the video. A weak opening can turn a good demonstration into a low-converting video. [L0657.010]
  • Text plus unexpected background: Combine an on-screen text hook with something happening in the background. The background element should be unexpected or slightly odd, so it catches viewers off guard and makes them curious. [L0764.007]
  • Text-to-speech or short text: Text-to-speech in the opening can keep viewers engaged. Alternatively, show a few sentences of on-screen text with a quick transition so viewers must re-watch the video to finish reading. [L0820.043]
  • Study viral on-screen text: Viral product videos often succeed because of their on-screen text. Study the text and structure of viral product videos and reuse the proven text pattern while filming your own product. [L1381.038]
  • On-screen hook example: On a short product clip, the first second shows the hook text "Do you see yourself drinking this?" on screen, followed by a recreated pouring sound and a text line describing the product's benefit. [L1383.044]
  • Short, fast hooks and music: A short, fast hook is key to TikTok creatives. Spend effort on the first seconds, the music and hook variations to find winning versions. [L1376.021]
  • Thumbnails matter less for video ads: Feed placements often skip past thumbnails. Test the first seconds of the video (the hook) more than the thumbnail. [L0648.024]
  • Hook formats for variations: Hook formats include a clip change, a music change, a "three reasons why" opener, a "wish I knew earlier" opener or a before-and-after opener. [L0902.017]
  • Hook scene template: Use this template as written for the first seconds.
Hook, first seconds. Option one, spoken: tell viewers this is for them if [PET] hair has taken over their home. Shot: a slow pan across a living room buried in [PET] hair. Option two, spoken: say that [PRODUCT] changed their [SPACE] and gave them back their health. Shot: show the product, cut to a before view of a grimy, hair-covered [SPACE], then cut to the same [SPACE] spotless afterward.

  • Hook as a pattern break in the visual: Ads must interrupt, not blend in. The unusual image is part of the pattern break that stops the scroll. [L0523.032]

Building hook variations

  • Variations per concept: Write one script for the concept, then make three versions in which only the first three to five seconds change while the rest of the video stays the same. [L0902.017]
  • Keep the hook separate for testing: The hook, meaning the first three to five seconds, decides whether viewers stay, so it must be tested separately from the rest of the video. [L0902.018]
  • One video, three tests: Keep the video and change only the opening hook, which is one of the most important parts for grabbing attention. One video can become three test videos this way. [L1107.006]
  • Start with about six hooks: Build a list of about six hooks and make several videos for each one. Once a hook starts performing, duplicate that video and post more variations of it. [L1383.027]
  • Hook library: A library of about 50 hook ideas can produce around 50 variations of the same product video and supply roughly three to six months of creative. Keep the library current, because hook trends change constantly. [L0398.015]
  • Test list of 50 hooks: A video hook test list of 50 hook ideas is recommended for TikTok-style first seconds. Use it to pick which hook goes in the first position. [L0637.056]
  • Keep notes on hooks that work: Hooks can start with a question about the audience's issue or with a direct solution statement, and should be tested. Keep notes on hooks that work so they can be reused later, including in TikTok ads once organic results fund them. [L0795.029]

Testing hooks

  • Test systematically: Take a winning creative and test many hook variations until one clearly outperforms. Hook ideas should come from audience research on language, problems and context. [L0640.019]
  • Three variations of one video: Create three variations of one video, replacing only the first 3 to 5 seconds of the clip with different openings. Run all variations inside the winning audiences. [L0648.057]
  • Lift a weak attention rate: To raise a weak attention rate, take the video that works and place several new hooks in front of it. Make sure every hook fits the marketing angle and matches the landing page copy. Keep testing new hooks continually, even when attention is already good. [L0644.007]
  • Hook testing with the 13-pattern classification: Use the 13-pattern set from the classification procedure to label each tested hook, so results show which pattern type performs. [L0613.012]
  • Hooks decide the click too: A strong opening drives views beyond the hook into the click, so the hook test should track clicks as well as watch time. [L0950.040]

Mistakes to avoid

  • Spending too long before the main benefit. If the ad takes 10 seconds or more to introduce the product, demonstrate the benefit within 3 to 5 seconds, then introduce the product. [L0067.021]
  • Leading with product features in the opening. Show the problem first, then the solution. [L0280.017]
  • Not addressing the jugular objection at the start of copy. [L0196.063]
  • Attention-grabbing content with no relation to the product, which attracts viewers who will not buy. [L0640.020]
  • Delaying the product in a non-unboxing video. Unless it is a strong unboxing, show the product within the first five seconds. [L1377.021]
  • Sensational shouts with no link to the offer. [L1369.041]
  • Gimmicks such as fake hazard warnings, which tend to fail. [L1370.019]
  • Relying on the follow-for-giveaway hook as the main sales driver. [L0657.014]
  • A product introduction that takes around 10 seconds, which loses viewers before the benefit appears. [L0640.048]

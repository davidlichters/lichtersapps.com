3.9 Follow-Up, rejections and re-Contact

Tracking and records

  • Record three things for every supplier: the date of first contact, the contact person's name, and detailed notes from each conversation with its outcome. This prevents accidental duplicate outreach, coordinates several supplier processes at once, and keeps follow-ups on time. [L0126.007]
  • Keep a Master Supplier list that tracks all outreach and contact attempts. For each supplier, record the contact person's name, the conversation date, the details discussed, and the outcome. [L0133.008]
  • Record the contact person and the date of first contact in your notes. A later follow-up can then address that person by name and refer to the earlier conversation. [L0938.015, L0936.015]
  • Detailed notes matter because with many brands it is easy to forget what happened on an earlier call. Notes show exactly where you stand with each supplier. [L0936.016]
  • Failing to log notes across a large list leads to repeated or inconsistent calls and emails. Record the outcome of every contact immediately. [L0936.017]
  • Log every supplier response in the notes, including reasons for refusal and conditions such as "contact again in three months" or visitor and revenue thresholds. Schedule follow-ups from these notes instead of relying on memory. [L0938.007]
  • Record every supplier response, including rejections, in a supplier tracking sheet and schedule a later follow-up date. [L0471.038]
  • Log exact requirements, such as a monthly visitor minimum or a monthly sales level, together with a re-contact date such as 3-6 months. These notes decide when and how to follow up. [L0191.011]
  • Typical conditions to record, and the trigger each one sets:
    • "contact again in 3-6 months" [L0191.012]
    • "requires 10,000 monthly visitors" [L0191.012]
    • "only works with stores doing $20,000+ per month" [L0191.012]
  • Keep a supplier tracking spreadsheet with the exact date you must contact each supplier again. A supplier that is out of stock or asks you to come back later gets a dated entry, such as the end of Q3. [L0968.003]
  • Use a supplier spreadsheet to track every supplier you contact, with a notes column for minimum requirements and the reason for each rejection. The spreadsheet from the Drop Ship Blueprint can serve as the starting template. [L0927.011]
  • Record every supplier conversation with the contact person, the date, and the planned follow-up date. Circle back later, because a rejection in a new niche often means the supplier wants proof that you are a real store. [L1113.014]
  • Track each conversation in a sheet with the date and what was said, and follow up within a set period. [L0803.027]
  • Note the contact date, the person and the outcome in the supplier sourcing worksheet. [L0665.022]
  • Record the requirements for each supplier next to its name in your supplier spreadsheet. [L0927.010]
  • Record a refusal and its date in your supplier spreadsheet. [L0358.007]
  • Record the stated reason for a rejection and the follow-up plan that goes with it. [L0835.014, L0468.034]
  • Do not list an out-of-stock supplier's products yet, and record its status so you can return to it later. [L1090.024]

First contact and the first follow-up

Sequence for each supplier on the list

  1. Start with the first supplier on your list and work down one supplier at a time. [L0936.019]
  2. Day 1: if the supplier has a phone number, call first. Ask for their application process and for the decision maker who handles new accounts. [L0936.019]
  3. Day 1: if the supplier asks you to email during the call, send the day-one email from the contact plan. [L0936.019]
  4. Day 2: if the day-one email gets no reply, send the follow-up email. It restates your earlier request, gives a near launch window, and asks for the application steps. [L0936.019]
  5. Day 3: make another phone call. [L0936.019]
  6. Days 4 to 7: continue the mixed calls and emails in the plan until you get a clear yes or no. [L0936.019]
  7. Stop the sequence early once you reach the right person and get a clear answer. [L0936.019]

Email or form requested by the supplier

  1. Send a short, simple email to the dealer or retailer contact address on the supplier's website. Introduce yourself and your store, and include a condensed version of your pitch on why they should work with you. [L0470.019]
  2. If there is no reply by the next day, call in the morning. [L0470.019]
  3. On the call, introduce yourself and the store, mention the email sent the day before, and say you want to confirm it arrived. [L0470.019]

Before ending the call, ask when you should expect a reply. Submit the email or form right after the call, and if nothing arrives within a couple of days, call the same contact again. [L0970.032]

  • Ask: "Okay, perfect, I'll do that. Right after this call I'll send it. When should I expect a reply? Is that something you'll handle today, later this week, or at another time?" [L0970.033]

Phone first, then email

  1. Call the supplier first, since a phone call reaches the right person and creates a first contact. [L0429.006]
  2. Follow up by email afterward, because the supplier then expects the message. [L0429.006]
  3. If there is still no reply, call weekly and record each follow-up in a supplier tracking spreadsheet. [L0429.006]
  • Call the supplier the next day, in a friendly way, to confirm the application was received. Follow up sooner only if the supplier asked you to expect a longer wait. [L0835.012]

  • Suppliers with online dealer applications expect the form to be filled out. If there is no reply after a couple of days, call to follow up. [L1112.049] (older advice, 2021)
  • If a supplier does not reply within about two days after a promised follow-up, call or email again and stay in regular contact. [L0358.049] (older advice, 2020)
  • Follow up with a supplier about three days after a call or email if you have not heard back. Keep the follow-up short, for example by confirming they saw your message. [L0941.018]
  • If there is no response to an email or contact form, wait one day, then follow up. Suppliers often have no phone number listed. [L1277.039] (older advice, 2020)
  • Email visibility: two to three days after the initial email, reply in the same thread. This creates two emails from you in the recipient's inbox on different days. [L0276.050]
  • Do not sort or prioritize suppliers when reaching out. Contact all of them, keep notes on those that decline, and circle back later. [L0934.037]
  • Start supplier outreach early, even before the store is ready. A supplier that is low on stock can still add you to a list for later. [L0803.027] (older advice, 2022)
  • Starting outreach early in the autumn avoids the busy and slow periods described in the holiday section. [L0360.012]

Warm leads first

When suppliers say "email me" or "fill out our form", prioritize them, because a dialogue has started. [L0251.025]

Procedure for a warm-lead batch: [L0251.025] (2023)

  1. Reach out to 20 or more suppliers as your minimum starting pool. [L0251.025]
  2. Prioritize suppliers that said "email me" or "fill out our form". [L0251.025]
  3. Follow up on these warm leads daily or every other day before contacting the remaining suppliers. [L0251.025]
  4. Once a supplier sends approval documents, drop other priorities and complete the paperwork immediately. [L0251.025]
  5. Upload their products and integrate them into your store. [L0251.025]
  6. Return to contacting the remaining suppliers on your list. [L0251.025]

Old onboarding forms

Do not simply answer an old onboarding form or email that has sat unanswered for a long time. Contact that supplier again as if the relationship were new, using the channel you originally used (phone, contact form or email), because contacts and forms may have changed. [L0360.007] (2024)

Gold-tier suppliers

  • Gold suppliers can take 3 months, 6 months, or more than a year to approve a store. Plan follow-ups on that timeline, while many other suppliers can be added immediately. [L0191.005]
  • You can contact gold suppliers early, but expect "not yet" until you reach a certain traffic or sales level. Use the time to build the store and track results so you can reapply later. [L0486.015]

Procedure: supplier who said approval takes 4-8 weeks

  1. Document the contact person's name, the contact date, and the timeline in your supplier tracking spreadsheet. [L0294.003]
  2. Set a calendar reminder for exactly 4 weeks after initial contact. [L0294.003]
  3. Follow up with the supplier on the reminder date. [L0294.003]
  4. For email-only suppliers that have not responded after nearly 2 weeks of follow-ups every 2 days, stop following up on Fridays before long weekends, when staff take extended time off. [L0294.003]
  5. Resume follow-ups on Wednesday or Thursday of the following week, once decision makers are back in the office. [L0294.003]

Unresponsive suppliers

Basic checks first

  • If no supplier responds, check the basics: that your email address receives mail, that your contact details on voicemails are correct, and that the store is not password-protected when you point suppliers to it. [L1002.056] (older, 2019)
  • If suppliers do not respond despite outreach, follow up persistently and check whether the outreach strategy itself is failing before changing anything else. [L0969.015] (older, 2022)
  • When many suppliers reject your outreach, the root cause is usually the approach: the wording of your message, the way you contact them, or the time of day you reach out. It is rarely the number of suppliers or the niche itself. [L1254.018]
  • Switching niches or contacting many more suppliers after repeated rejections wastes time if you have not first diagnosed why suppliers say no. Review your message, contact method and timing first. [L1254.019]
  • If hundreds of supplier applications produce only one approval, the application approach or the way suppliers are contacted is likely broken. Approval rates that low are not normal and must be investigated. [L0429.019] (older, 2022)

Silence is not rejection

  • Suppliers are typically busy, and a lack of immediate response does not indicate rejection. Consistent follow-up through the entire 7-day schedule significantly increases approval rates. [L0126.006]
  • Do not interpret silence as rejection. Assume the supplier is busy with other matters and keep following up every day or every other day by email or phone. [L0251.012] (2023)
  • Treat a silent supplier as a busy schedule. Follow up every few days rather than assuming rejection. [L0468.034] (older, 2020)
  • Suppliers that go quiet while approval is pending are often busy, not hostile. Follow up on the same channel first. [L0243.028] (older, 2019)
  • When suppliers stop responding after initial interest, it may mean they were not confident in the store's viability or have internal issues. Following up after getting the first two or three approvals from other suppliers often works better, because the store appears more established. [L0255.015] (2025)

Procedure: unresponsive supplier, phone and email

  1. Send a follow-up email to the unresponsive supplier. [L0255.008]
  2. Find a phone number on the supplier's website or through a Google search. [L0255.008]
  3. Call the phone number. [L0255.008]
  4. If the individual contact is unresponsive, email the main support address (support@, sales@, or info@ at the supplier's domain). [L0255.008]
  5. Continue with other suppliers while you wait. You may retry after getting the first two or three approvals. [L0255.008]

Procedure: follow-up after silence (2019 routine)

  1. Call the supplier rather than relying only on email. [L1002.053] (older)
  2. If the call goes to voicemail, leave a message asking for a callback. [L1002.053] (older)
  3. If nobody calls back within a week, call again. [L1002.053] (older)
  4. Keep following up with calls and voicemails until you get a clear yes or no. [L1002.053] (older)

Procedure: call at different times of day

If suppliers consistently do not answer during your usual calling time, try other times of day: early morning (8:30 AM), mid-morning (10 AM), afternoon (2 PM), and late afternoon (4 PM). Small supplier companies often have aligned schedules, scheduled meetings, or lunch breaks that follow their time zone or company culture. [L0251.059] (2023)

  • Call suppliers on business days. If a supplier does not answer, note it and call again on the next business day. [L0807.013]

Procedure: unresponsive large supplier

  1. Before cutting ties, reply to an email from the contact person. Say that messages seem unanswered and ask whether someone else at the company can be contacted. [L0805.026] (2024)
  2. Call the company again, ask to be connected to another person, and voice your concerns. [L0805.026]
  3. If the new contact is just as unresponsive, stop selling that supplier's products. [L0805.026]
  • A supplier whose sales contact does not answer emails or calls for weeks is a serious warning sign for later order processing. Move on, and revisit only after a long time, when the contact may have changed. [L0854.014] (older, 2022)
  • If a supplier does not answer a sourcing request within a few days, escalate through the account manager on WhatsApp or the public community channel. Public escalation often gets a faster reply than a private message. [L0524.033] (Source C)
  • If an order is delayed or a supplier does not respond, ask the supplier directly through its contact channels and keep your own record of the communication. Do not assume a supplier is reachable just because it is listed. [L0562.037] (Source C)
  • Approval timing from suppliers varies widely, from about an hour to many days. If a supplier does not respond, follow up consistently, since slow responders are often less reliable suppliers. [L0960.025] (older, 2019)

Out-of-stock suppliers

  • If a supplier is out of stock, it will usually tell you during outreach. Do not list its products yet. Work with suppliers that have stock, and circle back to the out-of-stock supplier after a few months. [L1090.024] (older, 2021)
  • A supplier that is out of stock or pressed by many inquiries: record the contact, ask when to follow up, and record that time. [L0665.022] (older, 2022)

Offline periods

  • If you will be offline for a stretch, do not start an outreach round you cannot follow up on. Use the offline time to build a complete supplier contact list, so you can start calling the moment you are back online. [L0941.107]

Objections and declines

"We don't work with new online stores"

  • Suppliers often use "we don't work with new online stores" as a standard rejection to test whether you are serious and trustworthy. Push back by asking which thresholds, such as traffic, Alexa score, or revenue, would qualify you for approval. [L0245.007] (older, 2020)
  • Procedure when a supplier says it does not work with new online stores:
    1. Respond: "I totally understand. What would it take for us to get approved?" [L0245.008] (older)
    2. The supplier may specify traffic per month, an Alexa score above or below a number, or a minimum revenue. [L0245.008] (older)
    3. Work toward those metrics rather than accepting "no" as final. [L0245.008] (older)
  • Alexa Score (alexa.com) ranks website popularity. Some suppliers require a minimum Alexa score to approve new retailers, so research and improve yours before approaching premium suppliers. [L0245.009] (older, 2020)
  • If a supplier says it does not currently work with online retailers, do not drop the outreach. Use a pitch built around how dropshipping increases the supplier's sales, online reach and revenue. Present the arrangement as a benefit for both sides. [L0935.011]
  • Supplier unfamiliarity with dropshipping was a bigger obstacle in the past than it is now, because e-commerce has matured. Only spend extra time explaining the model when a supplier actually does not know it. [L0935.012]

Suppliers that say they are already working with a retailer, or are not taking new retailers

  • Still start the conversation. Explain how selling with you benefits them, and ask to follow up three to six months after going live, once you have traffic and results. [L0470.021] (2023)
  • Many suppliers approve retailers only in a second round, after they see proof of the store's performance. Plan for a group of suppliers that will ask you to prove yourself first, and keep them on your follow-up list. [L0470.022] (2023)
  • When a supplier declines, end the conversation politely with a clear follow-up plan, such as saying you will get back in touch after launch once you have traffic and sales. Ending on a hard no closes off a future approval, whereas a polite follow-up keeps it open. [L0470.023] (2023)
  • If a supplier turns you down because the store is new, you may briefly mention relevant past business experience if the conversation leaves room for it, without giving detailed information. If the answer is a firm no, end politely and do not push further. [L0470.102] (2023)
  • Suppliers that are not accepting new e-commerce accounts are not a dead end. Note the date they gave for a follow-up, and return with sales results or traffic data at that time, since results raise the chance of approval. [L0801.023] (older, 2021)
  • When a supplier says it will not take new dealers, ask when to follow up and record that time. [L0665.022] (older, 2022)

Capacity-based rejection

Example: "we are not taking on more internet retailers right now".

  1. Do not accept the rejection passively with a polite goodbye, because the supplier will not reach out to you on its own. [L0927.007]
  2. Present a launch strategy that shows what you are doing to start the store: which suppliers you already work with or are onboarding, which marketing channels you will use, and which promotions you will run. [L0927.007]
  3. Use fear of missing out (FOMO) by showing that you are about to build a meaningful position in the market, so the supplier does not want to be left out of your growth. [L0927.007]
  4. Use this approach only if your launch strategy is real and well researched. A supplier that senses a bluff will not be persuaded. [L0927.007]

Standards-based rejection

A supplier may reject you by saying your store does not meet its standards. Treat this as a signal to improve the store and your pitch before calling again, not as a final answer. [L0927.015]

MAP policy decline

Hi [Supplier name], thanks for reaching out. Right now we only work with suppliers that enforce their minimum advertised price, so we will not be moving forward with your products at this time. If your MAP policy is enforced across retailers, please let us know and we can revisit this with you.

[L0941.021]

Responding during a follow-up call

  1. If they decline, ask what you can do to make it happen, and show genuine interest in their concerns. [L0133.016]
  2. Highlight how this project is already benefiting your company, and explain the growth potential of adding their product line. [L0133.016]
  3. If they continue declining, ask for the real underlying reason, and state that you genuinely want to understand their hesitation. [L0133.016]

Minimum requirements

  • A common reason for rejection is a minimum requirement, such as an Alexa score above 100,000, or around $20,000 in expected monthly business. A new store usually cannot meet these, and a large company will rarely make exceptions, however well you pitch. [L0927.009]
  • Some suppliers only authorize stores with at least 10,000 monthly visitors. Others require at least 20,000 euros in monthly revenue. Record these requirements in the supplier notes, so you can follow up when the store reaches them. [L0938.005]
  • Reference point from 2025: about 20,000 monthly visitors and around 200,000 USD monthly revenue as proof when re-contacting. [L0966.020]
  • If a supplier requires a minimum Alexa ranking under 100,000 or a marketing plan, note the requirement, respond that the threshold will be met, and reapply once the store reaches that traffic level. [L0967.027] (older, 2020)

Procedure: handling minimum requirements

  1. Ask each supplier that gives a minimum requirement exactly what it needs: analytics access, screenshots, Alexa score, or monthly sales volume. [L0927.010]

Ongoing outreach routine

Aim and pacing

  • The first goal is the first five suppliers to approve you. The real aim is approval from every supplier on your master list, including the gold-tier brands. [L0936.001]
  • Do not sort or prioritize suppliers when reaching out. Contact all of them. [L0934.037]
  • Upload a supplier's products to your store as soon as that supplier approves you, then return to outreach for the next supplier. Alternate between outreach and product uploads until every supplier on the list has been worked through. [L0936.002]
  • When a supplier approves you, mark the lesson complete and move to the product upload lesson. Upload that supplier's live products to your store, then return to the contact plan and continue working down the list. [L0936.018]
  • Approval rates differ by niche and store maturity; expect a partial approval rate with a new store. [L0966.017]
  • Repeated contact with suppliers is part of the routine. Do not stop after one attempt. [L0835.018]

Unresponsive suppliers

  • A supplier can go silent after an account setup or order, which is a form of ghosting. If a supplier does not respond, escalate to their sales or distribution contact early. [L1472.031]
  • If a supplier stops answering after being responsive, keep following up instead of giving up. Silence often comes from busy periods, such as holiday weekends or back-to-school season. (2025.) [L1287.016]

Holiday and seasonal timing

  • Silence during holiday weekends and back-to-school season is often caused by busy periods, not by a refusal, so keep following up rather than closing the lead. [L1287.016]

Objections and declines

  • Expect that not every supplier will approve a new reseller account, so contact several suppliers in parallel. In one case, one supplier approved within two minutes while the next one refused. A refusal alone is not a reason to stop. [L1437.007]
  • If one supplier declines, tell competing suppliers that demand for their products is high and that you have turned down sales for their brand. Once you have one supplier, word of mouth in the industry, especially in niche markets, often brings in other suppliers who reach out to you. [L1441.013]

Paused supply and reasons for rejection

  • When a supplier pauses new retailers because of inventory or supply chain problems, work through this procedure (older advice, 2021):
    1. Ask the supplier when its inventory is expected to stabilize and record the answer in your tracking sheet.
    2. Follow up on that date, or at the interval the supplier gives.
    3. Meanwhile, source backup suppliers from a trade show directory such as TSNN.com. [L1279.098]

  • A "we are not taking new retailers right now" answer usually means the supplier wants more authority, traffic, sales and a track record first. Return later with a live store and history, and ask again. (2023.) [L1283.006]

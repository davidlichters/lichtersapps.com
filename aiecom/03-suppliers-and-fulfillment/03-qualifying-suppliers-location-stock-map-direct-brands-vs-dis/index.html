3.3 Qualifying suppliers: location, stock, MAP, direct brands vs. distributors

Order of checks before you contact a supplier

  1. Check dropship friendliness of the niche before you build the store, because suppliers may refuse retailers that are not physical stores or do not hold their own stock. [L0460.002, L0460.025]
  2. Check how many online retailers already sell each brand, and prefer brands with fewer competing sellers, especially when the business depends on paid ads. [L0711.019]
  3. Check whether the supplier's products are already sold by other online-only stores, because that shows dropship friendliness. Not every supplier will approve, but enough approvals are likely when every criterion is followed. [L0359.022]
  4. Check whether competitors have a retail store, showroom or warehouse, because suppliers with retail presence often refuse drop shipping. [L0671.038, L0671.177]
  5. Verify the stock location (see the stock verification section below).
  6. Identify whether the seller is the brand, an importer, a manufacturer or a distributor (see the distributor section below).
  7. Check MAP policy and enforcement (see the MAP section below).
  8. Check the direct-to-consumer conflict (see the direct-to-consumer section below).
  9. Check marketplace and channel restrictions, and product shipping restrictions.

Stock location: the first filter

  • Source from domestic suppliers in the market where the store operates. For a US store, that means US suppliers. [L0247.018, L1069.006]
  • A supplier's location (warehouse or showroom) is treated the same way when deciding whether it is a valid source for a store. The decision is based on the same data for each. [L0964.025]
  • Choose suppliers that hold the product in stock in the country or region where you sell. Overseas suppliers shipping to customers are a very small exception and are not recommended. [L0962.012]
  • Most of the supplier base should be domestic. Foreign suppliers should be used only where they offer unique products and good quality. [L1086.035]
  • Domestic suppliers are preferred because shipping is faster and cheaper and customers are happier. [L0962.013]
  • When a domestic supplier with short lead times is available, prefer it over an overseas middleman with long lead times, even if the unit price is slightly higher. Long lead times create customer cancellations and backorder handling. [L0852.122]
  • Where possible, find a supplier of the same product located in the target country, such as the US, instead of shipping from CJ. In the US, the number of warehouses made little difference in the session. [L1108.039]
  • Products made overseas but stored in US warehouses are fine, since they ship quickly. [L0941.024]
  • Suppliers outside the US can still be a good fit if their products are stored in US warehouses and can ship quickly and efficiently. Warehouses close to your customers make shipping easier. [L0960.022]
  • Suppliers with warehouses in the country you sell in are a positive sign. [L0963.082]
  • Suppliers located outside the US can be used for US sales if they already have US distribution. In that case the sourcing process is the same as with a US-based supplier. [L0971.084]
  • Suppliers located in Canada or Mexico are fine to contact for a US store. Competitors selling for them often already use US distribution, a US fulfillment center or a US warehouse. [L0856.048]
  • Suppliers headquartered abroad that also have a US office may stock their inventory in the United States. Ask where a product is stocked before excluding an international supplier. [L0834.118]
  • Many large foreign brands already serve the US market through US distribution, a US fulfillment center or a US warehouse, and can be sold to US customers. [L0806.027]
  • A supplier with a foreign domain (for example.ca) that is listed on a US retail site probably also has US distribution. Record its contact details and check its contact page for a US option. [L0940.026]
  • North American brands can supply a US store. Ask them which carriers they use and which other companies already ship to the US, then reverse engineer what competitors do. [L0671.041, L0671.180]
  • Start with US-only shipping. Suppliers in Canada that sell to the US may already hold stock in US warehouses or fulfillment centers, so reach out to them if they appear on competitor sites. [L0470.085]
  • Prefer suppliers that hold inventory where you operate and where your customers are. [L0469.027]
  • Working with suppliers that hold stock in your target country is the standard. [L0671.039, L0671.178]
  • Suppliers outside the US may hold stock in US warehouses. Add them to your supplier list and ask whether they ship from a US warehouse and how stock reaches customers. [L0469.020]
  • Suppliers based in Europe: ask whether they have stock or distribution centers in the United States, because local stock speeds up delivery. [L1002.065]
  • Shipping to a different country from the sourcing market is possible, for example selling a product that performs well in the US in Australia, which is in winter when the US product has been popular. Sourcing a product from one market and selling it in another is a common way to test products. [L0678.028]
  • Ship international orders only through the supplier's own shipping arrangements. If a supplier does not ship to a given country such as Canada or the US, do not sell its products there. [L0934.041]

Delivery benchmarks

  • Customers in a country expect delivery within about a week, so local stock matches what they already expect. [L0458.059]
  • Faster fulfillment reduces perceived time delay and effort/sacrifice, which multiplies the value of the same product. [L0233.013]
  • Shipping time is a factor to review before choosing a supplier. Slow suppliers with long shipping times can hurt the customer experience and should be weighed against product, price and margin. [L0440.019]

Stock verification procedures

Find where a supplier is based when you only know its product from another store (Source A, 2024):

  1. Find the supplier's product listed on a competitor's store. [L0360.005]
  2. Google the supplier's name to find its own website and the country it operates from. [L0360.005]
  3. Contact the supplier and ask whether it holds stock or uses a fulfillment center in the target market. [L0360.005]

Check a foreign supplier's US stock before building a store (Source A, 2022, older):

  1. Find US retailers that sell the supplier's products. [L0854.020]
  2. Contact them as a normal customer and ask whether the product is in stock and where it ships from. [L0854.020]
  3. Alternatively, ask the supplier directly whether it has US distribution or a fulfillment center that ships to US customers. [L0854.020]

Ask a supplier about its stock location by phone or email (Source A, 2018, older):

  1. Contact the company by phone, email, live chat, or Facebook message. [L0466.017]
  2. Ask whether they keep the specific product in stock themselves and, if not, where it ships from. [L0466.017]
  3. Phrase the question as a shopper's question, not as a competitor's question. [L0466.017]
  4. If other sites seem to ship the same product from one location, mention it and ask whether they do the same. [L0466.017]
  • Do not tell a company you are building a competing store when you ask whether they dropship; it makes the answer less reliable and can end the relationship. [L0466.018]
  • If a supplier's location is unclear, check it directly rather than assume it holds stock. Most dropshipping websites can be identified from their site with about 80 percent certainty. [L0466.016]
  • When contacting a foreign supplier, it is professional and inoffensive to ask whether they have a U.S. warehouse or fulfillment center before requesting authorization to sell their products. [L0276.004]
  • A lead-time gap matters: contact the supplier, ask for its shipping and fulfillment location, and check whether it holds stock in your country. Overseas phone numbers do not always mean stock is overseas, so ask directly before deciding. [L0801.029]
  • For a foreign-based supplier selling into a market, confirm that it stocks inventory in that country, for example through a local distribution center. Ask directly by phone or email, since this is common among international brands. [L1069.007]
  • A store that says products from certain brands may not ship the same day is usually dropshipping those items from third-party warehouses it does not control. Only the brands it ships itself are stocked in its own warehouse, and those are the exceptions in the notice. [L0459.012]
  • Suppliers may hand out an international product catalog without stock in your market. Check whether they have stock locally before committing to them. [L1111.053]
  • A first order that takes 8 to 10 weeks while repeat orders ship within 4 to 10 days is unusual. Question the supplier about the stock location before accepting the arrangement. [L1111.035]
  • If a supplier says it is US-based but ships from overseas, drop-shipping from there will cause slow shipping and is a problem. [L0941.024]

Overseas and China sourcing

China

  • Do not dropship from China. Long delivery times, inconsistent quality and unexpected import charges make customer experience poor; work with domestic suppliers instead. [L0359.025]
  • Do not build a long-term model on drop-shipping from China. It creates more problems than it is worth. [L0469.027]
  • Do not drop ship from China. Work with suppliers that hold stock in your target country. [L0671.039, L0671.178]
  • Do not approve suppliers that only source from China or that produce products bearing company names or logos. Approve suppliers based in the US or other countries with brand-authorized inventory. [L0253.021]
  • Do not dropship from AliExpress. [L1069.006]
  • Products that are made in China or India can still be acceptable when the brand or importer holds stock locally. A seller should prefer those brands or importers. [L0852.121]
  • Products made overseas but stored in US warehouses are fine. Avoid drop shipping from overseas, such as China, when stock is shipped from there. [L0941.024, L0963.082]
  • Exception: a custom, high-demand item from a single overseas maker, where production and shipping time are acceptable to customers and the margin still works after all costs. Do not actively seek out overseas suppliers as a general sourcing strategy. [L0358.057]
  • Brands that manufacture outside the US often work only through approved master distributors. Expect to apply to those distributors, and plan for smaller margins when buying through them. [L1068.037]

Other overseas suppliers

  • Overseas suppliers are usually a poor fit for dropshipping because transit takes weeks, and freight on large or heavy products can approach the product price itself. [L0358.057]
  • Shipping from a supplier abroad often costs about as much as the product, so international dropshipping is rarely worth it. It can only make sense for very small, light items that ship fast. [L0468.045]
  • Do not drop-ship from other countries as a general rule, because shipping times and fees are often too high. Only consider it when the product is clearly in demand and customers accept long waits and extra cost. [L0834.030]
  • Avoid suppliers who ship from overseas to the target market when the product also has US stock. Long international delivery times and high shipping costs erode both margin and customer trust. [L1111.034]
  • Avoid dropshipping from Europe to North America or vice versa. Shipping costs are too high and reduce margins too much. If selling in Europe, create a separate store and source suppliers with inventory stocked in Europe. [L0270.063]
  • Shipping from Europe to the US is often not cost-effective, especially for large or heavy goods. Check the shipping cost against the selling price before deciding; a long delivery time can be shown as a benefit if the margin still works. [L0357.021]
  • Suppliers outside the US are acceptable when the products are only available there and quality is good. The risk to avoid is cheap, slow marketplace dropshipping, where delivery takes weeks, quality disappoints and customers keep asking where their order is. [L1086.033]
  • Suppliers in nearby countries such as Europe can work if shipping remains affordable and no high import taxes apply. [L0962.013]
  • Foreign-manufactured products are normal. Avoid suppliers that dropship from another country and look for stock in your country of business instead. [L0466.039]
  • Some suppliers are shipped from abroad under FOB or online-only landing prices. These import terms are rarely used in this model. Confirm that the supplier already has stock in the country where you sell. [L0468.044]
  • If a manufacturer is abroad, use a distributor only when the product is stocked in your country. Ask the manufacturer which distributor or local warehouse holds stock. [L0468.046]
  • If a supplier abroad has no stock in your target country, be hesitant, because shipping costs and delivery times will be high. [L0671.040, L0671.179]
  • Before selling a foreign supplier's products to customers in another country, ask whether the supplier has its own warehouse or a fulfillment center there with stock. If everything ships from abroad, shipping can be expensive and take two to four weeks. Such a supplier is usually not worth it unless it is a top-tier supplier. In that case, the origin and shipping time must be stated on every product page. [L0360.006]
  • Foreign supplier and sale in a second market: a separate store that only targets that region, loaded only with products that should sell there at the needed prices, with traffic only to that region. [L0853.102]
  • Use a matching country domain for a non-US market. A supplier's warehouse location affects lead times and costs. If suppliers are based in Europe and you sell from Europe, use a matching domain such as a.com.eu domain. [L0968.016]

Canada

  • A US business address is not required to sell to US customers from Canada. If you display an address on your website, a forwarding address from a US location (that forwards mail to Canada) is recommended for legitimacy. [L0253.001]
  • Many suppliers in Canada and other countries have a distribution center in the US, so ask for one before assuming high international shipping costs. Costs differ for each supplier and must be checked one by one. [L0357.027]
  • Procedure for a supplier in Canada (or another country) that sells to US customers (Source A, 2019, older):
    1. Contact the supplier individually. [L0357.026]
    2. Introduce yourself and your location. [L0357.026]
    3. State that you sell in the US. [L0357.026]
    4. Ask whether the supplier has a US distribution center and whether there are extra fees or shipping charges for US delivery. [L0357.026]
  • For Canada-based suppliers, first check whether they hold US inventory. If not, ask which carrier they use to ship to US customers and how they avoid border delays. [L0469.021]
  • Ask suppliers if they ship to Canada before you add Canada to your store, and whether they already use a go-to shipper for it. Start with the US market first because it is the bigger market. [L0804.033]
  • If products are already warehoused in the US, they can be sold to both the US and Canada. Choose Canadian suppliers only when the business sells only in Canada. [L0963.088]

UK, Europe and other markets

  • Products from UK suppliers are acceptable only when no US source exists or US sellers all source from the UK. Product pages must state clearly that the products are made and shipped from the UK, with the estimated shipping time to the US. [L0467.029]
  • Brexit and similar trade barriers make supplying UK customers from European warehouses more complicated. Check current import and export rules before sourcing across that border. [L0781.012]
  • Operators in the UK may need to adjust the standard drop-ship model and explain the process to local suppliers who are not familiar with it. Successful UK sellers have documented those adjustments and shared them with their supplier partners. [L1068.082]
  • The same MAP reseller rules are described as applying in the UK, Germany, Australia and the US. If a market appears to have no MAP for a brand, research how experienced resellers in that market handle the brand rather than assuming it cannot be sold. [L0663.077]

Brand, importer, manufacturer and distributor

  • Always start by seeking direct relationships with brands. Use importers, wholesalers or manufacturers that carry other brands only when you cannot get approved by enough direct suppliers. [L0962.006]
  • Going directly to the supplier is preferred over a distributor. Only use a distributor after an extra layer of research, not by default. [L0359.024]
  • Going straight to the brand gives you a direct contact, current product information, pricing control, and authorized retailer status. Direct brand relationships are the basis for profitable dropshipping. (Source A, 2018, older) [L0466.021]
  • Contact brands directly, not distributors or wholesalers. If an overseas supplier has US warehouse presence and carries legitimate branded products (verifiable through competing stores), contact them. Never lead with general distributors as primary suppliers. [L0276.042]
  • Prefer approval directly with the brand (the importer that owns the brand name) over a distributor, because distributors control MAP enforcement, pricing and relationship terms. Use a distributor only when no direct brand approval is possible. (Source A, 2022, older) [L0665.012]
  • Distributors do not own the brands they sell and rarely control prices, so margins are lower. Avoid distributors when a direct supplier approval is possible. [L0855.069]
  • Use dropship suppliers that own their brand and sell under it, not intermediaries, supplier directories, or dropshipping from China. The store sells the supplier's brand to the customer. [L0759.003]
  • Work directly with brands or their authorized local importers, and avoid supplier directories and middlemen. Middlemen add cost and make it harder to get approved. (Source A, 2020, older) [L0852.024]
  • Distributors that resell many brands as middlemen usually add a markup and often do not enforce MAP pricing. Buy directly from the brand whenever possible. [L0970.048]
  • Do not contact brick-and-mortar retailers that also sell online for wholesale, because they have access to brands that only work with physical stores. Focus supplier contacts on brands that work with online-only stores, so approval is possible across them. [L0968.015]
  • Work with the brand, since the brand name on the product is what matters. A brand may manufacture its own products or use one or several third-party manufacturers, so the setup behind it does not change which supplier you contract with. (Source A, 2019, older) [L1086.036]
  • An importer that owns the brand name is the supplier to approach for an imported product, because it is the company that brings in and sells the product. [L0665.123]
  • A holding company that owns several brands is an acceptable supplier, while a middleman distributor that does not own the brands should be replaced by direct brand relationships. (Source A, 2019, older) [L1069.009]
  • A supplier is any company you can be approved to sell for directly. Manufacturers can also be suppliers, but some suppliers do not manufacture their own products. (Source A, 2023) [L0855.070]
  • Verify the supplier's business model with these checks:
    • A company whose site looks like an agency or marketing firm can still be a wholesale distributor of brands. Check whether it lists brands and wholesale distribution before adding its contact details. [L0940.025]
    • A website selling several brands is not always a distributor, since it can be the parent company that owns those brands. Identify which model the seller follows before deciding whether to work with it. [L0970.049]
    • A third-party website that sells a supplier's products at wholesale is only acceptable if it is a distributor that still enforces MAP. If it is a company that buys from the supplier and resells, do not list those products. [L0964.029]
    • When the same company name appears on two supplier lists with different wholesale prices, it may be a distributor rather than the brand itself. Ask the supplier to explain the difference and keep a screenshot as evidence. (Source A, 2020, older) [L0852.025]

When a distributor is acceptable

  • When a brand routes business only through a distributor, it is acceptable to work with that distributor. Before doing so, ask the distributor whether it enforces MAP policies and whether its markup leaves enough margin for the store. (Source A, 2022, older) [L0429.026]
  • A distributor is acceptable only if it is the brand's wholesale arm that manages everything, enforces MAP, and gives you a margin you can compete with. Before deciding, request price lists, check whether MAP is enforced, and check the margin and competition. (Source A, 2023) [L0835.028]
  • Distributors are acceptable only if they enforce MAP pricing and the product margins are significant after their markup. Compare distributor price lists before committing. (Source A, 2021, older) [L0962.009]
  • Example: in medical equipment for home use, many manufacturers all pointed to one distributor that carried their full range. The distributor enforced MAP pricing and the margins were good, so that route was viable. (Source A, 2021, older) [L0962.010]
  • A supplier that sells through a distributor or dealer network may not offer a clean drop-ship account. Only work with it if it is the only way to win the brand, which happens for a very small share of suppliers. (Source A, 2020, older) [L0468.056]
  • Working through a distributor is a fallback when a brand sells only through distributors. It is worth doing if the products sell, but direct relationships with the brand are the goal. Distributors often have no MAP policy, so exclude their products from the price match policy. (Source A, 2019, older) [L1069.008]
  • A seller in a country should prefer brands or importers that hold stock locally. A distributor is acceptable only when no direct brand link exists. (Source A, 2020, older) [L0852.121]
  • In one past case about 90 percent of brands in a niche sold through distributors, and the distributor enforced MAP policies. Such cases are rare, so contacting the distributor can still be worth it when a store is already built around that niche. (Source A, 2023) [L1094.011]
  • Some suppliers claim they must work through distributors by region because of non-compete clauses. First confirm whether they actually enforce MAP; if they do not, the distributor requirement is not a real barrier and the niche can still be pursued. (Source A, 2021, older) [L1090.017]
  • Distributors can be harder to get authorized, so mention the name of the person or company that referred you when you contact them. Ask early whether they enforce pricing policies, since not all distributors do. (Source A, 2023) [L0470.092]

Distributor checks

  • Some brands only sell through a distributor or clearinghouse. If that is the case, vet the intermediary on MAP enforcement, pricing policy, policy toward competitors, how hard approval is, and shipping speed before listing its products. [L0359.023]
  • A distributor that is not a manufacturer usually makes profit harder to earn, and distributors often have no MAP policy. Contact the individual brands the distributor sells, because direct relationships give better pricing. (Source A, 2019, older) [L0357.022]
  • For supplier distributors such as TD Synnex or Ingram Micro, check whether they have a MAP policy. If none is enforced, do not use a price match guarantee and confirm the margins still work. (Source A, 2022, older) [L0963.087]
  • Do not work with a supplier that has granted sole distributorship for a market to a physical plus online store there. Pass on that supplier instead of competing with the exclusive partner. (Source A, 2021, older) [L0962.011]
  • Some suppliers route new retailers to third-party distribution centers, and those centers may refuse new accounts or ask for a large first order. Ask each center directly whether it opens accounts for new retailers before counting that supplier as available. Treat a refusal from a distribution center as a supplier-level blocker to work around, not as a final answer for the whole niche. (Source A, 2025) [L0966.107]
  • A distributor that bans returns while competitors and the manufacturer offer 30-day returns is a warning sign. Move on to other suppliers and reapply directly after three to six months of established business. (Source A, 2022, older) [L0429.062]
  • Brands that manufacture outside the US often work only through approved master distributors. (Source A, 2019, older) [L1068.037]

MAP policy

Definition and purpose

  • Minimum Advertised Price (MAP) is a pricing floor set by suppliers that prevents authorized retailers from advertising products below that price, protecting fair margins for all sellers and preventing destructive price competition. [L0110.005]
  • Minimum advertised price (MAP) is the lowest price a retailer may advertise a supplier's product for. When a retailer lists below it, a supplier that enforces MAP contacts that retailer, who must raise the price or lose its authorization to sell. [L0711.023]
  • MAP is a contractual floor: an authorized retailer signs an agreement not to advertise the product below a set price, usually calculated as wholesale cost multiplied by a stated factor. Some suppliers have no MAP at all, and in some countries MAP policies cannot be enforced by law. Check whether a supplier enforces MAP before judging it. [L1051.001]
  • Brands use MAP to protect their brand value and their retail partners: if one retailer sells below the floor, others lose sales and the perceived value of the product drops. MAP therefore keeps prices stable across all sellers. [L1051.002]
  • MSRP is the manufacturer's suggested retail price and MAP is the minimum advertised price. A supplier that sells online at MSRP while letting you sell at MAP gives you a price advantage over the supplier's own website. (Source A, 2018, older) [L1085.012]
  • Silver and gold suppliers expect you not to list their products below their Minimum Advertised Price (MAP). Undercutting their pricing breaks the supplier trust relationship. [L0121.001]
  • As an Authorized Retailer you represent the supplier's brand. Poor customer experiences with your business create negative associations that damage the supplier's brand reputation, not just your own. [L0121.002]

Testing MAP enforcement

  • Search for a supplier's products on Google Shopping before calling. Widely varying prices for the same product suggest that MAP is not enforced, which is a red flag. (Source A, 2019, older) [L1068.035]
  • Procedure to check whether a supplier enforces MAP (Source A, 2021, older):
    1. Search Google for the supplier's company name. [L0853.039]
    2. Look at the supplier's most popular products in Google Shopping. [L0853.039]
    3. Compare the prices sellers list for the same product. [L0853.039]
    4. If prices vary widely, the supplier probably does not enforce MAP. [L0853.039]
  • Procedure for a brand that unauthorized sellers list at MAP (Source A, 2023):
    1. Keep advertising your own authorized status and the brand's warranty. [L0970.050]
    2. Ask the brand to contact the unauthorized sellers and have their listings removed. [L0970.050]
    3. If that does not work, the brand can file a DMCA notice with the host company of the listing site. [L0970.050]
  • Sellers should check a supplier's MAP enforcement before approval. Being approved without a MAP policy to sign is a red flag that the supplier does not enforce MAP. (Source A, 2023) [L1094.017]
  • A supplier that approves a retailer without sending a MAP policy to sign is a red flag, since it likely has no MAP policy at all. Check whether a supplier enforces MAP before relying on it. (Source A, 2023) [L1094.121]
  • A supplier that says it has no control over MAP prices is not a true MAP supplier. For such suppliers, keep selling only if the products sell at MAP and traffic is profitable. (Source A, 2022, older) [L0429.024]
  • When a supplier with a MAP policy does not enforce it, it is usually not worth staying with them. First contact them directly and make the problem clear before deciding to drop them. (Source A, 2022, older) [L0665.013]
  • Competing stores should not be allowed to disrupt MAP through discounts. A non-competing store that advertises your brand keywords and sells other brands at low prices can be addressed by contacting the brand that you sell for, which can ask Google to block the advertiser from bidding on its brand name. (Source A, 2022, older) [L0665.021]
  • Ideally every supplier carries a MAP policy. A small number without one, such as two out of twenty, can be accepted. Those brands must be excluded from the price match policy, and their competitor prices should be checked before any ads run. (Source A, 2019, older) [L1069.092]
  • Suppliers that enforce MAP and ship on your own account are preferable to non-MAP suppliers. Shipping on your own account can feel intimidating at first, but it becomes routine once you work with several suppliers. (Source A, 2023) [L0835.027]

Non-MAP suppliers

  • A supplier without MAP pricing is not an automatic rejection. MAP suppliers are preferred, but the decision depends on what competitors charge for the same products. (Source A, 2021, older) [L0671.008, L0671.159]
  • Supplier MAP policies are enforced, but many MAP suppliers are workable. Non-MAP suppliers can also be profitable, though some countries do not allow MAP, so the decision depends on the market and what products are available. (Source A, 2025) [L0472.021]
  • Non-MAP suppliers can be a valid first step. Having live products on your store increases trust, which can help you get approved by other suppliers. (Source A, 2024) [L0471.039]
  • Non-MAP supplier research, in order (Source A):
    1. Apply to every brand in your niche that you can get approved for (example: 50 brands means contacting all 50), and collect their price lists. Being an authorized retailer does not oblige you to sell their products. [L1051.003]
    2. Identify which suppliers are MAP and which are not from the price lists. [L1051.003]
    3. For each non-MAP supplier, build your own price list with columns for Wholesale, MSRP (manufacturer suggested retail price) and Competition. [L1051.003]
    4. Search each product name on Google Shopping as a customer would, and record the average competitor price in the Competition column. Use established, legitimate-looking stores for the average. [L1051.003]
    5. Check each supplier's average competitor price against its wholesale cost using the 2x rule (see next units). [L1051.003]
    6. Only upload products that pass the 2x rule; skip the rest without regret. [L1051.003]
    7. Monitor KPIs in the Google Ads account on a fixed schedule (see below). [L1051.003]

Direct-to-consumer conflict

How common and how to judge it

  • Roughly 20% of suppliers also sell directly to consumers on their own website, and most do not. A supplier's direct website only leaves room for profitable drop shipping when the site is weak, outdated, slow, has poor images or poor product descriptions. (Source A, 2020, older) [L0664.015]
  • If a supplier's own site is polished and offers too little discount for you to afford paid traffic, skip that supplier. If the site is weak, you can still compete with a bonus gift, expiring coupon codes or by matching the price. (Source A, 2020, older) [L0664.016]
  • A supplier's own online store is not a red flag if it sells at MSRP and allows you to sell at MAP. Weak supplier websites (one photo, poor descriptions, missing features) are an opening to take sales with a better product page. (Source A, 2018, older) [L1085.013]
  • A manufacturer that sells direct to consumers is not automatically a problem. It is harder to compete with when its site uses advanced pixel tracking and is clearly built for online selling, but it is fine when online sales are only an add-on to a brand website. (Source A, 2024) [L0965.025]
  • Meta Pixel Helper is a free Chrome extension that lights up with the number of Meta pixels on any site you visit. Several pixels, such as a page view plus an AMP page view, show that a supplier or competitor is investing in direct online sales. (Source A, 2024) [L0965.026]
  • Check whether a supplier advertises directly to consumers (Source A, 2023):
    1. Search the brand name on Google. [L0804.024]
    2. Check Google Shopping for the brand's listings. [L0804.024]
    3. Check the search text ads at the top of Google. [L0804.024]
    4. Check whether you see their Facebook remarketing ads. [L0804.024]

Conditions for selling a brand that sells direct

  • Sell for suppliers who sell direct to consumers only if: (1) they sell at MSRP and allow retailers to sell at MAP, or (2) their direct-to-consumer experience is poor and you can provide superior UX, more information, and an easier buying process. (Source A, 2022, older) [L0249.012]
  • A supplier that sells direct to the public can still be acceptable if its direct price equals MSRP and it lets retailers sell at MAP. Example: MSRP of 1,500 USD for direct sales and MAP of 1,200 USD for retailers. (Source A, 2021, older) [L1090.015]
  • A supplier that sells direct can also be acceptable if its own online store shows clear neglect, such as outdated design, slow loading, broken links, poor product images or weak information. In that case a retailer can provide a better shopping experience. (Source A, 2021, older) [L1090.016]
  • Suppliers that sell direct to the public at MSRP while allowing retailers to sell at MAP are workable. Avoid suppliers with strong direct-to-consumer websites that compete with your store, unless their site looks outdated and shows little effort. (Source A, 2021, older) [L0968.014]
  • Suppliers selling directly to the public at MSRP that allow retailers to sell at MAP are acceptable. Suppliers selling direct where e-commerce is weak can still be profitable. (Source A, 2022, older) [L0969.043]
  • Supplier with a sale on its own website: add products only if it sells at MSRP and allows retailers to sell at MAP, or sells direct but shows little investment in its e-commerce site. (Source A, 2022, older) [L0969.044]
  • A supplier who sells direct to consumers is acceptable if they sell at MSRP while retailers sell at MAP, or if their direct site is weak and unmarketed. If they run a strong direct-to-consumer business with ads and remarketing, expect your margin to be eroded. (Source A, 2023) [L0804.022]
  • Sell a brand that also sells direct to consumers only if you gain an advantage: either the supplier sells at MSRP while you sell at MAP, or the supplier's site looks outdated so you look more trustworthy. Otherwise do not sell that brand. (Source A, 2022, older) [L0803.031]
  • Sell a brand that sells direct only if the supplier sells at MSRP while you sell at MAP, or if it sells direct from a weak site. Avoid suppliers with strong direct-to-consumer websites that compete with your store. [L0247.021]
  • Suppliers that sell both online and through physical retail locations are still viable. Cross-check their pricing on competitor stores and Amazon to ensure they are not undercutting your pricing significantly. Allow them through your approval process unless you identify specific margin or MAP policy issues. (Source A, 2023) [L0251.003]
  • A supplier that sells direct to consumers at MSRP, with a physical showroom, is acceptable if you can still sell at MAP. Supplier retail pricing should give you room to sell below it. (Source A, 2021, older) [L0853.101]
  • Choose suppliers whose competitors have no retail store, showroom or warehouse presence. Check competitor websites for this before approaching suppliers, because suppliers with retail presence often refuse drop shipping. (Source A, 2021, older) [L0671.038, L0671.177]

Undercutting risk

  • Suppliers can sell to the public directly and undercut retailers, which is a warning sign. If a supplier's recommended retail prices or its own site sell below what you can offer, do not list that brand or negotiate price parity before advertising it. (Source A, 2021, older) [L0801.030]
  • Avoid suppliers that sell directly to the public and would undercut your MAP price. Prefer suppliers who sell only to businesses and let you sell at MAP. (Source A, 2021, older) [L0469.024]
  • If a supplier sells directly to the public at a lower price than your planned selling price, do not upload that supplier's products when you know this in advance. If you find out later, keep the products on the store but do not include them in Google Ads. (Source A, 2020, older) [L0551.015]

Marketplace and channel restrictions

  • If a supplier forbids selling on Google, Amazon, eBay, Facebook or Bing, ask for clarification, because most suppliers only restrict marketplaces. Ask how the store is expected to advertise before deciding to work with them. (Source A, 2020, older) [L0967.028]
  • Suppliers that sell on Wayfair are not a problem, because large marketplaces often deliver poorly and treat their suppliers badly. Suppliers may then prefer to send business directly to the seller. (Source A, 2018, older) [L1085.015]
  • If a supplier requires an inquire-only button and no cart, do not list its products, because Google Shopping ads need products that can be bought directly. (Source A, 2021, older) [L0671.059]
  • Some suppliers require that orders are shipped using the retailer's own shipping account, and some do not accept customer returns at all. (Source A, 2025) [L0837.041]

Product restrictions that affect supplier choice

  • Battery-powered items such as Electric Bicycles, Electric Scooters, Go-Karts, Golf Carts and RC Cars, RC Planes, RC Boats and RC Helicopters can face shipping restrictions for their batteries. Confirm the carrier's and supplier's lithium battery rules before committing to these items. [L0546.016]
  • Products with a built-in battery often cannot be shipped to the US. Confirm with the supplier or carrier that a battery product can reach the target country before sourcing it or running ads. Discovering a shipping restriction after ads are live wastes test budget. [L0552.052]
  • On AliExpress, a product needs at least one listing with a 4.6+ rating and at least 50 reviews, ideally with all listings at 4.6+. If no seller reaches 4.6+, the product is probably low quality. [L0636.011]
  • Skip AliExpress products with more than 10,000 orders, because high order counts mean the product is already heavily scaled and riskier to sell. [L0636.012]
  • Check consumer reviews for damage before choosing a brand (Source A, 2019, older):
    1. Search for the brand's products on Google as a customer would. [L0663.039]
    2. Read the consumer reviews. [L0663.039]
    3. Look for a repeated pattern of items arriving damaged. [L0663.039]
    4. Avoid or adjust products where damage is a trend, especially if you must ship on your own account. [L0663.039]

Supplier vetting procedures

  • Procedure to confirm a brand is legitimate (Source A):
    1. Research brand by searching on Google. [L0255.007]
    2. Search brand name alone. [L0255.007]
    3. Search brand name with 'reviews'. [L0255.007]
    4. Evaluate search results for supplier reliability and reputation issues. [L0255.007]
    5. If problems appear, avoid listing their products. [L0255.007]
  • Procedure to confirm a brand's own website and independence (Source A):
    1. Search Google for each brand name in Column A. [L0056.009]
    2. Find and visit the brand's official website. [L0056.009]
    3. If it is a private label or marketplace-exclusive product, delete the row. [L0056.009]
    4. Confirm the supplier operates independently. [L0056.009]
  • Procedure to confirm a supplier serves only online stores (Source A, 2019, older):
    1. Search Google for the niche. [L1069.003]
    2. Use only the websites of brands that sell exclusively online, and skip brick-and-mortar retailers. [L1069.003]
    3. Confirm the company has no showroom and no warehouse; an office alone is acceptable. [L1069.003]
    4. Source supplier names from the websites of your true future competitors, since suppliers found there are more likely to work with online-only stores. [L1069.003]
  • Supplier traffic rank does not matter for choosing a supplier, since suppliers usually do not try to attract traffic. A competitor's traffic rank is a useful signal instead. (Source A, 2020, older) [L0551.013]
  • Search for a supplier's products on Google Shopping before calling, and treat widely varying prices as a red flag. (Source A, 2019, older) [L1068.035]
  • Before judging a supplier, check whether it enforces MAP. Some suppliers have no MAP at all. [L1051.001]

Supplier responsiveness, returns and support

  • Before approval, make sure a supplier answers the phone and emails. A brand that never answers may not support products after approval. (Source A, 2020, older) [L1111.041]
  • If a supplier cannot answer technical product questions such as steel type or weight capacity, treat that as a red flag for working with the supplier. If a question is about product selection (such as which barbell suits a given body weight and lift), create a buying guide on your site rather than dismiss the question. (Source A, 2022, older) [L0429.035]
  • A supplier that offers no return policy at all is a warning sign, and it is more worrying when every approved supplier has the same policy. Investigate those suppliers before building products on them. (Source A, 2021, older) [L0853.003]
  • Do not buy an expensive supplier demo product just to learn their customer support, because such requests are unusual. Only consider it when there is a strong resale market, and check first how quickly the product can be resold. (Source A, 2019, older) [L0356.032]
  • Avoid suppliers that expect the retailer to provide local support, repairs or solutions to customers' technical problems. These demands move a heavy support burden onto the store. (Source A, 2021, older) [L0247.028]
  • Some suppliers request an opening order before granting catalog access. Respond that you need assurance you can sell their products at the required price, and keep contacting other suppliers in the meantime. (Source A, 2021, older) [L0247.029]
  • A store that says products from certain brands may not ship the same day is usually dropshipping those items from third-party warehouses it does not control. [L0459.012]

Choosing a supplier by motivation

  • Choose a supplier based on your motivation: speed, quality or price, because these three priorities lead to very different supplier choices. [L0780.024]
  • Shipping time is a factor to review before choosing a supplier, weighed against product, price and margin. [L0440.019]
  • Partner with US-based suppliers achieving 2 to 3 day delivery instead of international dropshippers with 21 to 30 day turnaround. Faster fulfillment reduces perceived time delay and effort/sacrifice, dramatically multiplying the value of the same product. [L0233.013]

Where sources disagree

  • Overseas sourcing. The strict position is no dropshipping from China or from abroad by default [L0359.025, L0671.039, L0671.178, L0963.082, L0469.027, L0834.030]. The exceptions are a custom, high-demand single overseas maker where delivery and margin work [L0358.057], a nearby country such as Europe where shipping is affordable and no high import taxes apply [L0962.013], an outside-US supplier whose product is unique and of good quality [L1086.033], and a UK supplier only when no US source exists [L0467.029]. The 2019 position that long delivery can be a benefit if the margin still works is older and applies only when the margin holds after shipping [L0357.021].
  • Origin versus stock location. A product made in China is not a problem when a brand or importer holds it locally [L0852.121, L0941.024, L1085.014, L0466.039]. The problem is shipping from overseas. Use stock location as the test, not the country of manufacture. [L0963.082, L0941.024]
  • Delivery numbers. The 2 to 3 day figure for US suppliers comes from Source C [L0233.013]. The 2 to 7 day figure for a high-ticket US manufacturer comes from Source B, 2021 to 2022, older [L0458.059]. Both describe US stock. Use the number from your own supplier's stated process.
  • Non-MAP suppliers. Red flags are an approval with no MAP policy to sign and a supplier that has no control over MAP [L1094.121, L1094.017, L0429.024]. Non-MAP suppliers remain workable as a first step, when competitor prices support the margin, when a small share of brands lacks MAP, or in markets where MAP cannot be enforced [L0671.008, L0671.159, L0471.039, L1069.092, L0472.021, L1051.003]. The distinction is whether the supplier enforces MAP, not whether it has a policy on paper.
  • Distributors. The default is direct brand approval first [L0962.006, L0359.024, L0665.012, L0855.069]. Distributors are accepted when they enforce MAP and leave enough margin [L0962.009, L0835.028, L0429.026], when they are the only route to the brand [L0468.056], or as a fallback that is not the goal [L1069.008]. The 2019 statement that distributors often have no MAP is older and still applies as a check [L0357.022].
  • Direct-to-consumer. The 2021 position is that a strong supplier store that would match your price is not worth the time [L0247.021]. The 2023 position is that omnichannel suppliers are viable unless specific margin or MAP issues appear [L0251.003]. Both apply: check the pricing on competitor stores and Amazon, and decline a strong direct store that would match your price [L0247.021, L0251.003, L0804.022].
  • Showrooms. A supplier showroom selling at MSRP is acceptable when you can still sell at MAP [L0853.101]. The same chapter advises choosing suppliers whose competitors have no retail presence, because retail presence often leads to refusals [L0671.038, L0671.177]. Treat showroom presence as acceptable only after you have checked whether the supplier approves drop shipping.
  • Distributors for region versus MAP. A regional distributor requirement is not a barrier if the supplier does not enforce MAP [L1090.017]. Distributors that do enforce MAP are the exception that justifies working through them [L0962.009, L1094.011].

Supplier Location and Domestic Stock

Start Local

  • At the start, work only with suppliers based in the country where you sell, who already stock the products there, either directly or through a local distributor or subsidiary of an overseas parent company. For the EU, the products should ideally already be in the EU, possibly in several EU countries. [L1480.010]
  • Do not start by shipping products across borders, for example from China into your sales market. Cross-border shipping can come later, but it is not the starting model. [L1480.011]
  • Work only with local suppliers in the same country as the target customers, not with overseas manufacturers, so the product ships domestically. [L1481.013]
  • Local, independent, established suppliers make operations much easier in many ways. Suppliers are usually experienced and open to working with new sellers. [L1480.012]
  • Local suppliers in a market with a strong e-commerce and retail industry often have established, tested, high-quality products and are looking for online retail partners to sell them. [L1481.014]

Overseas Manufacturing and US Stock

  • Products may be manufactured overseas, for example in Italy, as long as the supplier keeps the stock in a US warehouse that the orders ship from; the headquarters location does not matter. [L1113.020]
  • Products from suppliers are usually from US-based brands that hold stock in the US, not products made in the US. Brands can be from the same country as the store's shipping base, and the store's suppliers do not need to manufacture locally. [L1114.017]
  • Source from suppliers with stock or a warehouse in the United States. Dropshipping from Europe to the U.S. is generally too expensive to be workable. [L1274.034]

Checking Stock Readiness

  • When a supplier cannot ship a product from U.S. stock right away, check whether it has a set of bestselling products that are always kept in its U.S. warehouse. Only list those in-stock bestsellers as U.S.-ready products. [L1115.007]

Brand, Distributor and Importer

Source Direct

  • Source directly from the brand rather than from wholesalers, because direct margins are much better. [L1113.027]
  • Work directly with manufacturers rather than distributors or middlemen. Manufacturers usually carry several sub-categories of products that you can select from. [L1274.022]
  • Buy from the brand itself rather than from a large retailer that resells its products. The closer you are to the actual brand, the better. [L1275.022]
  • A brand that sells direct to consumers will often refuse a dropshipper at first, which is normal. Use the same approval steps and expect the relationship to be slightly different. [L1275.021]

When a Distributor Is Acceptable

  • Use a distributor only when the supplier sells exclusively through that distributor. Also confirm the distributor enforces price policies; a middleman that adds a markup and has no pricing controls should be avoided when the supplier can be contacted directly. [L1283.009]
  • Only work with a distributor if it enforces MAP. Brands that sell outside the distributor can often be approached directly. [L1275.026]
  • In a niche where most brands only sell through a distributor, the rest of the brands (around 5 to 10 percent) may still be available for direct approval. Check MAP enforcement before signing up with the distributor. [L1275.027]
  • Suppliers that sell direct to consumers and use distributors in the US may raise prices or limit stock for resellers in shortages. Distributors are therefore not a preferred source. [L1112.047]

Conditions for Online Sellers

  • Some suppliers only approve stores that can show they sell online. Suppliers that also sell to brick-and-mortar retailers are acceptable if you found them through online-only retailers. [L1285.019]

Direct-to-Consumer Conflict

  • A supplier that already sells direct to consumers on Google Shopping is only worth approaching if it sells at MSRP and allows you to sell at MAP, or if its own product pages are weak enough that you can beat them. Otherwise, skip it. [L1113.088]

MAP Policy and Enforcement

What MAP Is

  • MAP stands for minimum advertised price, the lowest price a retailer may advertise for a product. After approval, the supplier gives you a MAP price list, and you may never list a product below its MAP. [L1422.004]
  • Example: if supplier ABC sets the MAP at $1,000, no retailer may advertise its products below $1,000, and that includes you. [L1422.005]
  • Track supplier price lists and MAP policies in writing from the supplier. Verify them before listing products because enforcement varies by supplier. [L1281.122]

Why MAP Matters

  • A supplier often already has about five other retailers selling its products. Those retailers depend on stable prices to keep their margins, so MAP violations hurt all of them. [L1422.006]
  • Undercutting MAP, such as selling a product with a $1,000 MAP for $700, may take all the sales from competing retailers. Those retailers then lose margin, get upset, and may stop carrying the brand, which damages the supplier's market. [L1422.007]
  • MAP protects the profit margins of retailers, so good retailers keep stocking the brand. Suppliers want as many quality retailers as possible and will act against anyone who breaks the pricing policy. [L1422.008]

Checking Coupons and Discounts

  • Each supplier's MAP agreement should be checked for whether discounts or coupons are allowed. If coupons are not allowed, the agreement will say so, and MAP software usually checks the displayed price, so a discount shown on the page is what gets flagged. [L1114.023]

Testing Enforcement in MAP Markets

  • MAP rules are country-specific, but most countries outside the United States have no MAP. In those markets, you should apply for approval with every supplier you find, not only with suppliers who enforce MAP. [L1146.001]
  • In MAP markets such as the United States, test whether a supplier enforces MAP by checking whether its retailers all sell at the same price, not below the minimum threshold. This test does not apply in non-MAP markets. [L1146.016]

Procedure: Check MAP enforcement before contacting a supplier (older advice, 2018)

  1. Search the brand names from your niche research on Google.
  2. Find each brand's best-selling products on the websites of the sellers that appear.
  3. Take the product names and SKUs of those best sellers and search them on Google Shopping.
  4. Compare the prices that different sellers list.
  5. Consistent prices suggest MAP is enforced. Widely varying prices, such as $500, $550 and $800 for the same product, suggest it is probably not enforced, even if the supplier has a MAP policy. [L1274.025]
  • If a product sells for $1,000 at five stores while one store sells it for $600, treat this as a red flag. Avoid advertising that supplier because its prices are not controlled. [L1274.026]

Regions with Restricted MAP (older advice, 2018)

  • In regions where MAP policies are restricted, such as parts of Europe and possibly Australia according to the call, drop the price match policy. Screen every supplier more carefully to ensure no one is selling their products at a loss. [L1274.041]

Marketplace Restrictions

  • A brand that is already sold on large online-only retailers such as Wayfair can still be considered a dropship-friendly supplier. Being listed on a big marketplace does not on its own disqualify a brand. [L1223.034]
  • Products sold on AliExpress may be near-copies of a legitimate brand's product, which can lead to a supplier being shut down and to legal trouble. A product that is suddenly removed from AliExpress may have been taken down after a rights holder complaint. [L1440.016]

Drop-Ship Friendliness Checks

Quotes, Pricing and Fulfillment

  • Source and validate the supplier before selling: get proper quotes, confirm pricing and confirm that the supplier can fulfill the product. [L1259.054]
  • Get proper quotes, confirm prices and confirm the supplier can fulfill the item before selling. Contact several suppliers as backups to compare prices and quality and to ensure after-sale support and return policy. [L1260.055]

Pre-Upload Quality Check

  • Before uploading a supplier's products, search the brand online for reviews and product quality. Check that the listing is worth the effort, since you will rely on organic traffic. [L1277.045]

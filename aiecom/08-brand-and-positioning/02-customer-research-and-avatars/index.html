8.2 Customer research and avatars

Research before the store and the copy

  • Understanding an audience means looking past demographics (age, gender, socioeconomic status) to behaviour and the underlying motivations, meaning the reasons why people act. This distinction separates effective advertisers from those who work by trial and error. [L0258.001]

  • Before branding, the store owner must know what the product is, what it does and how it benefits customers. The owner must also know who the customers are, how they behave, and what their needs, goals and motivations are. [L1044.010]

  • Understand the customer before designing the store. Review a customer Facebook group, the photos members post and what they watch online. This removes guesswork about layout, imagery and messaging. [L0709.030]

  • Before writing any copy, define the target market: the problems, desires and other facts about prospective customers. Copy works as a conversation with those people, so it only persuades when you know them well. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0907.001]

  • Keep the customer avatar sheet as a one-page reference that anyone can check before writing copy for a single product, email or ad. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0907.002]

  • Customers are defined as the group of people who experience the problem your product solves, so customer research must confirm that this group exists. Knowing the ideal customer in detail is required before you can communicate the problem and solution effectively. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0872.004]

  • Empathy and the ability to see your business from the customer's perspective are core marketing skills. The more you put yourself in the customer's position, the better the marketing becomes, especially for fields that must be estimated by intuition. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0872.034]

  • Customer research is not a one-time task. Review the customer research document at least once a year and update it whenever you learn something new, because the customer base can shift over time. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0872.013]

  • Completing the avatar profile before writing copy shortens the copywriting process. Treat the avatar as a living document and update it as you learn more about the market. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0907.029]

  • Audience research should cover four pillars:

    Pillar What it describes
    Demographics Who they are
    Psychographics What they value
    Behaviours How they act: research habits, loyalty, online communities
    Goals, needs and motivations What they want and what belonging they seek

    Use these pillars so that all brand messaging speaks to a defined group rather than everyone. [L1071.005]

  • Before filling in the audience section of a brand document, prepare two earlier research outputs: a detailed customer research spreadsheet and a shorter summary document. Base demographics, psychographics, behaviours and goals on these sources rather than on assumptions. [L1071.022]

  • For performance-oriented products, audience psychographics typically include a desire for quality and results, a strong interest in the activity the product supports, and appreciation of brand status and reputation. The brand image and product descriptions should reflect these values directly. [L1071.023]

  • When defining a brand's target audience, the overall group can be broad, spanning both genders, several age groups and low to high income levels. Name one focused core age range where the product category is most active, such as 15 to 45 for a sports brand, and let that core guide the messaging. [L1071.021]

  • Enter the research findings into the research form to sharpen the ICP and USP before any creative is produced. [L1019.027]

The four layers: demographics, psychographics, behaviour and motivation

  • The four segmentation components:

    Component What it covers Examples
    Demographics Static attributes of a person Age, gender, income level, education, marital status
    Psychographics Personality, values, beliefs and attitudes Openness, conscientiousness, level of organization, tendency to worry; values such as control or belonging; beliefs about the world, religion, opinions
    Behavior What people do, their context and where the problem fits into their daily life or browsing situation Using a product in a kitchen, scrolling a feed while queuing in a store
    Goals, needs and motivations The underlying why behind what people do Status, power, security, freedom, belonging

    [L0993.005]

  • Demographics are the most surface-level layer and should be considered last when defining a target audience. They matter when they change whether the product fits. Example: a menstruation product, since people over 50 generally no longer menstruate, as the average age of stopping is above 50. [L0993.006]

  • Demographics alone often fail to explain buying. Buyers of one jewelry piece can range from 20 to 50 years old. The underlying need for status is usually a stronger basis for targeting than age or income. [L0993.007]

  • Psychographics cover how a person is wired and what they believe: personality traits, values such as control or belonging, beliefs about the world, and attitudes and opinions. Use them to choose the tone and angle of the message. [L0993.008]

  • For a viewer persona, needs and motivations matter more than demographics. Capture what the person desperately wants, what they fear not getting, what stands in their way and what outcome they want. Age, income and education serve only as background. [L1427.003]

  • Behavioural segmentation asks what the person is doing and in which context the problem appears. Place the product at the point in the daily routine where the problem or trigger occurs, not only where they browse. [L0993.009]

  • Example: a vegetable slicer's trigger is the moment someone cuts vegetables by hand, finds it tough and annoying, and gets an uneven cut. Describing the product through that exact moment makes the problem and solution immediately recognizable. [L0993.010]

  • Sharpen the buyer persona with concrete details such as reading habits, where they shop, income level, occupation and how often they buy. For example, a buyer who purchases four times a year as a gift for friends. These details guide ad and website messaging. [L1016.021]

  • Premium beauty-tool example: the buyer invests in appearance and wellness, buys from Instagram and Facebook ads, and trusts beauty influencers and stylists. Describing the target customer and their psychology this concretely gives the audit enough to write relevant messaging. [L0886.028]

  • The persona does not need every field completed. Fill in as much as you can to get a usable picture of the customer. Demographics are static facts, psychographics cover values and personality, behaviour covers what they do and the context of their problem, and motivations cover the deeper needs and feelings. Desire, feature and persona together are enough to start writing a decent headline. [L0516.004]

The iceberg model

  • Use the iceberg model to understand the customer in layers. Demographics (age, gender, location) are the visible surface, behaviour is what they do, the need is the reason behind the behaviour, and the motivation is the deep human driver. Marketing that reaches the deeper layers converts better and builds a sustainable brand. [L1426.034, L0993.015, L0998.018]

  • Goals, needs and motivations explain why people act. Demographics and behaviour are what you usually observe, while the reasons behind them make a message powerful. They usually drive purchases more strongly than demographics or behaviour do. [L0998.017, L0993.012]

  • Think of an iceberg: demographics and behaviour form the visible tip, while needs, tensions, fears and motivations sit underneath. Your task is to uncover the layer below what people do. [L0998.018]

  • Motivation chain, from visible purchase to underlying need:

    • A commuter buys coffee and a bagel on the way to work. The visible behaviour is the purchase.
    • The need is that they have no time to make breakfast.
    • The fear is being late, which could damage their reputation at work or cost them a promotion.
    • The motivation is avoiding shame, losing control of their life and running short of money.

    Use this chain to move from the visible purchase to the underlying need and motive when building a buyer profile. [L0993.015, L0998.019, L0644.046]

  • Status can be a hidden buying driver even when convenience appears to be the reason. A buyer may choose a premium café because its setting makes them look successful while working, not only because it is close by. [L0998.022]

  • Coffee-chain example: people do not buy mainly for the coffee quality. They buy for a pleasant social environment and the status of looking successful while working with a laptop, and proximity is secondary. The deeper status and environment need keeps them coming back. [L0993.013]

  • Ask why the buyer really buys and how the product fits into their life. The surface action, such as buying a quick snack on the way to work, often hides a deeper motive like protecting their reputation or sense of control. [L0644.036]

  • Deeper motivations matter more than surface demographics. A buyer often wants to avoid a deeper consequence, such as missing work and losing a promotion, not just to remove a single pain. Writing copy about that underlying need speaks more intimately. [L0999.046]

  • Ask why repeatedly to move from a visible behaviour to the need behind it. Keep asking until you reach a need such as security, status, freedom or belonging. [L0998.021]

  • Procedure for finding the real motive behind a purchase, asking why at each layer:

    1. What does the customer do?
    2. What need does that action meet?
    3. Why does that need matter to them, for example to protect their reputation or control at work?
    4. What deeper motivation sits underneath, such as identity, control, belonging or status?

    [L1426.043]

  • Procedure for working the iceberg with an audience:

    1. Record the surface facts about the audience: demographics and observable behaviour.
    2. Ask why they do this behaviour and identify the need it serves.
    3. Ask what they fear will happen if the need is not met.
    4. Ask what deeper motivation drives them, such as control, status, security or growth.
    5. Use the deepest layer to shape the message, without necessarily stating every motive openly in the ad.

    [L0993.016]

  • The deepest motives do not always need to appear in ads, but you must understand the audience closely enough to speak to them at that level. Ads that touch the motive often outperform ads that only describe the behaviour. Knowing these motives is what makes a message feel relevant. [L0993.017, L0998.020]

Human drivers and motivations

  • Nine core human drivers motivate purchasing: power and control over outcomes, freedom from pain, conserving or gaining resources (time, effort, money, physical), status and superiority, connection and belonging, sexual selection, social conformity (keeping up with peers), personal growth, and providing for loved ones. Map the product to one or more of these drivers. [L0258.002]
  • Base human needs to check against an audience include: power, control over outcomes, freedom from pain, gaining or conserving resources (time, energy, money), status and superiority, sexual selection, survival, connection, love and belonging, keeping up with peers and not falling behind, growth, and caring for loved ones or having a purpose bigger than oneself. Each need is felt to a different degree depending on the person. [L0993.018, L0998.023, L0997.023, L0997.024]
  • Strong messages tie a product to a fundamental human need instead of just describing its function. Common drivers include power, control over outcomes, freedom from pain, and gaining or conserving resources such as time, energy and money. [L0997.023]
  • These needs are present in every audience but weighted differently depending on the person. Other core needs include status and superiority, connection, love and belonging, keeping up with peers, growth, looking after loved ones, and having a purpose bigger than oneself. [L0997.024, L0998.023]
  • Buyer motivations often sit in opposing pairs, such as control versus enjoyment on the personal side and power versus belonging on the social side. Decide which pair the product speaks to before writing the message. [L0644.037]
  • Opposing motivations create tension that good marketing can address: control versus enjoyment, power versus belonging. Identify which side a product serves and speak to that need in the copy. [L1426.035]
  • Map both directions for each segment. Buyers move toward a goal they want or away from a pain they want to escape. Ads and offers work better when they name the specific pain to remove or the specific goal to reach. [L0497.026]
  • Negative emotions such as fear, shame, guilt, anger, helplessness and grief narrow attention onto one point, which makes them useful for hooks and problem agitation. Positive emotions such as hope, relief, pride, belonging and love broaden attention, which makes them useful for presenting the solution and the transformation. [L0613.024]

Status, identity and social comparison

  • People compare themselves to others and feel envy toward those who have something they lack, while they scorn those who lack something they already have. Brands can use this status comparison dynamic to position a product as the visible sign of being ahead of others. [L0497.025]
  • Status and belonging are powerful because standing within a group affects access to resources and opportunities. Today that means career progression, social acceptance and keeping pace with peers rather than physical survival. [L0997.025]
  • Status, attractiveness and social standing are sensitive needs. Blunt claims about them can backfire, so phrase them carefully and indirectly rather than shaming the viewer. The need should be understood without being stated in a crude way. [L0993.019, L0998.024, L0997.026]
  • Status, comparison and social claims should be articulated in a way the customer can accept about themselves, because blunt wording can insult or alienate the audience. [L0997.026]
  • Desires can include social status, such as wanting others to see the buyer as cool. Status is a strong angle for copy and should be recorded next to practical desires. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0907.021]
  • Stopping at the product as the only desire misses the status, identity and pain angles that drive buying. Always list what the customer wants beyond the product. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0907.022]
  • Record the identity the customer wants, meaning who they want to be. For example, a parent may want to be a reliable parent, and a host may want to be a relaxed host. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0907.026]
  • A customer who wants to be seen as successful by friends may buy products to project that identity. A parent buying a new bed for a child may want to be seen as providing the right things and being a good parent. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0872.033]
  • Complete the motivation fields by asking what the customer wants to gain, what they want to be, what they want to do, and what they want to avoid. The avoid field captures the negative experiences the problem causes, which the purchase helps the customer escape. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0872.032]

Customer avatars: worksheet and fields

  • Define the ideal customer avatar with a worksheet before setting up the store's structure:

    1. Open a customer avatar worksheet built for your niche.
    2. Fill in every field for the person who should land on the site and buy.
    3. Use the answers to decide which visitors to attract and how to convert each of them into a buyer.

    [L0355.003]

  • Define one dream customer with a name, age, relationship status and income level, and choose products that fit her daily life and needs. Check every new product against this customer profile before testing. [L0388.022]

  • Example: a dream customer described as a 40-year-old single woman with money maps to different products by life situation: home decor for a home-improvement-minded person, stretch bands for someone who goes to the gym, and a relaxed hoodie for evenings at home. [L0388.023]

  • Segment customers by use case or buyer type (avatars) rather than by job title, because the best customers may come from very different professions. In the example, the top customers spent over $1,000 per year and included people from very different fields, so avatars built on use cases were more useful for targeting. Build a bundle or promotion for each main avatar. [L0334.059]

  • A business with one customer type may fill in only one avatar column. Businesses with different groups of buyers, such as B2B and B2C or customers of different genders, should keep a separate avatar for each because their needs can differ greatly. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0872.016]

  • The first pass at the avatar document should contain only what you already reasonably know. Accuracy is not required at this stage, because the document is a living resource that grows with each review. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0872.017]

  • Give each customer avatar a name, because team members and agencies can then refer to it quickly, for example when briefing an ad for a specific avatar. The name can be anything. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0872.018, L0907.013]

  • The customer avatar document is not confidential. Share it with anyone who creates work for you or interacts with your customers. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0872.019]

  • A one-page customer research document can be handed to agencies, freelancers, consultants, and new employees or customer service staff so they understand who the customers are without lengthy briefings. Agencies, especially traffic agencies, will ask about target market, customer attributes, and what customers look like. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0872.005]

  • Customer avatar tool: a spreadsheet, either a Numbers or Excel file on Apple or Windows, or a Google Sheets copy made in your own Google Drive that you can share with a team. Spreadsheets are easier to customize and expand than PDFs, whose formatting is hard to manage. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0907.005]

  • Avatar sheet fields (three avatar slots, one column each):

    Field What to record
    Customer Avatar 1 / 2 / 3 One column per avatar; the sheet has three avatar slots
    Avatar Name Any memorable name, used as shorthand in team discussions
    Age Range Basic demographic data
    Gender Basic demographic data
    Average Income Basic demographic data
    Experts Followed Bloggers, YouTubers, authors, TV personalities, athletes and influencers the customer follows
    Favorite Online Spaces Facebook groups, online forums, Reddit communities, Quora, fan pages
    Prior Relevant Purchases Other versions of your product, supporting products, accessories, and products bought alongside yours
    Primary Problem(s) The main problems the avatar wants to solve by buying your product; one or several
    Wants / Desires Desires that follow from the primary problems and relate to your product
    Emotions Currently Feeling How the customer feels now about the problem, e.g. frustrated, sad, excited
    Beliefs What they believe will happen if they buy, what they believe about your product, and what they believe about themselves
    What do they want to gain? Outcomes beyond the physical product, such as pleasure or status
    What do they want to be? The identity they aim for
    What do they want to do? The actions they want to take, as specific as possible
    What do they want to avoid (pain)? The pains they are trying to escape

    (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0907.006]

  • Start the profile with basic demographics (age range, gender and average income), then move to the deeper fields such as problems, desires, beliefs and emotions. The basic demographic fields to record are age range, gender and average income target. Record gender separately for each avatar if customer groups differ in ways that matter. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0907.008, L0872.020]

  • Procedure for building or checking avatars (older advice, 2021-22):

    1. Check whether you already have customer profiles. If they are current, review them for accuracy and stop there; if not, fill in the full sheet.
    2. Decide how many avatars you need. One avatar is enough if one product solves one problem for one type of buyer; otherwise make one per distinct buyer type, such as B2B and retail, or different experience levels within retail.
    3. Give each avatar a short, memorable name.
    4. Record age range, gender and average income.
    5. List the experts, bloggers, YouTubers, authors, TV personalities, athletes or influencers this avatar follows.
    6. List prior relevant purchases: other product versions, supporting products, accessories and products bought alongside yours.
    7. List favourite online spaces: Facebook groups, forums, Reddit communities, Quora and fan pages.
    8. Brainstorm the primary problems the avatar wants solved by your product; several are fine.
    9. Turn each problem into specific wants and desires that relate to your product.
    10. Record the emotions the avatar feels right now.
    11. Record beliefs about what happens after buying, about the product, and about themselves.
    12. Record what they want to gain beyond the physical product.
    13. Record who they want to be.
    14. Record what they want to do, as specifically as possible.
    15. Record what they want to avoid (pain).
    16. Research anything you do not know, and keep the sheet updated as your knowledge grows.

    [L0907.007]

  • Procedure for customer research (older advice, 2021-22):

    1. Start with your own knowledge and experience from serving customers, and the signals customers have already given you.
    2. Open the customer avatar spreadsheet and fill in every field you already know or can reasonably estimate, without aiming for perfect answers.
    3. Give each avatar a name so it can be referenced quickly in team and agency work.
    4. Use Google to research the fields you do not know yet, such as experts, books, magazines, movies and TV, and online spaces.
    5. Fill in the remaining fields: prior purchases, primary problems, wants, emotions, beliefs, and the gain, be, do and avoid questions.
    6. Carry out the exercise within the current month, then keep updating it as you learn more.

    [L0872.012]

  • Record prior relevant purchases, such as other product versions, supporting products and accessories. If your product is an accessory, note which main products your customers already own, for example which phone models buyers of a phone case use. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0907.016, L0872.023]

  • Fields you cannot answer yet can be left empty and completed later. For passion-based niches, find relevant books and magazines by searching Google for titles about that passion. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0872.024]

  • A half-hearted profile leads to half-hearted marketing and weak results. Treat the exercise as serious research and look up anything you do not know instead of guessing. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0907.011]

  • Describing the customer as a broad group such as 'everyone interested in the activity' is the lazy approach. Dig into sub-groups, since their knowledge, needs and problems usually differ. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0907.010]

Problems, desires, beliefs and motivations in the avatar

  • Primary problems are the problems the customer experiences that the product or business solves, and there can be several per avatar. Note every problem you can identify, even if the list grows over time. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0872.026]

  • Define primary problems as the main problems the product helps solve. There may be one or several, and there is no single right answer. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0907.019]

  • Ask why the customer wants the product to find the deeper problem behind it. A person who wants a rowing machine may really want to feel fitter after a stressful year, and that underlying problem drives the purchase more than the item itself. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0872.027]

  • Wants and needs are different. A customer can want a product without needing it, and the want usually comes from an underlying problem such as a status concern. Record both the want and the problem behind it. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0872.029]

  • Turn each problem into a desire tied to how the product is used, not just the product itself. For example, a first-time home cook's problem is making a meal quickly, so the desire is a meal kit that matches their skill level. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0907.020]

  • Record beliefs in three areas: what the customer thinks will happen if they buy, what they believe about the product, and what they believe about themselves. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0907.024]

  • Customers buy for more than the physical product, so list what they want to gain, such as the pleasure of use or social status. Each gain should connect back to their original desires. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0907.025]

  • Record what the customer wants to do, and make it as specific as possible. A precise action, such as mastering one particular technique, gives sharper copy than a vague goal. (Older advice, 2021-22.) [L0907.027]

  • Core problem excavation, for each avatar:

    1. Surface problem: how people describe it publicly (at least 10 quotes).
    2. Real problem: what they think at 2 a.m. and never say aloud (at least 15 quotes).
    3. Identity problem: the negative labels and self-concept the problem created (at least 10 quotes).
    4. Relational problem: strain on partners, family, friends, and avoided social situations (at least 10 quotes).
    5. Trajectory problem: what they fear becoming and the worst case they imagine (at least 10 quotes).

    [L0613.038]

  • Target avatar research inputs to request before starting:

    1. The brand website or key product information.
    2. Facebook ad account data: gender split with spend and performance by gender, performance by age cohort, time-of-day and day-of-week spend patterns, top-performing creative themes, and geographic concentration if relevant.

    [L0613.036]

Desires, emotions and distress

  • A persona is the same as an avatar or an ideal customer profile (ICP). The most attributed persona in a competitor's data is the audience its ads target most, so study that persona first. [L0374.013]

  • A desire is the result the customer wants, such as fading acne scars, even skin tone or smoother skin texture. Desires work better when an emotion is attached to them. [L0374.014]

  • Emotions attached to a desire make the message more effective. For an acne product, love or attraction can be the emotion: the acne stops a person from being seen as a partner, and the product is the fix. Broad, non-targeted love messaging can also be strong for acne products. [L0374.015]

  • Top emotions in a competitor's data, such as hope and relief, are a data-backed signal of what to target. Use the emotion with the most attributed ads as the starting point for your own messaging. [L0374.016]

  • Distress segments:

    Segment Intensity range Baseline state Attention Message receptivity
    Low (curious or preventive) 1-4 Mild curiosity, optimization focus Broad, can process positive and negative cues Responds to aspirational and lifestyle messaging
    Moderate (problem-aware) 5-7 Frustration, concern, mixed hope and skepticism Beginning to narrow Needs problem acknowledgment first; wants proof; distrusts overpromising
    High (desperate) 8-10 Chronic shame, fear, desperation, anger, helplessness Severely narrowed Weak to positive cues; needs extensive validation; responds to 'finally, someone who understands'

    [L0613.027]

  • Label-emotion map, one entry for each of at least 10 significant labels:

    • Label.
    • Primary emotion.
    • Secondary emotions.
    • Attention state while the label is active.
    • Behavioural consequences (what they do or avoid).
    • Which messages can reach them in that state.
    • The aspirational label that could replace it.

    Also map the transition path from each of the top 5 negative labels: trigger, required proof, and typical duration of the change. [L0613.030]

Interviews and surveys

  • In the early stage of a new product, run customer interviews yourself instead of delegating them, even when your time is expensive. Raw answers in the customer's own words are the source for headlines and copy, and a summary removes the phrasing you need. [L0378.117]

  • Before any content work, build a customer profile with a questionnaire of about 15 questions covering at least these areas:

    1. Demographics: age, sex, race.
    2. What a typical day in the customer's life looks like.
    3. Their biggest pain points.
    4. What they value most.
    5. Their goals.
    6. The shopping experience they expect.
    7. Which magazines and blogs they read.

    Use the answers for every article, email and image, and phrase content in the customer's own language. [L0380.005]

  • Survey procedure for current and future customers:

    1. Ask current or future customers what they have at the moment and how they use it.
    2. Ask how your product would help them and what they would need to know before buying.
    3. Run the questions as a survey funnel, so a prospect answers a few questions before getting the offer.
    4. Segment the respondents by their answers (for example colour, resistance level, whether they train with a partner).
    5. Use these segments later: a new product or variant goes only to people who already said they want that feature.

    [L0643.006]

  • The 'Ask' approach (book 'Ask' by Ryan Levesque) is about asking customers directly instead of guessing what they want. Perceived value matters more than the number of added extras: a message that addresses the buyer's specific problem makes the same offer feel more valuable. [L0643.007]

  • Survey template question: "What led you to make a purchase with us?" [L0757.016]

  • Customer research for offers:

    1. Survey new customers with the questions in the survey template.
    2. Read the answers and note what the buyer cared about most when choosing.
    3. Decide what your specific customer would value the most.
    4. Write down your own store ideas as you work through the offer steps, so the list is ready when you need it.

    [L0757.017]

  • Use the following prompt, unchanged, to pull core mass desire, benefits, pain points, proof and transformation stories from a product description and customer reviews:

    Act as a senior direct-response researcher who receives only two inputs: (1) a product description covering technical details, features and specifications, and (2) customer reviews containing genuine feedback and testimonials. Working exclusively from these two inputs, pull out the core mass desire, the main benefits and features, the customer pain points, the proof points and testimonials, and the transformation stories.

Core mass desire:
1. Study how customers phrase things. Spot emotional themes that recur across the reviews and map the before and after states they describe.
2. From the feedback, answer: What is the deepest emotional driver? Which transformation appears again and again in the reviews? What do customers say they can now do or feel after using the product? Which pain or frustration are they most relieved to be rid of?
3. Phrase the core mass desire in one of these two formats: To [emotional benefit] without [pain point], or To finally [desired outcome] despite [previous obstacle].

Required inputs:
Product description: [insert the complete product description]
Customer reviews: [insert 3-5 detailed customer reviews]

Do not make up any facts that are absent from these inputs.

    [L0887.020]

Identity markers and negative labels

  • Identity marker research for identity-based products, in order:

    1. Document at least 10 common values of the identity group, with examples.
    2. List visual markers (clothing, accessories, aesthetics).
    3. Collect at least 20 group-specific phrases or terms.
    4. List at least 15-20 influencers and authorities the group respects.
    5. Analyse at least 10 other brands that market to the identity.
    6. Document at least 5-7 shared experiences.
    7. List the podcasts, YouTube channels and publications the group consumes.
    8. Describe their aspirations and fears, how they signal belonging, which opposing identities they define themselves against, and how the identity has changed over the past 5 years.

    [L0613.008]

  • Negative identity label research: collect labels in five categories (body or health, capability or intelligence, relational or social, financial or success, moral or character), with at least 10 labels per category. Each label needs an exact quote, primary emotion, intensity 1-10, frequency, origin (self-applied, applied by others or both), duration, and source link. Also collect at least 20 aspirational labels, each with the emotion it would trigger, the distance from the current identity on a 1-10 scale, what would make the person feel they had earned it, what blocks them now, and a source link. [L0613.029]

  • Avatar research counts:

    • At least 20 negative identity labels.
    • 15 aspirational labels.
    • 10 failed solutions, with why they failed and how that felt.
    • 20 failure phrases.
    • 10 skepticism patterns.
    • 10 things the avatar refuses to do, each with the real reason.
    • 25 hurtful quotes across relationship types (5-7 per relationship type: partner, children, parents, friends, coworkers, self).
    • 3-5 secret conversation scenarios.
    • 5 quotes in each post-transformation category (supporters, doubters, self, strangers).
    • Top 10 objections, with the real concern under each.
    • 5 label transition paths.
    • 3-5 complete avatars.

    [L0613.039]

Complete avatar profile and transition stories

  • Complete avatar profile sections, in order:

    1. Demographic snapshot: name, age range validated by ad data, gender validated by ad data, location and situation, income, life stage.
    2. Ad data validation: share of spend, engagement rate, conversion rate, time patterns, resonant creative themes.
    3. Core problem: surface, real and identity.
    4. Emotional architecture: distress level, five ranked emotions with intensity and quotes, attention state, emotional entry point, emotion that shuts them down, transition threshold, and the permission needed to hope.
    5. Identity profile: primary negative label with duration and origin, secondary labels, aspirational label with distance on a 1-10 scale, identity wound summary in 2-3 sentences.
    6. Top 5 fears, with quotes.
    7. Relationship impact on partner, children, parents, friends and self, each with a hurtful quote.
    8. Failed solution history (5 items with result and quote) and refusals (3 items with real reason and inner monologue).
    9. Transformation they want: a 3-5 sentence genie-wish description, how relationships would change, and the identity shift.
    10. Top 3 objections with the real concern under each, skepticism triggers, and what would make them believe.
    11. Purchase psychology: emotional trigger, proof, risk reversal, permission, purchase-trigger emotion, breaking-point scenario.
    12. Messaging strategy: emotional sequence, hook approach (problem, mechanism, identity or aspiration), tone, format, and best time to reach.
    13. A mirror-moment opening line.
    14. A 5-7 sentence raw monologue of what they would say to a close friend after drinks.

    [L0613.040]

  • Transition story procedure:

    1. Write down the negative label the person started with.
    2. Write down the positive label they now claim.
    3. Note what made the change possible.
    4. Record how long the transition took.
    5. Identify the turning point in the story.
    6. Trace the emotional path from the starting state to the end state.
    7. Compare how they describe themselves before and after.
    8. Attach the exact source quote and its link.

    Collect at least 10 complete transition stories. [L0613.068]

Persona brief before scripting an ad

  • Fill in this persona brief before scripting an ad:

    Persona brief to fill in before scripting an ad:
- Demographics: age range, gender mix, income level, education, location.
- Psychographics: attitudes toward life and general psychology.
- Behavior: what the person does in a typical day.
- Goals: what they want more than anything.
- Fears: what they are afraid of not achieving.
- Desires: the outcome or feeling they are after.
- Pain points: the problems blocking that outcome.
- Motivation: the underlying drive behind their behavior.

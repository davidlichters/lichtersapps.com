8.1 What a brand is and when to build one

Brand beyond the logo

  • A brand is the set of beliefs, emotions, perceptions and attitudes a customer holds toward a company and its product. Logo, colors and typography are representations that reinforce these feelings, so they are not the brand itself. [L1044.001]
  • A brand means everything that shapes how people perceive a company, not only its logo. It includes the aesthetics (logo, website layout, store or office look), the verbal style, the company persona, the customer experience, and the expectations customers bring to it. [L0487.001]
  • A brand comprises five elements: aesthetic design, verbal language (verbiage), company persona, customer experience, and customer expectations. Together they create the complete perception of your company. [L0237.001]
  • Brand equity is the value a brand earns beyond its product, built from its values, tone of voice and the culture it represents. A brand sells who you represent, not only the item itself. [L0561.022]
  • Brand perception is governed mainly by how the business acts: how it handles customers, its tone of voice, and its conduct on social media. A name only sets the first impression, so consistent good conduct matters more than the perfect name. [L0990.012]
  • A dropshipping store becomes a brand when the product gets a name and people recognise it by that name rather than by its generic category. Brands show faces, custom packaging, labels, and custom content in their ads and store. [L0638.031]
  • Building a loyal customer base through good support and consistent branding is what turns a dropshipping product into a brand. Brands often reach this stage with branded packaging and branded creative. [L0638.032]
  • When branding is done correctly, the specific products you sell are irrelevant. The brand succeeds through connections built with customers via content, not through product features or benefits. [L0237.002]
  • Consistent design across logo, colors and typography lets customers recognise the store immediately and attach their past experiences to it. Inconsistency weakens these associations. [L1044.004]
  • Visual choices must match customer expectations for the niche: green and earthy tones for gardening products, luxury type for luxury goods, and friendly type for family or children's products. Designs that clash with the niche make customers doubt the store. [L1044.005]
  • Brand attachment can be built even on mundane everyday products such as toothpaste, shampoo or coffee. These brands gain loyalty through trust in the name and by reinforcing their differences in a commodity market. [L1044.016]

What a brand creates

Lower customer acquisition cost

  • Building a brand lowers customer acquisition costs because referrals come without ad spend. [L0237.004, L0487.004]
  • Brand awareness moves the buying journey upstream. Customers who already know the company find it when they are ready to buy, instead of comparing product listings. Product selection matters less when the relationship comes first. [L0487.002]
  • A real brand attracts customers who know your name before they search for the product. [L0237.003]
  • Your dream customers are your biggest source of new traffic through word-of-mouth and referrals. Building content they engage with creates a self-sustaining growth engine. [L0237.014]
  • A dream customer buys, engages with content, and recommends the brand to others, so the goal is an advocate rather than a one-time buyer. Advocates bring in new people and reduce the cost of acquiring them. [L0487.003]

Repeat purchases

  • Building a brand increases repeat purchases. [L0237.004, L0487.004]
  • Brands that generate returning customers can afford higher customer acquisition costs. Stores that rely only on new buyers become unsustainable as ad costs rise. Trust built through good product quality, on-time delivery and working tracking drives repeat purchases. [L1251.011]
  • Customers return when their first order went well: the product was on time, tracking worked and the quality matched expectations. Trust is the core reason repeat purchases happen. [L1251.012]
  • A brand creates value through identity alignment, trust and loyalty, emotional attachment, a clearly defined market segment, and influence on how customers buy, post and recommend. Each of these increases repeat sales and organic reach over time. [L1044.002]

Identity and loyalty

  • Customers defend what they identify with, so a brand that represents a customer's identity has a strong position. Designing the store around the customer's self-image is more effective than designing it around the founder's taste alone. [L1044.003]
  • Large sportswear brands often sell a culture and values rather than the product itself, so their ads may barely show the shoe. A brand that represents a lifestyle can charge more than a product-focused competitor. Brand building is a long-term discipline that must be earned through consistent messaging. [L0561.063]

Pricing power

  • Commodity products can carry high prices when the brand supplies identity and status. For example, a running shoe with little material difference from cheaper alternatives can sell for around 120 to 140 dollars because of the brand story and the athletic identity it represents. [L1044.015]
  • Strong brand equity makes new products easier to sell, since customers pay more for a known name with comparable quality. The speaker reported that 70% to 80% of new items sold for his established brands. [L1508.029]

Resale value and market recognition

  • Building a brand increases business valuation if sold and establishes market recognition. [L0237.004]
  • A recognisable brand is also harder to copy, which raises the valuation when the business is sold. [L0487.004]

Ownership of the customer relationship

  • On marketplaces such as Amazon, the platform owns the customer relationship. Buyers see Amazon as the service provider, and the seller cannot build a brand identity or community from customer data. [L0760.015]
  • Build the brand for the long term, aiming to be competitor-proof, marketplace-proof and timeless. Treat your own website as the main sales channel even if a marketplace like Amazon also sells, because the own store has higher margins and lets you control the customer interaction. [L0334.005]

Brand versus commodity

  • A seller who looks and sounds the same as competitors becomes a commodity and can only compete on price. This is a losing position because someone will always undercut you. Price-only competition also fails for resellers who cannot lower prices further. [L0411.005]
  • A business that customers see as essentially the same as its competitors becomes a commodity, and commodities are much harder to sell. Being perceived as unique makes selling easier. [L0893.004]
  • Competing only on product features or technology turns a product into a commodity that buyers compare mainly on price. Stores should add identity, experience and a clear promise so customers have a reason to choose them. [L1044.014]
  • Selling a generic product sourced from a marketplace such as AliExpress without your own branding leads to the same item appearing from many sellers in Google Shopping. Shoppers then see the same product across the whole platform and have no reason to choose you. [L1508.028]
  • The product must be desirable. It does not have to be totally different from competitors, but it must give buyers a clear reason to want it. Desirability comes first, and the rest of the marketing builds on it. [L1438.034]
  • Brand and product support each other. Some buyers purchase because they love the brand, others because they love the product. A strong brand identity helps buyers choose your label over copies and lookalikes. [L1438.035]
  • Competition can serve as inspiration, but the aim is a brand that is desirable regardless of how saturated the market is. Other brands leave room for many players if the product is distinct and wanted. [L1438.039]
  • A brand you create yourself usually has zero search volume at launch, so generic keyword ads will not bring in the first customers. [L0768.003]
  • Ads that target competitor brand and product names work for dropshipping early on, but this approach does not carry over to your own brand. Do not expect the same ad approach to bring the first customers for your own label. [L0768.004] (older source, 2021-22)

Naming the brand

  • A general store name is acceptable when the owner is testing several products, but the store should still define what it stands for. Colors and typography should reflect how the store wants to be perceived, even if the positioning is somewhat vague. [L1044.018]
  • Give the brand a name you can build, so that people can search for it once demand exists. [L0768.003]
  • A recognizable store name only helps once customers search for it. [L0664.081] (older source, 2020)

Staging: validate with sales first

Core rule

  • Validate a product with dropshipping sales first, then build a brand around it once there is proven demand. [L0661.056]
  • A brand should not be built before a drop-shipping store works. Learn the skills by running a profitable drop-shipping store first, then apply them to a brand once several six figures are generated. [L0561.026]
  • Starting a brand from scratch can cost thousands of dollars and requires extensive testing to confirm product-market fit, so a drop-shipping store is a cheaper way to build skills first. [L0561.027]
  • Do not build a brand before you have sales. [L0664.081] (older source, 2020)
  • Branding builds loyalty and repeat purchases, and it works best after a product has proven sales. Build it through custom images, a clear story, a visible face, reliable shipping and good customer support. [L1377.049]
  • A brand built in a single high-demand niche can perform well, as shown by a leather jacket store doing strong daily sales. Brand work often starts after general stores already produce steady revenue. [L0660.025]

What to avoid before sales

  • Beginners waste weeks on logos, colors and brand storytelling for a pump-and-dump product. A simple brand name and product images are enough to test. [L0347.026]
  • Spending time on a logo, a custom branded store or polished brand assets before a product has any sales wastes money and attention. Publish content first, learn from its performance, and build the brand once sales arrive. [L0820.049]
  • Spending too much time on branding, such as styled photos, models and Instagram posts, slowed down sales testing. Focus first on making sales from the product, then build the brand later. [L1444.054]

Minimum viable brand early

  • Minimum professional standards are sufficient for startup success. You do not need multi-thousand-dollar branding packages to look professional and attract customers and suppliers. [L0323.010] (older source, 2021-22)
  • In the early stage, spend on paid traffic and sales instead of brand design, social followers or custom photography. Build social channels after traffic and an email list exist, and link to them from emails. [L0853.091] (older source, 2021)
  • A minimum viable brand (MVB) is the smallest brand that is enough to go live and give customers a credible perception of the store. It avoids a full brand exercise at launch, and it is expanded as the store grows and the test results show what works. [L1044.017]

Early-stage creative spending

  • Early stage, do not hire an expensive creative director. That salary margin can be pumped straight into ads, and with a good product, more ad spend grows your brand faster than brand planning. [L0041.011]
  • The product is the brand in early stage. Invest in the product itself and let it speak through advertising and word of mouth. Good products go viral. Do not pretend you are too sophisticated for direct response. [L0041.012]
  • Athletic Greens is the highest-performing direct-to-consumer brand ever. It prioritised direct response advertising and conversion metrics over design aesthetics, achieved massive scale, and now has Joe Rogan as a promoter. This shows that a brand grows from performance and ad spend, not from early-stage creative direction. [L0041.010]
  • Do not sacrifice advertising performance for brand consistency in the early stage. Brand continuity (colour palettes, tone of voice, iconography) elevates you, but only if it does not reduce ad spend. [L0041.009]

Testing a product before its own design

  1. Test a product by using an existing design from another seller. [L1508.030]
  2. Shoot your own photos for it, so the listing does not match the photos competitors use. [L1508.030]
  3. Once results show demand and the brand has traction, move to your own designs. [L1508.030]

Full brand later

  • Brand continuity and consistency pay off once demand is proven and the ad spend is working. [L0041.009, L0334.005]
  • Three critical requirements for brand success: you must know your customer better than anyone, you must invest significantly in content creation, and you must commit to playing the long game rather than seeking quick results. [L0237.012]
  • CrossFit demonstrates complete brand alignment. Its aesthetic is not a normal gym, its verbiage includes specialised terminology (Box, WOD, AMRAP, Games), its persona is polarising (love it or hate it), its customer experience emphasises family and community, and its expectations centre on consistency. Every element reinforces a cohesive identity that attracts dream customers. [L0237.013]
  • Build the brand for the long term, aiming to be competitor-proof, marketplace-proof and timeless. [L0334.005]

Timing: where the sources disagree

Sources differ on when a brand investment makes sense. Each value applies to a different stage.

Further details

  • A brand built from scratch requires research into the niche and sub-niches, a clear customer avatar, and a sample that proves product-market fit before scaling. Skipping this makes brand building far riskier than dropshipping tests. [L1217.031]

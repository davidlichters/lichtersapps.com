8.18 Influencer, endorsement and persona-led branding

Set the goal and audience before choosing a partner

Before searching for influencers, define the following in order:

  1. Brand objective: decide whether the goal is brand awareness, driving sales conversions, or engaging with the community. The objective changes which type of creator fits.
  2. Target audience: analyze demographics, interests and behaviors of ideal customers.
  3. Preferred social platforms: determine which platforms the target audience uses and pick creators who have a presence there.
  4. Influencer selection: choose creators whose audience and messaging match the brand objective and target audience, keeping messaging authentic.

[L0505.007]

Clear objectives give influencer marketing direction and purpose, which helps maximize the impact of each collaboration. Each partnership should contribute meaningfully to the overall marketing strategy rather than running as an isolated post. [L0868.010]

Before choosing an influencer, define your target audience in this order:

  1. Define the demographic (age, location and similar traits) of your target customers.
  2. Define their interests.
  3. Define their behaviour.

Use these three points to find the influencer whose audience resonates most with yours. [L0480.005]

Match the influencer to the campaign goal

  • Brand awareness: choose an influencer with broad reach and a large, diverse audience. [L0480.007]
  • Engagement: choose an influencer whose audience is active and interacts through comments, likes, shares and story reposts. Such an audience can be smaller than a mass-reach audience and still be more valuable. [L0480.008]
  • Conversion or sales: choose an influencer with a proven record of influencing purchase decisions. Look for in-depth product reviews, demonstrated product use, or exclusive discounts for their followers. [L0480.009]
  • Product launch: pick an influencer who has a history of introducing new products to their audience. A promotion or giveaway with that influencer is a suitable launch approach. [L0480.010]

A large follower count alone does not make an influencer a good fit. The influencer's audience must relate to your target demographic, because a big following does not automatically mean the followers will buy. [L0480.006]

Influencers must share the brand's values and messaging style, not only its audience. A creator's content tone and behavior reflect on partner brands, so a family-friendly brand should avoid creators who use profanity or post inappropriate content. Creators whose lifestyle matches the brand's message are better partners than creators who only cover a related topic. [L0505.011]

Pitch and work with influencers

  • Be transparent about what the collaboration involves from the start, and tell the influencer it is mutually beneficial. Stay flexible on how the work is done, especially with larger influencers, to build a positive working relationship. [L0782.008]
  • In the first outreach, clearly state how the collaboration fits your brand and the influencer's goals. Clarity about this alignment is a core part of a successful pitch. [L0782.009]
  • Brand physical products and digital products heavily when working with influencers, because influencers avoid promoting cheap-looking products. Digital products need mockups, a polished website and a branded PDF with the influencer's photos. [L0789.043]
  • For dropshipped products used in influencer promotions, at least one branded unit must be sent to the influencer. Terms and conditions can state that branded packaging depends on stock, so unbranded packaging may be sent when stock is missing. [L0789.044]
  • Influencers test products before promoting them, so send a high-quality, fast-delivered sample. Packaging quality directly affects whether they will promote the product. [L0789.046]
  • Partner with people who already have authority in the community instead of positioning your brand against others as the right choice. Bringing established voices in builds trust faster than claiming to be better. [L0487.011]

Affiliate partners

Older source (Source A, 2021). To set up an affiliate program for your store:

  1. Decide if you will use Refersion app (paid, not free) to manage it.
  2. Create a small affiliate page that explains why you have an affiliate program.
  3. Describe the type of business partner you want (niche, audience size, etc.).
  4. Include an email address or contact form for interested partners; do not auto-approve.
  5. Manually review and approve only qualified affiliates who align with your brand.

[L0270.055]

Authority endorsements and credibility transfer

  • Credibility transfers from respected institutions, professional bodies, and recognized symbols to a product that carries their endorsement. A prospect who respects the endorser often stops checking the claims themselves. [L0340.019]
  • Older source (Source B, 2021-22). As a newer business, aligning with trusted market figures transfers some of their credibility to you through association, significantly accelerating your own authority development compared to building alone. [L0297.004]
  • The most powerful endorsement is an official approval from a respected institution, and it can be backed by a seal or logo that signals review. Where a full endorsement is impossible, a recognizable symbol of authority can still provide a similar boost. [L0340.020]
  • Health and science products gain credibility from authority cues such as a figure in a white lab coat presenting statistics. Use only authority cues that are real and relevant, since the trust they carry depends on the reader's respect for that authority. [L0340.021]
  • Signed ads, a named spokesperson or a clear personal authority make claims feel more credible than corporate voice. Use a real person to represent the brand when the claim depends on personal expertise. [L1261.114]

Use authority endorsements in this order:

  1. List the people, groups, and institutions in your industry whose respect your target buyers already have.
  2. Ask one of them for a testimonial, endorsement, or official approval of your product.
  3. Get written permission to use their name and image in your marketing.
  4. Optionally pay for a short video of them explaining the product for your website or a DVD.
  5. Place a list of verifiable facts, figures, and charts next to the endorsement so the authority supports the argument rather than replacing it.

[L0340.085]

Identity and group appeals

  • Buyers often purchase to enhance their self-image and to close a perceived gap in identity. Ads can therefore show the traits the target customer wants to display, such as attractiveness, intelligence, economic success, or status, and link them to the product. [L0340.017]
  • Ways to create identity appeal without persuasive argument: link the product to respected users in the same field, such as a martial arts school naming well-known fighters who train there, or a printer offering free work to local public figures and naming them afterward. A security firm can also cite the choices of professionals who have stricter needs than the average buyer. In each case the copy shows the image the buyer wants rather than proving a claim. [L0340.018]
  • Groups shape purchase decisions in three ways: aspirational (people the buyer wants to become), associative (people who share the buyer's values), and dissociative (groups the buyer wants to avoid). Link the product to one or more of these to push the buyer toward a decision made on group identity. [L0340.022]
  • For an aspirational group appeal, the prospect must be able to recognize and identify with the people shown. Featuring people who are too distant, too old, or too ordinary for the target market weakens the appeal. [L0340.023]
  • Associative group appeals work by speaking directly to the attitudes of one segment, or by distancing the product from a rival group, which can make a product feel cooler or more accepted to younger buyers. [L0340.024]
  • A brand name or tagline can signal distance from a group the buyer does not want to resemble, such as an older generation, which attracts a younger segment that wants to look different. This dissociative signal works best when the target group clearly identifies with the contrast and the brand delivers a product that fits it. [L0340.086]

Attractive characters

  • An Attractive Character is a persona that draws people to you across any platform, whether email, Facebook or YouTube. It does not have to be physically attractive. What matters is that the persona gives audiences a reason to follow and trust. [L0523.092]
  • Build an Attractive Character from three parts: elements, identity and storylines. Each part is needed to create a character people will like and follow. Start by assembling the elements and identity before writing storylines. [L0523.093]
  • Every Attractive Character needs a backstory that connects directly to the product being sold. For weight-loss products, use a weight-loss story, and for financial advice, use a financial story. The backstory creates the hook that makes people willing to follow the path you lay out. [L0523.094]
  • If you lack a relevant backstory, borrow a story from a customer, student or client success case and use that person as the character. The character does not have to be the founder, but the backstory must be real and relevant. Success stories from others are a valid source of character material. [L0523.095]
  • Use parables, which are short memorable stories from your life, to illustrate the lessons you want customers to learn. People remember stories longer than bare facts, so teaching through parables makes messages stick. Look through your own experiences for these stories. [L0523.096]
  • People value what they pay for and act on it more than what they receive free. A small charge can increase follow-through, which is why a parable about paying for a free-looking gift works so well when asking for investment. Use this idea when you want customers to commit. [L0523.097]
  • Share real character flaws and vulnerabilities, because perfect characters are hard to relate to. Audiences empathize with imperfect people and trust them more. Hiding every weakness makes you seem untouchable. [L0523.098]
  • Take clear positions on hard issues, because a polarizing character creates supporters, neutral people and opponents. Supporters become loyal fans who share and buy repeatedly. Staying neutral to please everyone reaches almost no one. [L0523.099]
  • Personal life details, such as a family milestone, can open an entirely new segment of buyers who relate to that part of your persona. This new segment often trusts you enough to buy for the first time. Personal stories widen your audience beyond your original niche. [L0523.111]

Identities

  • Leader: guides the audience from one place to another. The leader has walked a similar backstory path, knows the hurdles and has achieved the result the audience wants, so people follow for help along that path. [L0523.100]
  • Adventurer or Crusader: curious, goes on a journey to discover truth, then brings back answers to share with the audience. Reporter or Evangelist: interviews many people to learn and share their stories. Interviewing experts is an effective way to build authority in a niche you do not yet know well. [L0523.101]
  • Reluctant Hero: humble and does not seek attention, but feels a duty to share important knowledge despite being uncomfortable in the spotlight. This identity suits people who have hard-won secrets and a sense of responsibility to share them. Choose the identity that fits you naturally and build your character around it. [L0523.102]

Storyline templates

Loss-and-redemption narrative skeleton: 'I used to have it all and felt like I was at the peak. Life was going well. Then [event] struck. I was forced to find a way out of [situation]. Still, it ended up being a silver lining, since I got through [experience] and came away with [lesson or result]. These days I'm [current position].' The narrative shows how hardship can lead to gain. If you don't have a personal story like this, take one from a follower, a client, or mainstream media.

[L0523.103]

Before and after storyline template: 'First I was [starting condition]. Now I am [current condition].' Use it to show transformation in any market, for example 'First I was [pain point]. Then I tried [product]. Now I [desired result].' Transformation stories are simple to write, can often be shown with pictures alone, and are strong motivators.

[L0523.104]

Amazing discovery storyline template, for selling webinars and teleseminars: 'Everyone, wait until you hear about this amazing new thing I just discovered! You will not believe it, but I got results on my very first try. I was not sure it would work, but it is amazing, so you have to try it [specific call to action].' This story helps people believe the answer they have been searching for is finally available.

[L0523.105]

Secret-driven narrative skeleton: 'I'm keeping a secret. To learn what it is, you have to [specific action].' Intrigue about a concealed method pulls readers deeper into your funnel and higher up your value ladder. A secret-based narrative performs well in most markets when the secret connects to what you sell.

[L0523.106]

  • Us versus them storylines set your audience against outsiders, such as talkers versus doers, and create a rallying cry for loyal followers. Use them to polarize and to pull your fans closer. Make the choice of identity clear so people decide which side they are on. [L0523.107]
  • Third-person testimonials provide social proof. Gather as many customer, client and student success stories as possible, then weave them into your messages or use them as standalone case studies. Other people's results often persuade more than your own claims. [L0523.108]

Cases

  • Fast-food chain (weight-loss character): a traditional fast-food chain shifted its marketing around a customer who had lost weight from about 425 pounds to about 190 pounds by eating from the chain twice a day. The company used his before and after story in commercials and on billboards, which set the brand apart from competitors. The positioning moved the chain from generic fast food toward a weight-loss plan, and the same character kept bringing in business for more than fifteen years. [L0523.109]
  • Course company (student as character): a course company built its continuity program around a student's success story as the Attractive Character instead of its founder's story. The launch sold over 8,000 units and generated more than 1,000,000 USD in sales within two weeks. A later workshop for buyers was split about 50/50 between men and women, where it was usually about 90% men, and women said they wanted to be like the featured student. [L0523.110]

Persona-led ads and challenge formats

  • Ads framed as a person recommending something are expected to earn higher engagement than brand-voiced ads for the same product and offer. Treat this as a hypothesis and verify it with your own split tests before committing budget. [L1475.005]
  • A famous personality can work better as a challenge or opponent than as an endorser, because the challenge creates a story and the endorsement only adds a name. Build the ad around the contest and let the product be what gets challenged. [L1366.025]
  • Chess computer campaign (1978): the product sold on features and was not moving well against competitors. The ad was reframed as a public challenge to a famous chess champion, run as three ads: the first announced the challenge, the second responded to a dispute over permission, and the third showed the champion declining to play but endorsing the product. The three ads with different concepts sold more than 20,000 units, while competitors selling only the product did not succeed. [L1366.026, L1366.108]

Whitelist character pages

  • A whitelist page is a Facebook page you own that presents a fictional character with perceived authority in your niche, instead of your corporate logo. Ads run from that page so the same product and offer look like a recommendation from a person. The page, audience and pixel all stay in your own account. [L1476.001]
  • Every whitelist character needs five elements: a name, a face, a voice, a story and a purpose. If one of them is missing, the page reads as a brand and the illusion of a person breaks. [L1476.005]
Component Rule Example
Name Real first name, casual last name if needed, optional title such as Coach, Doc or Trainer Coach Sam, Doc Ellie, Trainer Mike
Face Clear headshot, direct eye contact, warm expression, AI-generated or licensed stock; the same photo across the page and all ads One consistent portrait
Voice Choose 3 personality traits and keep them in every post and ad; direct, first-person Direct, self-deprecating, practical
Story Personal experience with the exact problem the product solves; the origin story that earns credibility Fixed own posture after 10 years at a desk
Purpose One clear helping goal tied directly to the product category, not multi-niche 'Back pain from desk work', not 'wellness'

[L1475.009]

Name

  • The character name is a real first name with an optional last name and an optional title such as Coach, Doc or Trainer. Titles signal authority without impersonating anyone. Avoid titles like Doc if the character has no real basis for implying that credential. [L1475.010]
  • Give the character a real-sounding first name, an optional casual last name, and a title such as Coach or Trainer when it fits. Avoid 'Doc' and other medical titles, because they conflict with the medical caution for character pages. [L1476.006]
  • Note on the conflict: the example table above uses 'Doc Ellie', but for character pages the medical caution in L1476.006 applies, so do not use Doc or other medical titles there. [L1475.009, L1476.006]

Page category

  • Set the page category to a person-like label such as Coach, Enthusiast, Blog or Personal Trainer. Never use E-commerce or Retail as the category, because that makes the page look like a store and breaks the person illusion. [L1475.013, L1476.007]

Face and cover

  • Use a clear headshot with direct eye contact and a warm, friendly expression for the profile photo. It can be AI-generated or licensed stock, but never a logo or a product shot. Use the same photo on the page and in all ads. [L1476.008]
  • Use a lifestyle scene from the character's world as the cover photo, such as a desk setup, gym or outdoors. Never use a product shot or a discount graphic as the cover. [L1476.009]
  • A face instead of a logo does about 80 percent of the work of building trust, even if the character profile is not perfect. This is why the whitelist page is worth starting on day one of testing. [L1476.033]

Voice

  • Choose three personality traits for the character and keep them consistent across every post, primary text and ad. Use them as the framework for how the copy is written. Mixing tones between posts and ads breaks the illusion that a real person is speaking. [L1475.011, L1476.010]

Story and purpose

  • The character's story is a personal experience with the exact problem the product solves, which builds credibility. Pin a personal organic post to the top of the page explaining why the character started recommending the product. [L1476.011]
  • Each character needs one clear purpose tied directly to the product category, such as helping with back pain from desk work. Avoid vague purposes like 'wellness' and never cover several niches with one character. [L1475.012, L1476.012]

Bio

  • The page bio is a short first-person backstory about the character's own experience with the product's problem. It should be 60 to 100 words, sound like a real person, and contain no hashtags, emojis or salesy language. [L1475.014, L1476.013]
  • The bio opens with a backstory hook tied to the product's problem area and ends with what the character shares or helps with. [L1475.014]

Choosing the archetype

  • Pick the archetype by asking who the buyer already trusts, such as a coach, a peer, a parent figure or a quiet expert. Match the character to that trust, not to the product itself. Base this on the product and avatar research. [L1475.006, L1476.014]
  • A quiet expert does not need medical qualifications to be trusted. For example, a herbal product could be recommended by an older gardener with decades of hands-on experience rather than by a doctor. [L1476.016]

Workflow:

  1. Read your product and avatar research: who buys, their age, life stage, self-image and insecurities.
  2. Ask who this buyer already trusts: a peer, a coach, a parent figure or a quiet expert.
  3. Pick the archetype that fits that trust source.
  4. Attach your customer research and product documents to Claude.
  5. Paste the full generator prompt (see the prompt unit for this topic) and let Claude read the research, pick the archetype and generate the assets.
  6. Check the 3-line research summary Claude prints to confirm it understood the buyer and product correctly.
  7. Copy the outputs: profile photo prompt, cover photo prompt and page bio.
  8. Paste the profile and cover photo prompts into ChatGPT, DALL-E or Midjourney to generate the images.
  9. Upload the images and the bio to the character page.

Whitelist page results and common mistakes

  • Starting point: a brand page held a steady ROAS of 1.8 to 1.92. Change: a whitelist page with a fictional character was introduced for the ads. Result: ROAS rose to about 2.1 to 2.3, a marginal gain that matters more at scale. [L1476.023]
  • Common mistakes with character pages: using a logo or product shot as profile or cover photo, using E-commerce or Retail as the category, giving the character several niches as its purpose, using stock-photo perfection or polished corporate headshots, and impersonating a real person. [L1476.032]

Clubs, teams and local partnerships

  • For a brand focused on local awareness, approach large local sports teams to propose collaborations. These partnerships build brand recognition in a home market. They are aimed at awareness rather than direct sales. [L0661.069]
  • A brand can be built around a local market by partnering with local sports teams or clubs for custom-branded merchandise, such as fan hoodies or skiing-related products. Custom logos on hoodies at the supplier used required orders of at least 500 pieces. [L1212.054]
  • Custom-branded versions of a product can be sold at a higher price than standard designs. Club or partner logos add value and give fans a reason to buy. [L0492.055]

Club partnership procedure:

  1. Identify a local football club with passionate fans and a sold-out stadium, relevant to the brand's audience.
  2. Email the club's marketing department with a simple collaboration proposal.
  3. Hold a call with the club representative to discuss terms.
  4. Agree on a one-time payment for one year of logo usage rights, reported at about 10,000 USD.
  5. Produce custom designs with the club logo and sell them to fans at a higher price.
